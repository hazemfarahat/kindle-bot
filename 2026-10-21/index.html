DeepInfer: High-performance inference framework for DeepSeek models

hackernews • 2249 points • by ggcr • October 20, 2026

DeepInfer is the high-performance inference framework that powers DeepSeek's large-scale production services. This open-source release provides offline inference with the core features needed for serving at scale.

Supported models:

  • DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash

Supported features:

  • Optimized kernels, model and sampler runtime
  • Parallelism: Attention DP / balanced CP, MoE EP + EPLB
  • DSpark and confidence-scheduled verification
  • SWA bounded replay
  • Asynchronous scheduling (zero-overhead batch scheduler)
  • Batch-invariant inference
  • Deterministic sampling
  • Structured outputs

Feature details and configuration

  • Optimized kernels: FlashMLA, DeepGEMM, DeepEP, TileKernels, DeepSelect, and custom TileLang kernels.
  • DSpark and confidence-scheduled verification: accelerate decoding with draft tokens and use confidence estimates to schedule verification. Set 
    DEEPINFER_ENABLE_MTP_VARLEN=1
    ; 
    DEEPINFER_USE_NEXTN_DECODE
    controls the maximum draft length (default: 5).
  • Decoder SWA bounded replay: reduce decoder prefill work by replaying only a bounded suffix. Set 
    DEEPINFER_YOCO_PREFILL_TRUNCATION_MODE=compact
    and 
    DEEPINFER_YOCO_PREFILL_KEEP_TOKENS=140
    for the default V4.1 settings; the retained suffix must cover the SWA window plus the maximum backtracking length.
  • Asynchronous scheduling: overlap CPU batch scheduling with device execution. Enabled by default.
  • Batch-invariant inference: keep model results identical across different batch compositions. Enable with 
    DEEPINFER_BATCH_INVARIANT=1
    (NVIDIA only).
  • Deterministic sampling: produce identical outputs for identical input tokens and sampling parameters. Set 
    DEEPINFER_BATCH_INVARIANT=1 DEEPINFER_ENABLE_MTP_VARLEN=0
    and pass a fixed 
    seq_seed
    to 
    SamplingParams
    (NVIDIA only).
  • Structured outputs: constrain generation to a regex, JSON schema, or EBNF grammar. Pass 
    so_args={"schema_type": ..., "schema": ...}
    to 
    SamplingParams
    ; see examples.

Supported hardware:

  • NVIDIA SM100 GPUs
  • Huawei Ascend 950 NPUs

Required for a complete inference service (not included in this release):

  • A KV cache system
  • VE/Prefill/Decode disaggregation (most required engine functionality is already implemented)
  • Load balancing across data-parallel replicas for both prefill and decode
  • Linux
  • GCC >= 13.0
  • Python 3.12
  • CUDA 13 (NVIDIA only)
  • CANN 9.2 (Ascend only)

Before building DeepInfer, please create and activate an empty Python 3.12 environment.

# Use `ascend` instead for the Ascend platform
# Keep it exported in the environment where you run DeepInfer
export DEEPINFER_ACCELERATOR=nvidia
./build.sh

The default checkpoint, 

deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017
, is downloaded into the Hugging Face cache on the first run; set 
DEEPINFER_HF_MODEL_DIR=/path/to/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017
to run from a local copy instead.

python examples/offline_inference.py

See examples for details.

Prefill Decode

Measured on 4 SM100 GPUs for prefill and 64 for decode — each within one scale-up domain. To reproduce the benchmarks, see run_sweep.sh.

See eval/README.md.

eMudhra intermediate CA compromise

hackernews • 946 points • by galadran • October 20, 2026

20th October 2026

The private keys of eight eMudhra intermediate CA certificates have been compromised. They chain to roots trusted by the Mozilla, Microsoft, Apple and Chrome root programs and by many downstream trust stores.

To demonstrate that the keys are compromised, the original reporter Wayne signed the following message with the private key of each of the eight intermediate CAs and reported it to the Root Program Teams on the 17th of October:

We have a problem here - Wayne

proof.zip contains the message, and for each intermediate the certificate, its public key and a signature on the message. To verify with OpenSSL:

openssl x509 -in EM-DV-TLS-CA-G3B.crt.pem -noout -pubkey | diff - EM-DV-TLS-CA-G3B.pub.pem openssl dgst -sha384 -verify EM-DV-TLS-CA-G3B.pub.pem -signature EM-DV-TLS-CA-G3B.sig message.txt

The root programs and many downstream trust stores have taken action to protect their users. The certificates have been blocked in Firefox and Chrome since the 17th of October, in Apple devices since the 19th and on Microsoft platforms since the 20th.

The issue was responsibly disclosed to downstream trust stores on the 19th of October. Many have already released updates or are in the process of releasing updates.

An attacker with the private key of any of the compromised intermediate CAs could impersonate any website or service.

This has already been remediated by Mozilla, Microsoft, Apple, and Chrome for their users through their tools for distributing revocation information such as OneCRL, CRLite and CRLSets.

The CA has also revoked the compromised intermediate certificates. However, many other clients do not consume revocation information and require software updates to ensure they no longer trust the compromised intermediates.

Intermediate CAChains toResult

To check curl or other command-line clients, run:

curl https://dv-g3b-emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/ curl https://dv-g3b-aaa-emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/

A certificate error means the client does not trust the compromised intermediates.

To check a trust store offline, chains.zip contains the certificate chain served by each of the twelve hosts above in PEM format, with instructions for verifying them with OpenSSL.

impacted-intermediates.json lists the eight intermediates and their Sectigo cross-signs. roots.json lists the roots they chain to.

The private keys for the intermediate certificates were incorrectly generated by the CA. This means that anyone can recover them from the certificates.

This would have been possible since the certificates were created in April 2025. There are no entries in the certificate transparency logs for these intermediates in that period, indicating that CT-enforcing clients like Firefox, Chrome, Edge and Safari could not have been attacked.

However, many clients outside of the browser ecosystem do not enforce Certificate Transparency. We do not know if attackers exploited the flawed key generation to target these clients.

Further technical information about the cause is captured in the initial incident report in Mozilla's bug tracker.

Please see this thread in Mozilla's dev-security-policy forum.

Users should update their browsers and operating systems to the latest versions as soon as possible.

macOS blocks the compromised intermediates, but the built-in 

/usr/bin/curl
verifies certificates against 
/etc/ssl/cert.pem
without checking revocation, and that file has not been updated. To remove the affected roots from it, run:

sudo cp /etc/ssl/cert.pem /etc/ssl/cert.pem.bak &&
awk '/^(###|===) /{skip=0}
  /^=== .*\/CN=(AAA Certificate Services|emSign ECC Root CA - G3)$/{skip=1}
  !skip' /etc/ssl/cert.pem.bak | sudo tee /etc/ssl/cert.pem > /dev/null

Until a distribution ships an updated 

ca-certificates
package, expert users can distrust the affected roots in the system trust store. This is NOT recommended for ordinary users.

On Debian and Ubuntu, run as root:

sed -i \ -e 's|^mozilla/emSign_ECC_Root_CA_-_G3.crt|!&|' \ -e 's|^mozilla/Comodo_AAA_Services_root.crt|!&|' \ /etc/ca-certificates.conf && update-ca-certificates

On Alpine, run as root:

apk add ca-certificates && sed -i -e 's|^mozilla/emSign_ECC_Root_CA_-_G3.crt|!&|' \ /etc/ca-certificates.conf && update-ca-certificates

On Fedora, RHEL, CentOS Stream, Rocky Linux, AlmaLinux and Amazon Linux 2023, run as root:

d=/etc/pki/ca-trust/source/blocklist; [ -d $d ] || d=/etc/pki/ca-trust/source/blacklist trust extract --overwrite --format=pem-bundle \ --filter='pkcs11:object=Comodo%20AAA%20Services%20root;type=cert' \ $d/Comodo_AAA_Services_root.pem && trust extract --overwrite --format=pem-bundle \ --filter='pkcs11:object=emSign%20ECC%20Root%20CA%20-%20G3;type=cert' \ $d/emSign_ECC_Root_CA_-_G3.pem && update-ca-trust

Updates to site guidelines and AI/LLM tags

lobsters • 365 points • by 355E3B • October 18, 2026

Hey folks,

I've been having to perform an increasing number of moderation actions surrounding the 

vibecoding
tag and AI/LLM related content. I started reading Lobsters because it had the most interesting viewpoints related to computing. During that time, I've found that this community is at it's best when it's trying to, in good faith, help others grow. In lieu of a larger and more rambling discussion on this point, we would like to try two changes encourage that behavior again and re-focus topicality around ensuring that content submitted to the site should, at a minimum, be respectful of a reader's time and intelligence. 

The first adjustment is two updates to the about page, which try to provide a bit more clarity in what we consider topical and what constitutes self promotion:

Intro:

Lobsters is a computing-focused community centered around discussing links. Our goal is to learn, craft, collaborate, and share interesting viewpoints.

The administrator is Peter Bhat Harkins ("pushcx"), contact him or another moderator with any support issues. Lobsters was launched on July 3rd, 2012 by joshua stein (who is no longer running it, so please quit bugging him with site stuff) with careful design touches to encourage a healthy community:

At this risk of writing a mission statement, it seemed worth explaining what the site is and hopes to be to visitors from the first sentence of /about. (This original text came from an intro post.)

Then the Guidelines:

Topicality: Lobsters is focused pretty narrowly on computing; tags like art don't imply every piece of art is on-topic.  Some rules of thumb for great stories to submit: Will this improve the reader's next program? Will it deepen their understanding of their last program? Will it be more interesting in five or ten years? Is this compelling material that will seed a discussion, or is it just an artifact?

Pending an upcoming deploy, we no longer allow submissions which are code repositories. While, at first blush, this may seem odd for a community which is primarily centered around programming but it does eliminate the bulk of the "low effort, self promotional" submissions we've been seeing on the site. We've also found that being dropped into a new codebase doesn't start discussions because the reader has to have enough subject matter experience to immediately recognize what's novel or otherwise worth talking about. Instead, we are more interested in content around code bases which cover why it was created, the purpose it serves, the challenges overcome, and the reasons why it's a useful artifact in the ecosystem.

Some things that are off-topic here but popular on larger, similar sites: entrepreneurship; management; careers; news about companies that employ a lot of programmers; investing; world events; anthropology; self-help; personal productivity systems; last-resort customer service requests via public shaming; "I wanted to see what this site's amazing users think about this off-topic thing"; and defining the single morally correct economic and political system for the entire world when we can't even settle tabs vs. spaces.

We're listing "careers" as off-topic to try to head off posts about interviewing, promotions, burnout, and farming. We have the job tag because it's nice to help people connect to jobs, but the topic of getting a good job, weaving jobs into a career over your lifetime, and work is a different thing than the actual programming itself.

Self-promotion: It's great to have authors participate in the community, but we do not want the community to be exploited as a write-only tool for product announcements or driving traffic to their work. As a rule of thumb, self-promotional activity should be less than a quarter of one's stories and comments in the last year. New users (first 70 days) are not allowed to submit self-promo.

This is how we've been enforcing the rule for a while, better to be explicit. It's unfortunately common that authors see their work posted, join the site, but then only use us for traffic. Hopefully this time helps them understand the difference between a symbiotic and parasitic relationship.

The second adjustment is that the 

vibecoding
tag is now marked as inactive. It still appears on old stories but can't be applied to new ones. From my perspective, the tag was overloaded with six differing responsibilities:

  • First, it was the tag which was trying to capture the discussions related to the use and implementation of AI/LLM tools.

  • Second, it was a marker for content which is partially related to AI/LLM tools, tooling or companies.

  • Third, it was a marker for content which includes a discussion on or output from AI/LLM tools in a part of a larger work.

  • Fourth, it was a marker for content which was generated using AI/LLM tools.

  • Fifth, it was being used in competition to the 

    spam
    flag, for really low-quality content which was generated via AI/LLM tools but without apparent significant human authorship.

  • Sixth, it was being used as a tag of shame by people who really dislike AI/LLM tools and used it to insult people at every passing mention.

The last couple years have been the biggest upheaval in the field for decades. While it's acceptable, within limits, to express frustration towards circumstance or technology, it's not acceptable to direct that behavior towards individuals whether they be members of the community or not. Other people can't be your punching bags and Lobsters can't be your boxing ring. That's not acceptable.

While `vibecoding` is no longer an active tag, we still feel that a subset of the tooling related discussions are relevant to the site. While this is reaching far back into the annals of computing history, tools like compilers, databases, testing, and vcs had similar points of consternation and concern when they were introduced. I'm not going to claim that I'm able to guess how the industry is going to shape up, but my gut feeling is that but my gut feeling is that it's going to be closer to something like we've seen with systemd than compilers. This new tag `ai-tooling` covers "The design, ux, development of AI/LLM-powered tools themselves".

As a fun addendum, there's a variant of Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs which exist for websites, we kinda fell on the wrong side of that trying to migrate the site and get this out on a Saturday. Whoops!

Thanks, 355E3B

The Future of CSS

hackernews • 372 points • by soheilpro • October 21, 2026

For the past few years, all of the major browser vendors (Google, Apple, Microsoft, and Mozilla, along with Igalia and Bocoup) have been working together on Interop(opens in new tab), a project to improve universal browser support on web technologies. It’s been a remarkably successful effort; when a focus area gets added to Interop, it often winds up reaching near-100% support across all major browsers within a year. ✨

CSS Subgrid is a good example. Subgrid is a super-powerful extension to CSS Grid which enables entirely new layouts. It was implemented in Firefox back in 2019, but there wasn’t much movement with other browsers for years. It got added to Interop 2023, and by the end of that year, it was widely available across all modern browsers:

allbrowsers.

As we reach the end of 2026, Interop 2027 is currently being planned, and the team at Mozilla has created a ranking system(opens in new tab) for us to upvote our most-requested features. They plan on using the ranking data to decide what to include for next year’s Interop!

In this blog post, I want to dig into the top 10 features I voted for, to offer a bit of a preview of what CSS might look like within the next year. There will also be some little tips and tricks you can start using today!

Link to this heading1. CSS functions

More info on

caniuse(opens in new tab)

One of the most exciting ideas is the proposal to add functions to CSS(opens in new tab):

/* Define a function: */
@function --gradient-to-transparent(--color, --direction: bottom) {
  result: linear-gradient(
    to var(--direction),
    var(--color),
    transparent;
  );
}

/* Invoke it: */
.elem {
  background: --gradient-to-transparent(red);
}

/* …which produces: */
.elem {
  background: linear-gradient(
    to bottom,
    red,
    transparent;
  );
}

If you’re familiar with JavaScript, this should feel pretty familiar. We define new functions with 

@function
and specify the returned value with 
result
.

There’s so much cool stuff we can do with this. Personally, the use case I’m most interested in is the ability to convert between polar and cartesian coordinates, a concept we use over and over in my Whimsical Animations course(opens in new tab):

@function --translate-polar(--angle, --distance) {
  result: translate(
    calc(cos(var(--angle)) * var(--distance)),
    calc(sin(var(--angle)) * var(--distance))
  );
}

/* Move this element 100px at a 45° angle: */
.particle {
  transform: --translate-polar(45deg, 100px);
}

This feels pretty “science-fiction” to me, but it’s already implemented in Chromium browsers, and if it gets added to Interop 2027, I think there’s very good odds that it could become widely available sooner rather than later!

Link to this heading4. Anchored Container Queries

More info on

caniuse(opens in new tab)

I recently published a blog post on Anchor Positioning, one of the most exciting modern CSS features (which was actually prioritized in Interop 2026, and is now widely available!).

The Anchor Positioning API allows us to anchor one element to another. For example, we might anchor a floating tooltip element to its trigger, so that no matter where that trigger is on the page, the tooltip will always be connected to it.

If you’ve ever tried to solve this sort of problem yourself, you know it’s surprisingly tricky. For example, we might want our tooltip to float above the anchor, but what if the anchor is near the top of the viewport?

The tooltip fits comfortably.

The tooltip’s getting cut off!

The original Anchor Positioning API gave us most of the tools needed to solve this problem easily in CSS. It allows us to specify fallback positions; if the target element doesn’t fit above the anchor, it can flip automatically to the bottom.

The only thing missing was the ability to tell whether or not the target had flipped. We had no way to know, in CSS, whether the target element is currently sitting above or below the anchor, whether it’s using the default or fallback position. For tooltips, this is a problem because we typically want to add a little protrusion that points towards the anchor.

The “Level 2” version of the Anchor Positioning API includes a new type of container query that allows us to check whether the target element is using a fallback position or not. You can learn more about it in my Anchor Positioning blog post, or check out the Interop 2027 proposal(opens in new tab).

This is one of the great things about responsive CSS, but it leads to an unfortunate side effect: tab order is always based on the DOM order, not the visual order. So, if someone uses assistive technologies to navigate between elements, that navigation order will always be the same, even as the layout changes dramatically depending on screen size.

On desktop, the first visual item is that list of Redacted on the left, but on mobile, the first item is that big red button. Whichever element comes first in the DOM will be the first item to receive focus, which will invariably be wrong for a percentage of our users.

  reading-flow: grid-rows;
}

There are values for both CSS Grid and Flexbox, and if you need even more control, the 

reading-order
property allows us to create a custom order, sorta like

in HTML.

This property has been championed by one of my personal heroes, Rachel Andrew. You can read more about it in her blog post on reading-flow.

Apple broke an essential presentation feature of iPads

hackernews • 559 points • by evanb • October 20, 2026

A brief digression from my usual teaching posts to complain about a devastating regression in iPadOS 27.

If you’ve followed my work up until now, you’ll know that I rely heavily on writing on handouts from my iPad, projected via AirPlay to an external screen that my students can see. When I’m doing this, my iPad is also doing many other things: controlling a PowerPoint presentation on my Mac, showing me notes that I’ve taken on points I want to mention and students I want to call on, and showing me the results of polls before I reveal them to the class. This ability to do other things on your iPad while the audience just sees your presentation or handout is an essential part of a variety of pedagogical moves that I use in my teaching.

Unfortunately, Apple just completely broke it.

The problem

As of iPadOS 27, if you have multiple windows open while AirPlaying (whether or not Stage Manager is on), tapping on anything other than the presenting app will snap the external display into showing your whole iPad screen. This means that my students see all my notes on who I want to call on, or poll results that should be hidden from them. They also get distracted from what we’re learning by all these jarring windows popping up and disappearing. If you’re presenting at a big conference, it’s extremely embarrassing.

In GoodNotes, the presenter view will return once you tap back on the app. In Keynote, it will just exit the presentation entirely. In PowerPoint, it will put the app in a limbo where you have to restart the presentation to proceed. Here’s a Reddit post illustrating the behavior in GoodNotes. But don’t just take my word for it; try it at home!

I sort of understand why Apple did this. In iPadOS 26, which app could “control” the external display was hard to identify. Essentially, you had to swipe up as though going to the home screen, then tap on the app you wanted in the dock, and that app would take over the external display if it had a presentation mode. This was weird and hard to understand, but Apple’s solution is to just make it so that whatever is the most recently tapped app takes over, which is much, much worse.

Ask what you can do

As for what the behavior should be, I’m not a developer, but here’s a proposal: if you’re using an app that is in presenter mode (e.g., PowerPoint playing a presentation, or a GoodNotes document open), it should always get precedence on the external display, until that presentation gets stopped, the document is closed, or the app is quit. Period.

I talked/griped/whined/begged about this on an episode of Mac Power Users in early September (here’s a link to the video), and as of now this behavior hasn’t changed, including in the latest betas. I’m hoping that this post can get the word out, or that maybe someone has figured out a workaround. I’ve also filed a feedback report with Apple and encourage you to do the same (click here and mention my feedback code FB23615646).

I’ll update this post if anything changes, but for now, I can’t recommend this operating system to anyone using their iPad to teach or present.

What shall I teach my son?

hackernews • 400 points • by Curiositry • October 20, 2026

What Shall I Teach My Son?

What is the purpose of education in the age of AGI?

October 19, 2026

Yesterday, the title The End of Education by Neil Postman called to me from my bookshelf, whispering that it might be the right book at the right time. It was. Published in 1995, it was surprisingly appropriate balm for the soul of a confused programmer and soon-to-be father.

My wife and I are expecting our first child. My economically valuable skillset – programming – suddenly feels less valuable. What shall I teach my son? And to what end?

Mr. Edison says
That the radio will supplant the teacher.
Already one may learn languages by means
Of Victrola records.
The moving picture will visualize
What the radio fails to get across.
Teachers will be relegated to the backwoods.
With fire-horses,
And long-haired women;
Or, perhaps shown in museums.
Education will become a matter
Of pressing the button.
Perhaps I can get a position at the switchboard.

– Quoted in The End of Education by Neil Postman, quoting Teachers and Machines: The Use of Technology Since 1920 by Larry Cuban

In Edison’s own words:

Books will soon be obsolete in the public schools… Our school system will be completely changed inside of ten years.

– Thomas Edison, The New York Dramatic Mirror, 1913

Back in 2013, I boldly predicted that MOOCs would upend education. Ah, my past self, you sweet summer child.

Nassim Nicholas Taleb has helped me appreciate the staying power of staying power, sometimes called The Lindy Effect. Merely staying alive implies some anti-fragility to adapt and survive. Consider that Harvard, Yale, and Princeton were founded to educate the clergy. From The End of Education:

In the Western world, beginning in the thirteenth century and for five hundred years afterward, that God was sufficient justification for the founding of institutions of learning, from grammar schools, where children were taught to read the Bible, to great universities, where men were trained to be ministers of God.

Schools survived what happened to religion. It should be no surprise that they’ve survived the radio, motion pictures, and the internet.

We typically confuse why something claims it exists, ie to glorify God, vs the forces that actually perpetuate it, which are subtler, we’d prefer not spoken aloud, and more prosaic, ie endowments, prestige, and needing someone to watch one’s kids.

It has been a surprisingly good bet that schools will continue to exist in roughly their current form for many years to come. Ironically, there also seems to be some staying power to technologists boldly (and so far incorrectly) doubting the staying power of schools! I myself have contributed to this grand tradition multiple times, and find myself at risk of doing it yet again.

Surely this time must finally be different, right? We have captured intelligence in a bottle, and you’re telling me that “Ok class, turn to page 17” and “Does anyone know how to solve for x” are still going to be around in the 2030s?

There are many shifting purposes to education, which underpin how it has survived this long. As the times change, so do its purposes shift and adapt, such as:

  • become a good person

  • become a good citizen

  • earn a living

  • make the world better

  • learn basic facts

I once tried to come up with a single-word purpose for education, and I landed upon the word “resourceful”, because I reasoned that if you “were able to use arbitrary resources to uncover and solve problems”, you had no more need for schooling. In the age of Google, email, and the internet, a person was educated when they could wield them. But now I see my vision of resourcefulness perfected in the AI agent and its tool loop. Maybe resourcefulness will go the way of the Dewey Decimal System which in turn supplanted memorization and an oral tradition.

Imagine education as a table held up by its many purposes. I didn’t realize how centrally important “the ability to earn a living” was for me until it started to wobble. A big reason is because I’m looking for a more objective arbiter of “good” in education. Education’s own tests and measures can feel stifling. The market offered a counterpoint. Steve Jobs and Bill Gates were college dropouts, but the market deemed them good. Now that I think of it, apparently Einstein was a mediocre student, but his contributions to science were his way out. With recent advances in AI mathematics, both of these paths to glory are under fire. Clara Collier makes similar points in her exceptional essay “After Work, We’ll Have Each Other.”

If AI takes our ability to contribute, what’s left for our children?

Neil Postman offers a few suggestions for positive purposes for education that I found heartening, not mainly for their content, but for the fact that it’s possible to make up new purposes for education and advocate for them. My favorite suggestions of Postman’s are:

  • Spaceship Earth – we’re all in this together; let’s take care of each other and our planet

  • The American Experiment – let’s revive our sense of wonder, curiosity, and responsibility about what America is and can be

  • The World Makers – as a student of McLuhan, Postman would be remiss if he didn’t invite students to make the tools and narratives that thereafter make us

At the risk of going too meta, I do strongly believe in Postman’s point (in his other education book, Teaching as a Subversive Activity) that “the medium is the message.” That the way we teach what we teach is often more important than the what. And likewise, I believe in Dr. Alfie Kohn’s notion that we should relate to our educational charges as humans, not objects – “doing with” vs “doing to”. Thus, I believe it may be most honest and effective to invite our children to help us craft the purpose for their education as we figure it out together.

Wish me luck.
