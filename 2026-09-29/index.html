Introducing Claude Sonnet 5.5, the second model in the Claude 5.5 family. It’s a clear upgrade over Claude Sonnet 5, runs 30%+ faster, and costs up to 30% less for most work.

Sonnet 5.5 is a faster, lower-cost complement to Claude Opus 5.5. Where Opus 5.5 is built for complex work requiring careful judgment, Sonnet 5.5 is strongest at well-scoped everyday tasks, fixing bugs, and creating polished documents, slides, and spreadsheets. It’s also got a sharp eye for design. Claude Haiku 5.5, built for high-volume and cost-sensitive applications, will join the Claude 5.5 family in the coming weeks.

Sonnet 5.5 improves over Sonnet 5 on:

Performance. Sonnet 5.5 scores 70.6% on Terminal-Bench 4.0, an agentic coding evaluation, compared to Sonnet 5’s 10.3%. It scores two points below Opus 5.5 on GDPval-AA, a test of real-world work across a variety of occupations. And it’s strong on long-horizon work and image understanding—it’s the first Sonnet model to beat Pokémon Red working only from screenshots.

Collaboration. Like Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 writes more clearly than our previous generation of models; early testers described it as a better partner for collaboration than Sonnet 5. Its speed also makes it well suited to fast iteration on less complex tasks.

Cost. Sonnet 5.5 is priced the same as Sonnet 5 at $2 per million input tokens, $10 per million output tokens, and $0.20 per million tokens for cache reads, but it typically needs far fewer tokens to do the same work. In our testing, it costs up to 30% less per task than its predecessor.

Speed. Sonnet 5.5 generates outputs 30%+ faster than Sonnet 5, making it our fastest Sonnet model to date.

Alignment and safety. On our automated behavioral audit, Sonnet 5.5 improves on or matches Sonnet 5 on most measures of alignment. Because its cybersecurity capabilities are comparable to Opus 5’s, it’s the first Sonnet model to launch with cyber safeguards and fallbacks like those we’ve developed for our most capable models. Its biology safeguards are the same as Sonnet 5’s. Both safeguards target a narrow set of high-risk requests; routine software development and most life sciences work are unaffected.

Alignment

It's Time to Investigate the AI Labs

hackernews • 517 points • by ibobev • September 28, 2026

Over the last several months, the two leading frontier AI labs have shown some brazen behavior.

It started with a series of​ carefully planned announcements​ and reports from OpenAI that try to establish how unnerving and powerful (not to mention ​felonious​) their LLM-powered agent systems have become.

They then handed the baton to Anthropic, whose employees began ​publicly debating​ the exact probability that these technologies would lead to human extinction. They delivered this all with an eerie calmness that conveyed a nihilistic inevitability about their work.

The ground suitably softened, the campaign crescendoed with Anthropic CEO Dario Amodi releasing a letter, titled ​“We Must Pace the Frontier,”​ in which he enumerates all the harms his own company’s research might cause, and then, instead of apologizing and promising to stop, concludes that catastrophe can only be avoided if we “build the technology in the right way,” which involves – surprise, surprise – the government slowing down potential competitors and allowing the labs to take the lead in advancing the relevant tech. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman quickly ​tweeted his support​ for this brave plan.

Internally, these labs have long embraced this style of messianic thinking, casting themselves as humanity’s only hope against the defied powers of superintelligent AI. This summer, in some sense, was their attempt to induct the rest of us into this long-held ideology.

It didn’t work.

Instead of inspiring the masses to stand up and applaud these engineers’ heroic rationalism, it made us instead start to ask, “What the hell is going on over in those labs?”

To that end, last Thursday ​I published an op-ed in The New York Times​ calling on Congress to begin a public fact-finding mission to uncover what research projects OpenAI and Anthropic are conducting, how they’re conducting them, and for what purposes.

I suggested three key areas to explore with this questioning:

  • We must move past vague discussions of “AI” and instead isolate the specific types of systems that are creating problems. The frontier labs want us to think of AI as a singular technology on an inevitable, fixed trajectory. In reality, most of the problems in recent months result from a​ narrow band of incautious experiments​, conducted mainly by the frontier labs. They need to justify why they’re running them.
  • We must examine the internal safety procedures surrounding these research efforts. ​OpenAI revealed​ a long series of unauthorized hacking attacks by their autonomous agents. Why weren’t these efforts stopped after the first such incident was revealed? Why not pursue criminal liability for them running systems they knew would likely commit crimes?
  • We have to investigate the role of apocalyptic futurist ideologies in the decisions being made by the frontier labs, both about what research to pursue and how fast to pursue it. I’m increasingly concerned that OpenAI and Anthropic, in particular, are becoming increasingly reckless because they believe collateral damage is justified in a race to redeem humanity. (For more on these beliefs and their connections to the labs, see: ​this​ and ​this​ and ​this​, all published in the Times in just the last several weeks.)

“We need to stop letting a small number of private companies, acting and talking in increasingly erratic ways, dictate how we’re supposed to feel about A.I.,” I concluded. “We’ve heard what they want to say; now it’s Congress’s turn to step in on our behalf to find out what’s really going on.”

Pirating the Pirates

hackernews • 600 points • by piotrgrabowski • September 28, 2026

Home video release images from The Good, the Bad and the Ugly (Sergio Leone, 1966). Clockwise from top-left: Arrow Video (4K UHD, 2025), Kino Lorber (4K UHD, 2021), MGM (4K Blu-ray, 2014), MGM (DVD, 1998).

“
People who alter or destroy works of art and our cultural heritage for profit or as an exercise of power are barbarians, and if the laws of the United States continue to condone this behavior, history will surely classify us as a barbaric society. ”

—George Lucas, 1988

“
To me, it [the original Star Wars trilogy] doesn’t really exist anymore. It’s like this is the movie I wanted it to be, and I’m sorry you saw half a completed film and fell in love with it.”

—George Lucas, 2004

Vancouver, 2011: My then-roommate Willa Ross and I are at our wit’s end in one of Simon Fraser University’s characteristically antiseptic editing suites, having just spent the weekend hacking together three distinct copies of Sergio Leone’s 1966 western The Good, the Bad and the Ugly, all illegally ripped. The film had fallen victim to an MGM “restoration” in the early 2000s: Scenes Leone cut were reinstated, inflating the English-language runtime from 161 to 179 minutes, with Eastwood and Wallach dubbing the missing dialogue and a soundalike standing in for the late Lee Van Cleef. The mono soundtrack, meanwhile, had been reworked into a surround mix with jarringly modern gunshot effects. This “extended” cut was the only version commercially available for nearly a decade, an all-too-common fate in an era of revisionist studio reworkings.

Our goal was simple: undo this butchery. We planned to combine sound from an out-of-print 1998 DVD (an approximation of the 1967 international release) with video from a recently imported Blu-ray of the Italian cut. After successfully navigating the various pitfalls of late-2000s home video ripping, our hopes were dashed by a catastrophic realization: The Italian cut was even more dissimilar to the international cut than the extended cut, the infamous torture scene entirely restructured and various shots missing heads and tails. We gave up for nearly a decade.

***

Publicly accessible versions of major works of cinema are often wholly inadequate for reasons as diverse as the resultant shortcomings. A major studio’s home video arm might “expand” a film, remix a monaural audio track, wipe away digital grain, oversaturate or oversharpen the image, or even change the aspect ratio for the sake of an attractive sticker promising a new-and-improved experience.

At other points, low budgets and corporate apathy lead to corners being cut. Spencer Draper—best known as the Damn Fool Idealistic Crusader on YouTube, where he reviews the technical and historical aspects of digital releases with a rigor usually reserved for scholarly peer review—characterized the attitudes of negligent studios harshly in my recent interview with him: “‘Oh, you got a problem? The Kino [an American boutique label] disc is defective? So what. So what if it uses a defective Paramount master with frozen grain and [has] problems throughout.’ [The studios] don’t care that any given audio file is 30 years old and the channels are reversed.” Sometimes, this persists for decades: Every copy of Once Upon a Time in the West (1968)released since its 1984 reconstruction has featured a major soundtrack mistake during the end credits brought about by a botched attempt to fix another error introduced in 1970, one example that particularly rankles Draper.

This is where boutique labels come in—companies that independently license and release archival titles, in something like the spirit of Criterion’s founding aim: to gather significant films from around the world and present them as close to a filmmaker’s intent as possible, in the best technical condition available. Outfits like Arrow, Kino, and Eureka operate on a version of that premise. What any given label actually restores versus what it merely receives is a separate question, running a wide spectrum. Sometimes a label might receive a fully mastered digital file and find themselves contractually obliged to release it as-is; at other times, they might receive raw digital scans with partial or no cleanup; or they might be required to source, scan, and restore a film by themselves.

James Flower, director of production at Arrow Films, a British boutique label renowned for its rigorous approach to preservation, raises a different culprit for historical inaccuracies: living filmmakers. The revisionist impulse is nearly as old as cinema—from Charlie Chaplin modernizing his silent classics in later years to Wong Kar-wai radically altering the color grading, aspect ratios, and soundtracks of his back catalog for a 2021 Criterion box set. In his note accompanying this set, Wong Kar-wai justified his alterations with a simple philosophy: “No man ever steps in the same river twice, for it’s not the same river and he’s not the same man.”

However, this revisionist approach often leaves release-accurate versions unavailable to historians and audiences alike. In our interview, Flower recalled a director enamored with a digital aesthetic “wanting his films to look sleek and modern and degrained, with oversaturated colors that would not have been achievable photochemically.” Flower suggested including both the revised cut and a historically accurate version, but the director vehemently refused. Facing a deadline, Arrow acquiesced. “For me, he was vandalizing his own work,” says Flower.

Vancouver, July 2019. Radicalized by a disheartening screening of the official MGM masters of Leone’s Dollars trilogy at a local cinematheque, Willa and I resume work on our cobbled-together cut. We now have better raw materials: a new Blu-ray with far superior picture quality, and an old LaserDisc with an uncompromised mono track. A decade of expertise (I have become a colorist and Willa an editor), alongside a decade of technical progress in editing tools available for independent filmmakers, enables us to properly process them. A local repertory cinema programmer catches wind of this, and a plan is hatched: book an official Digital Cinema Package from MGM and swap it out with our homebrew copy for a public screening. A magical evening ensues: It was as if we were seeing the film for the first time, smuggled before a live audience.

After the screening, I’m approached by a man with an attitude that screams “so you think you know a thing or two, huh?” It turns out his attitude is warranted: He enthusiastically informs me that he’s spent years on his own far superior reconstruction—a 4K scan of a well-preserved IB Technicolor print of The Good, the Bad and the Ugly.

While digital tools have evolved, this pattern of unofficial preservation is by no means new. It’s the reason F. W. Murnau’s Nosferatu (1922) survives: after Bram Stoker’s widow won a 1925 copyright suit, a German court ordered every print to be destroyed, but copies slipped past German jurisdiction and circulated for years as unauthorized bootlegs. Similarly, Kevin Brownlow’s reconstruction of Abel Gance’s Napoléon (1927) began as an illicit teenage hobby. Starting in 1954, he spent decades tracking down fragments and duplicating fragile, rights-encumbered prints to keep them from disappearing—essentially a massive fan edit, eventually completed with Gance’s own participation.

The most famous retrospective cinematic alteration was committed by George Lucas with massively controversial Special Editions of the original Star Wars trilogy—and the subsequent unavailability of unaltered versions in anything resembling acceptable quality. Both Draper and Flower cite the 2004 DVD release and emergence of communities such as the one found on OriginalTrilogy.com as key personal entrees into the world of fan preservation. Draper jokes that “I’ve learned more from George Lucas than anyone ever because he made certain choices that I didn’t agree with, so I started digging!” Starting in the mid-2000s, he took an active role on various forums, contributing wherever he could: “They eventually made a sub-thread about preservation efforts, which was everybody capturing VHS tapes and laserdiscs and trying to upscale LDs and sync other audio tracks. One thing led to another as the preservation scene grew out of one little thread.”

Eventually, two distinct projects emerged. Petr “Harmy” Harmáček’s “Despecialized” edition, a Frankenstein’s monster cobbled together from various official releases, came first. Next was Team Negative1’s Project 4K77, which used homebrew 4K scans of 35mm theatrical prints to create accurate bootlegged digital copies. Both projects remain freely available to anyone willing to jump through some trivial internet hoops.

When an informal network of amateurs questions the work of industry standard-bearers, it is rarely a matter of raw technical superiority but rather a fundamental difference in constraints. While restoration entities often operate on strict budgets and tight deadlines, preservationists can spend years cross-referencing decades of older releases. This is not to say fan efforts are infallible—debates in online forums about accurate color grades spurred by the recent influx of amateur 35mm scans of release prints demonstrate how certain elements remain moving targets—but for objective elements like editorial cuts, missing frames, or unaltered audio, historical reference materials generally leave little room for debate.

Furthermore, fans operate entirely outside the structural limitations that thwart even skilled industry technicians. A professional lab might make all the right archival choices only to have those compromised by a revisionist director or a studio’s marketing needs. Fans, in contrast, answer to no one but themselves, which has let them accomplish what studios with exponentially more resources and infrastructure haven’t: banding together to preserve and disseminate historically accurate versions of some of the most beloved films in history—an end-run around the rights and financial issues that hold legitimate enterprises back. Flower notes: “We don’t always have the time or budget, or even the legal right, to do multiple versions. Fans don’t have those restrictions. They can do as many variations as they want, and that’s fine.” As of this writing, Project 4K77 features dozens of audio tracks and a file size exceeding 100GB: both impossible on any commercial Blu-ray release, and unfeasible for online distribution via legitimate channels.

Beyond Star Wars, an illicit cottage industry has sprung up to patch the holes in any given film’s digital representation. Of particular note is the “remux” community, dedicated to seamlessly stitching together the best video and audio streams from different releases without any generational loss of quality. Want a version of Stop Making Sense (1984) in which you can freely select every soundtrack mix ever released? You won’t find it on the official A24 disc; you have to turn to a remux circulating on a few highly exclusive private torrent websites. The original 5.1 mix of Master and Commander (2003) married to the new 4K transfer? Remux. Dr. No (1962) with audio that hasn’t been scrubbed of all its detail? Remux.

Draper sees this pooling of resources as an incredible service: “Somebody’s already done a great job of the work you were going to do yourself.” His thoughts quickly turn, however: “We’re all worried about the crackdown coming at any point. Put your hood on, look over your shoulder, go to the back door video speakeasy and give the secret recognition code.”

The elephant in the room: Legally, this is piracy. Under Section 1201 of the Digital Millennium Copyright Act, merely breaking a Blu-ray’s digital lock to rip the video files is a federal violation, regardless of intent. The law makes no distinction between mass-distributing a movie for profit and preserving the historical record. To take part in these preservation projects is to take on significant legal risk, with admonishments from rights holders and cease-and-desist letters being common.

Flower sees copyright law as a double-edged sword for film preservation. Though intellectual property rights—and the resultant studio-mandated limitations—hold Arrow back from certain actions that would be theoretically possible otherwise, these same rights in many ways ensure their income: “We have to protect our interest as well as that of the rights holders that have entrusted their films to us. We spend a lot of money on licensing and restoring. I certainly don’t think that copyright is something that should be abolished, unless someone can think of another commercial framework that would allow us to invest in the work we do.” Yet he maintains that there is a place for piracy-based preservation: “It comes down to who is financially benefiting from these things. In terms of using a physical copy and then manipulating it or finding another use for it beyond just watching it, that is a gray area that I’m personally more inclined to be generous towards.”

A sort of “pirate’s code” has emerged among these communities: an attempt to create a parallel set of norms in a context in which the strict definition of “legal” can no longer operate as a guiding principle. “You have to own a physical copy of at least one version of any given film,” says Draper. “We’re not trying to make any money out of this. Don’t make your own bootleg copies and throw them on eBay, or throw them on Twitter of all places.” Nevertheless, the clandestine nature of these efforts weighs on Draper: “It would be nice if there was at least a little bit more recognition of unofficial preservation efforts. I feel like there’s a gigantic weight about to come crashing down on me at any second because, heaven forbid, I bought another twenty laserdiscs and captured audio tracks and bought VHS tapes on eBay to compare audio captures to fix the bad audio they didn’t care about the first time around.”

Fine-tunes of Qwen3.5 and Gemma 4 for zero-shot classification: small, fast decision models you slot into your code, with the same request format as Jev. lobsters • 185 points • by raymii • September 27, 2026

“They had no concept of a duty of care to their users.”

A Mastodon post by computer scientist David Chisnall has a very Unsung opener:

I have used vim since around 2000. I have written five books, a PhD thesis, a few dozen papers and over 150 articles with it. At this point, my higher brain functions are not engaged at all when I use a bunch of common vim commands, they just happen. Documents I wrote with anything else have random :w in the middle.

Chisnall goes on to talk about one specific vim feature:

Persistent undo is one of my favourite features of vim. […]

I don’t often need the persistent undo. But on the few occasions when I have needed it, it’s been invaluable: ooops, I deleted something from this file, maybe last week and one reboot ago, what was it? Undo until I find it, copy it, paste it into the current version. Or, a bit more commonly: I had this working, then I tidied it up ready to commit, now it isn’t working, what did I do?

Vim has kept this working across major version upgrades over a period of about 20 years. I don’t even think about it, it’s just part of Raskin’s First Law: A program may not harm a user’s data or, through inaction, allow a user’s data to come to harm. If vim or the computer crash, or if I close a file and come back to it six months later, my undo history is still there.

NeoVim is a fork of vim (in news for other reasons):

So I tried NeoVim when it was quite new. Vim that you are familiar with, but better? Great!

The first thing I noticed in NeoVim was that undo didn’t work. I tried opening the file in vim and undo didn’t work there either.

Neovim had changed the format of the undo files. It hadn’t upgraded the old one. It hadn’t used a different name for its undo files. It had just noticed the existence of a vim undo file, deleted it (losing all of the data in it) and replaced it with one that vim couldn’t read.

I raised an issue about this and was told that the persistent undo format was unstable and users should not rely on data being preserved in a feature explicitly called persistent undo. It had changed once and would probably change again.

And that ended my experience with NeoVim. The authors showed immediately that they absolutely could not be trusted with any of my data. Breaking persistent undo is something I could forgive as a bug, but the attitude that just because something is a persistent file on your filesystem that contains data that you might want is no reason for their program not to delete it meant they had no concept of a duty of care to their users.

I liked this post (which I quoted almost in its entirety), because it covers a few important things:

  • I have never heard of the persistent undo like that, and it seems kind of amazing.
  • People do remember when software loses their hard work or disrespects them.
  • I can see how “It had changed once and would probably change again” can be such a powerful feeling.

I also loved it for the appearance of Raskin’s First Law. Jef Raskin, of Macintosh and Canon Cat fame, put together the three laws in his 2000 book The Humane Interface, and they go as follows:

  • A computer shall not harm your work or, through inaction, allow your work to come to harm.
  • A computer shall not waste your time or require you to do more work than is strictly necessary.
  • An interface is humane if it is responsive to human needs and considerate of human frailties.

It was a very important and formative book for me to encounter as a young designer. I have no idea how these laws haven’t made it to Unsung before today.
