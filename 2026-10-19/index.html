It's enough - vibecoding tag crossed from useless to harmful

lobsters • 294 points • by viraptor • October 18, 2026

The vibecoding tag has been getting more and more useless for a while now. It lost the original meaning as the culture of software engineering with LLMs matured a bit. It got silly when posts talking about LLMs in general got tagged. It got useless when posts even mentioning some project created with LLMs in passing got tagged. Now it actually got harmful to the community when tagging posts turns away very valuable members https://lobste.rs/c/vecuxj

I sighed and moved on for a while now as almost everything gets tagged - you can skip a word. But now it not only carries no signal whatsoever, it turns away people creating original technical content - which is what I assume anti-vibecoding people would actually like to see more of.

Please split it or kill it. But we'd be better off without what exists today.

Added: To be clear, I totally support people filtering out llm-generated or llm-centric posts. But right now everyone loses. And every day we lose a little bit more. Whether it's an original, interesting article that turns out to be nth-degree related to llms and filtered out, or a community member annoyed by this recurring problem and leaving us with even fewer actual non-llm-centric entries.

Updates to site guidelines and AI/LLM tags

lobsters • 170 points • by 355E3B • October 18, 2026

Hey folks,

I've been having to perform an increasing number of moderation actions surrounding the 

vibecoding
tag and AI/LLM related content. I started reading Lobsters because it had the most interesting viewpoints related to computing. During that time, I've found that this community is at it's best when it's trying to, in good faith, help others grow. In lieu of a larger and more rambling discussion on this point, we would like to try two changes encourage that behavior again and re-focus topicality around ensuring that content submitted to the site should, at a minimum, be respectful of a reader's time and intelligence. 

The first adjustment is two updates to the about page, which try to provide a bit more clarity in what we consider topical and what constitutes self promotion:

Intro:

Lobsters is a computing-focused community centered around discussing links. Our goal is to learn, craft, collaborate, and share interesting viewpoints.

The administrator is Peter Bhat Harkins ("pushcx"), contact him or another moderator with any support issues. Lobsters was launched on July 3rd, 2012 by joshua stein (who is no longer running it, so please quit bugging him with site stuff) with careful design touches to encourage a healthy community:

At this risk of writing a mission statement, it seemed worth explaining what the site is and hopes to be to visitors from the first sentence of /about. (This original text came from an intro post.)

Then the Guidelines:

Topicality: Lobsters is focused pretty narrowly on computing; tags like art don't imply every piece of art is on-topic.  Some rules of thumb for great stories to submit: Will this improve the reader's next program? Will it deepen their understanding of their last program? Will it be more interesting in five or ten years? Is this compelling material that will seed a discussion, or is it just an artifact?

Pending an upcoming deploy, we no longer allow submissions which are code repositories. While, at first blush, this may seem odd for a community which is primarily centered around programming but it does eliminate the bulk of the "low effort, self promotional" submissions we've been seeing on the site. We've also found that being dropped into a new codebase doesn't start discussions because the reader has to have enough subject matter experience to immediately recognize what's novel or otherwise worth talking about. Instead, we are more interested in content around code bases which cover why it was created, the purpose it serves, the challenges overcome, and the reasons why it's a useful artifact in the ecosystem.

Some things that are off-topic here but popular on larger, similar sites: entrepreneurship; management; careers; news about companies that employ a lot of programmers; investing; world events; anthropology; self-help; personal productivity systems; last-resort customer service requests via public shaming; "I wanted to see what this site's amazing users think about this off-topic thing"; and defining the single morally correct economic and political system for the entire world when we can't even settle tabs vs. spaces.

We're listing "careers" as off-topic to try to head off posts about interviewing, promotions, burnout, and farming. We have the job tag because it's nice to help people connect to jobs, but the topic of getting a good job, weaving jobs into a career over your lifetime, and work is a different thing than the actual programming itself.

Self-promotion: It's great to have authors participate in the community, but we do not want the community to be exploited as a write-only tool for product announcements or driving traffic to their work. As a rule of thumb, self-promotional activity should be less than a quarter of one's stories and comments in the last year. New users (first 70 days) are not allowed to submit self-promo.

This is how we've been enforcing the rule for a while, better to be explicit. It's unfortunately common that authors see their work posted, join the site, but then only use us for traffic. Hopefully this time helps them understand the difference between a symbiotic and parasitic relationship.

The second adjustment is that the 

vibecoding
tag is now marked as inactive. It still appears on old stories but can't be applied to new ones. From my perspective, the tag was overloaded with six differing responsibilities:

  • First, it was the tag which was trying to capture the discussions related to the use and implementation of AI/LLM tools.

  • Second, it was a marker for content which is partially related to AI/LLM tools, tooling or companies.

  • Third, it was a marker for content which includes a discussion on or output from AI/LLM tools in a part of a larger work.

  • Fourth, it was a marker for content which was generated using AI/LLM tools.

  • Fifth, it was being used in competition to the 

    spam
    flag, for really low-quality content which was generated via AI/LLM tools but without apparent significant human authorship.

  • Sixth, it was being used as a tag of shame by people who really dislike AI/LLM tools and used it to insult people at every passing mention.

The last couple years have been the biggest upheaval in the field for decades. While it's acceptable, within limits, to express frustration towards circumstance or technology, it's not acceptable to direct that behavior towards individuals whether they be members of the community or not. Other people can't be your punching bags and Lobsters can't be your boxing ring. That's not acceptable.

While `vibecoding` is no longer an active tag, we still feel that a subset of the tooling related discussions are relevant to the site. While this is reaching far back into the annals of computing history, tools like compilers, databases, testing, and vcs had similar points of consternation and concern when they were introduced. I'm not going to claim that I'm able to guess how the industry is going to shape up, but my gut feeling is that but my gut feeling is that it's going to be closer to something like we've seen with systemd than compilers. This new tag `ai-tooling` covers "The design, ux, development of AI/LLM-powered tools themselves".

As a fun addendum, there's a variant of Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs which exist for websites, we kinda fell on the wrong side of that trying to migrate the site and get this out on a Saturday. Whoops!

Hybrid Reasoning Agents: LLM agents that think in latent space, act through text

Title:Hybrid Reasoning Agents

View PDF

HTML (experimental)

Abstract:How can we build an agent that thinks beyond human language, yet still acts through it in the external environment? Modern LLM agents still reason and interact through text, while latent reasoning offers an efficient internal medium but lacks native tool access and reasoning transparency. We bridge these two directions with Hybrid Reasoning Agents (HybridAgents), which dynamically navigate between latent reasoning and text-based interaction throughout a unified generation process. Architecturally, a lightweight HybridLink enables seamless transitions between text and latent modes, while the agent's continuous working memory preserves lossless hybrid context across multiple turns. These designs preserve tool-integrated agentic capabilities while making latent thoughts directly inspectable. Building on this architecture, we further develop dedicated post-training algorithms for hybrid reasoning: HybridSFT stabilizes tool-integrated hybrid generation by teaching the backbone to coordinate with HybridLink, then HybridRL scales reasoning by learning when to think in latent space and when to act through text. We evaluate HybridAgent at 3 model scales across 11 benchmarks covering mathematics, science, medicine, search, and agentic reasoning. Compared with frontier LLM Agents and agentic frameworks spanning both text-based and latent methods, HybridAgent consistently improves performance by 10.4%, while delivering 35.5%-54.7% token reduction, 1.8x-2.6x faster inference, and 2.7x-3.7x cost savings. Someone behind the software

hackernews • 448 points • by tosh • October 18, 2026

Someone behind the software

I think good products carry something of the people who make them. Their experience, what they care about, and how they think things should work. You might not know who those people are, but you can feel their perspective in the product.

The industry often talks about software as though it comes out of a factory. You collect requirements, build features, and measure whether they work. Follow the process and you should get a good result. Now we're talking automating this whole pipeline with agents.

But two teams can talk to similar customers, have access to the same technology, and make very different things. The difference comes from how they understand what they see and the style they go about building it. The founder background or company DNA also shapes this a lot.

At Linear, we have always valued intuition over process or data. I think people sometimes understand that as making decisions based on personal preference, without much thought or evidence. What we mean by it is that your intuition develops through experience and understanding. You work on something, talk to customers, make mistakes, and learn what to pay attention to. The intuition is your point of view on something, and forcing to use your judgment, not hide behind faceless data.

Before we launched, we talked to people across companies and roles about how they worked. We had our own frustrations with this type software, but those conversations helped us understand what mattered beyond our own experience. Our perspective got better after those conversations.

So eventually the products develops a character through what its makers pay attention to, what they decide not to do and what they keep working on after it technically works. I sometimes think of this as the soul of a product. You feel that someone cares about how the whole thing comes together.

A feature can work perfectly well on its own and still make the product worse. Recognizing that requires some belief about what the product should be. Someone has to make the call, then stay close enough to customers to see whether they were right.

Recently, many products are starting to feel messy to me, including products from very successful companies. There seems to be more thought going into what the technology can do and what can be added than the form it should take. The product changes are primarily additive, not improving the core problem.

I think the reasons for this is that it's lot easier to add new capabilities, than rethink existing capabilities. So each product becomes a collection of abilities, without actually having unified point of view.

I wonder whether some of this comes from how the work is organized. Individuals work with agents to produce changes independently, each operating a small software factory. Each change might make sense on its own, but together they start to resemble Frankenstein’s monster rather than a carefully shaped sculpture.

Without the shared perspective, soul, or thought, the product becomes an average of all those outputs. The choices that once gave it character are being diluted by day. More things work, but it becomes harder to feel someone or even something behind them.

Making software is getting easier. I hope that gives people more room to develop a perspective and follow it further. To understand a problem deeply and keep shaping an answer until the pieces belong together.

I want to feel the creator behind the software, not the factories.

Microsoft’s decision threatens to undermine Nscale’s attempts to push ahead with a Wall Street flotation worth up to $35bn (£26bn), despite a brutal sell-off in AI computing stocks.

Nscale, which is backed by tech giants including Nvidia, has positioned itself as Britain’s “sovereign” data centre provider.

Ministers have talked up its Essex data centre project as emblematic of the UK’s AI ambitions, and have lobbied local councillors to push it through.

Microsoft’s backing for the project formed part of £22bn in UK investments promised by the tech company last year during Donald Trump’s state visit. Sir Keir Starmer said it was a step towards Britain “becoming a world leader in AI”.

The data centre site, which contains thousands of high-end Nvidia AI chips, was originally intended to come online next year.

Separately, Nscale has also faced delays at a flagship data centre in Norway, reportedly leading to heavy penalties.

Australian rival Firmus cancelled plans for a $20bn IPO earlier this month, citing market conditions. Other “neocloud” companies such as CoreWeave and Nebius have seen their shares and bonds slump.

Neoclouds are heavily dependent on raising new investment and debt to keep building, and soaring market interest rates have increased concerns about the sustainability of their business models.

An Nscale spokesman said: “We remain committed to both the Loughton project and the UK and we are exploring alternative power sources for the site, in addition to other expansion sites in the UK.

“Our relationship with Microsoft remains strong and long-term, and this unfortunate London-wide grid issue has no impact on our other contracts.”

Nscale was founded by Australian entrepreneur Josh Payne, a former coal miner, just two years ago, and has faced scrutiny for signing large contracts despite a lack of a track record in building data centres.

However, Jensen Huang, Nvidia’s chief executive, praised Nscale on Wednesday at an event in London, saying: “You could be the most valuable UK startup they have created in the last 10 years.”

High electricity prices and long waits for grid connections have been seen as a major barrier to building AI infrastructure in Britain.

The µRepRap is intended to be a RepRap (a replicating 3D printer) capable of micron (1/1000th of a millimetre, or 1µm) and sub-micron fabrication, made with only common and easily accessible materials. This degree of accuracy was initially made possible by 3D printed microscope platforms designed by The OpenFlexure Project. Variations more suitable for prototyping and fabrication have since been developed, but kudos to them. Expectations for the initial μRepRap prototype were to demonstrate repeatable positioning to better than 3µm on a work area 10mm across and to produce a probe tip in lieu of a print head that is suitable for manufacture in a simple home workshop. This goal has effectively been achieved.

0.5mm "Benchy" 3D test model printed with μRepRap, 4μm layer height

"PIKA" V0.02 hardware is producing repeatable positioning of approximately 1μm and reliable 10μm or better accuracy in an approximately 8mm cubic volume. Using a fine probe etched with the PIKA mechanism itself, UV resin droplets as small as 4μm have been deposited. With a fibre optic probe 9μm resin lines have been cured. This is the current recommended build.

PIKA V0.02 Complete Circa May 2026

This was devised as a testbed for flexure components, and is only mentioned because it has been thoroughly documented with a bill of materials etc. It was updated to V0.05 to give compatibility with PIKA Axis Drivers, but the full documentation is for the "MAUS C" V0.04 (essentially a debugged V0.03). This achieved approximately 5μm positioning best case over a 4mm square, though motion is not linear. Maus-C-Build-Process instructions have been created. If you want to download the pre-built STL files go here to Printables and day-to-day progress is detailed on the RepRap blog.

MAUS 'L' V0.05 μRepRap, in development Feb 2026

Microelectronics and Micro-electromechanical Systems (MEMS) are essential components of most of the electronic wizardry we use in our everyday lives, whether we realise it or not. In the way that RepRap brought Open 3D fabrication to the masses, the aim of the µRepRap Project is to bring users the same capabilities on a much smaller scale and allow those components to evolve in the same way. The solution is effectively a very precise and compact CNC machine.

The objective has two related parts: To create a macro scale 3D printed micron fabrication and manipulation system, and while doing this develop the techniques needed to reproduce a similar system printed at the micron scale.

A 1.3mm "Hello World" mechanically etched in Sharpie Marker on glass slide mid-2025

Precision manufacturing began somewhere in the 1700's, and the first micron scale electronic devices were fabricated from silicon in the 1960’s. The Intel 4004, for example, was made with a feature size of 10μm. Ever since then the technology focused largely on silicon, with fabrication systems becoming ever more complex, esoteric, and costly. The techniques used are difficult for the average hobbyist to manage, and in many cases are downright dangerous.

In biology and medicine, equipment to measure and manipulate objects on the micron scale are relatively common - though these devices tend to be large, specialized, and expensive. Recently though, microscope platforms capable of sub-micron resolution were developed by The OpenFlexure Project, and these have created an interesting opportunity for developing micron scale fabrication.

If micron scale manufacturing can be achieved by RepRap-like technology, it is likely that these fields will be advanced in the same way that desktop fabrication was by the RepRap. The biological sciences will gain from inexpensive, rapidly-evolving equipment. The microelectronics field will regain the potential for independent communities develop on the micron scale, and break away from its fixation on silicon as its main platform. As with 3D printing, there will certainly be new developments in fields that do not currently even exist.

There is also the possibility (and intent) that this technology could replicate in the future at a yet smaller scale.

Micron scale 3D printing has many of the same requirements that The RepRap Project developed when initially printing on the macro scale:

  • A 3-axis positioning system
  • CAD/CAM software
  • Axis zeroing sensors
  • A deposition system
  • Building material

In addition there is the practical aspect that humans are unable to directly manipulate micron scale assemblies and sub-assemblies. Novel systems are therefore needed to:

  • Detach printed items
  • Transport items
  • Rotate and position items
  • Conduct micron scale maintenance tasks
  • Operate and interface with items

Similar issues will exist if and when a micron scale system is developed.

While conventional optics are readily available to allow humans to initiate and inspect the fabrication processes, it is likely that some customisation of the optics will be desirable.

For the moment two USB microscopes are sufficient. One to observe the initial Z probe height during setup, and one for the XY positioning of the probe when commencing a print. A minimum of 8Mpixel resolution is recommended. Additionally a conventional bench microscope of 100x to 200x will be needed to examine the output.

"Hello, World" and a 100μm square set in resin using UV and a 125/9 optical fibre

Additionally, an optical fibre may be attached as the probe. This can be used to cure UV resin by projecting a sufficiently powerful UV source down the probe. This section describes how a 125/9 optical fibre patch lead and 3W UV LED module can be attached to the μRepRap as a print head.

The axes are driven by 400-step NEMA17 stepper motors, but 200-step motors will get quite close to 1μm. The driving hardware and firmware is left to user preference, though existing developments assume interactive control using GCODE on a serial connection. Arduino GRBL is adequate.

The probe is used as a Z touch sensor, engraving tool, and deposition head. A robust probe for initial testing can be made from a 0.5mm hypodermic needle or 0.25mm accupuncture needle. These are sufficiently robust to use subtractively on resist-covered surfaces such as Sharpie marker on a glass slide.

For fine deposition an electrochemically etched wire point is recommended. Instructions for fabrication with minimal equipment are in the Probe documentation. The PIKA is capable of etching its own probes.

Additive fabrication is currently implemented by transferring minute quantities of "Top Coat" UV nail gel resin with the probe. The reservoir is a smeared layer of resin. The probe accepts this resin by capillary action. When depositing, capillary action forms relatively consistent droplets. The number of consecutively deposited droplets and their consistency depend on the qualities of the resin and probe, but sub-15μm droplets are achievable.

Other resins and ionic gels may also be used. A UV LED in the print bed operated by the hardware controller sets the resin.

Note: Thin layers of 3D printer UV resin do not readily set in air as oxygen neutralises the activated photoinitiator.

Other substances may be transferred. They key limitation is that their solvent should not evaporate during deposition.

Materials that readily adhere to the resin (which may be partially cured first) such as gold leaf may be added.

The current μRepRap 3D printer is intended to provide a platform for further hardware and software development on the micron scale.

Much work remains to be done in determining what can be printed and in what materials. If objects cannot be printed high enough, or with overhangs, folded structures, hinges, and flexures will need to be developed to produce complex 3D shapes and mechanisms.

It is hoped that μRepRap may be used to eventually construct useful devices in its own right, and an ecosystem of fabrication utilities that operate on a yet smaller scale.
