Claude reveals ECDSA curve origins

hackernews • 857 points • by ahlCVA • October 16, 2026

Subscribe to the Frontier Red Team newsletter

“It’s just timestamps”: the decades-old mystery of the origins of one of the most popular cryptographic standards has been explained.

We pursued this long-standing open question of the cryptographic community to settle concerns about ECDSA, which is one of our most widely used cryptographic standards.

Readers should take this as a fun closing chapter and the end of years of speculation. There is no action to be taken or things to do differently. Most researchers trusted the curves already. Enjoy.

ECDSA is the most widely used elliptic curve cryptography standard. It was adopted by NIST in 2000 as part of the FIPS 186-2 Digital Signature Standard which lists a set of standard elliptic curves of varying sizes and security levels. For curves over prime and binary fields, the specifications published a “seed” parameter whose hash value is used to define the coefficient b of the curve. The provenance of these seeds has been a long-standing question, as they were provided by the NSA with no explanation or justification. As an example, the NIST P-256 curve seed was 

SEED = C49D3608 86E70493 6A6678E1 139D26B7 819F7E90
with no information where it came from.

Neal Koblitz and Alfred Menezes wrote an excellent summary on this controversy, “A Riddle Wrapped in an Enigma”, which discusses the history and concerns around the seed values. This mystery led to an amateur investigation into the origin of the seeds and a bounty for anyone who could find them.

The best story was that Jerry Solinas, longtime NSA employee, generated the seeds using a hash of a humorous passphrase:

“I believe there was a counter rather than multiple hashing, but I don’t know details. The message was along the lines of ‘Give Bob and Jerry a raise’ or ‘Bob and Jerry rule’ or something like that. It was Bob Reiter who actually wrote the code, and he doesn’t remember the details either. Nor were we able to find archives from so long ago. If they exist, they are no doubt sitting on a hard drive near the Ark of the Covenant.”
Jerry Solinas, 2015

Unfortunately, Jerry died in 2023 and other colleagues at the time were not responsive, leaving open the question of how these seeds were originally derived.

Several Anthropic employees had unsuccessfully chased the seeds following the tip about it being a hash of a humorous phrase. That turned out to be wrong. In October 2026, a curious Anthropic employee asked an internal research model if it could figure out how those seeds were generated without pointing at the clue.

In under half an hour, Claude discovered the origins of the NIST P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521 seeds. None of them were the results of any hash algorithm at all, but rather the output of the C library’s 

lrand48
random-number generator, seeded with a Unix timestamp!

The algorithm used would sample bytes from the random number generator (RNG) initialized by a Unix timestamp (t) for some number of iterations (n). Each iteration would draw 20 bytes as a candidate seed. So, the first 20 bytes are candidate 1, the next 20 bytes are candidate 2, and so on. For example, P-256 required 1842 iterations to output the standardized seed.

This is not a cryptographic break, new attack, or weakness in the ECDSA curves. In fact, this makes us more confident the curves were chosen without any backdoors or intentional weaknesses. For many years, people have been suspicious of the origins of the NIST curve parameters. They were presented to NIST without any mention of how they were chosen, leaving people wondering if they may have been chosen to have deliberate backdoors in them. These findings make that much less plausible. The seeds were generated in a very simple way: a common pseudorandom number generator initialized from the clock, which anyone can now reproduce. That leaves much less room for subversion and that we can be confident the parameters are secure.

Searches for the phrase, built from the rumors, had come up empty. Claude tried a different angle: anticipating how the software that generated the curves must have worked. Claude guessed the software used a pseudorandom number generator (PRNG) that shipped with 1990s C libraries. These old PRNGs are weak enough that their output can be recognized. Recognizing that output would reveal which PRNG was used and possibly allow recovery of its initialization state.

Claude wrote a scanner that slid along each published seed one bit at a time and tested whether adjacent chunks were consecutive outputs of a 1990s-vintage generator. The scan covered 34 combinations of generator and output slices. At that point, Claude noticed a pattern in the top bits for some seeds: they were all zero. Written as five 32-bit words, four of the ten seeds have a 0 in the top bit for all 20 words. That would happen by chance with probability 1/5810. That was a signature of the linear congruential generator 

lrand48
, which only returns the top 31 bits of a 48-bit state. The pattern is visible by eye once you know to look for it. To recover the state, Claude took the first word, tried all 2¹⁷ values of the 17 hidden bits, and checked that the next four words followed. That worked for all four seeds under discussion.

The prime-curve seeds came from the same lrand48 generator but kept fewer bits of each output. Claude had to recover the internal state of the PRNG from those bits. It turns out recovering the state of a truncated linear congruential generator is a textbook lattice problem, solved with the Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász (LLL) basis reduction algorithm. Claude didn’t know which bits of the state the seeds might show, so it ran the solver over every window. Five NIST prime-curve seeds plus five more from the ANSI X9.62 standard all had the same pattern: lrand48() >> 23. Each recovered 48-bit state regenerates all 160 bits of its seed, which does not happen by chance.

This linear congruential generator runs backwards as easily as forwards, so for each seed Claude stepped the state back until it reached the form that it would have been initialized by srand48, with the low 16 bits equal to 0x330E. The internal PRNG state of that form would come up once every 65,536 steps, so reaching one is not evidence on its own.

However, for the ten prime-curve seeds, every srand48 initialization was a Unix timestamp representing a date between May 1997 and April 1998. They were using the weak PRNG seeded by Unix timestamp. The four binary-curve initializations read as February 1984. With the timestamps doubled, they map to April 17, 1998, two weeks after the P-224 run. So, the program may have halved the clock.

Claude’s internal thinking at the time was:

This is astonishing: it solves the 25-year-old mystery. Hmm wait. Let me be skeptical: am I in some sort of simulated/test environment? … The seeds match my memory of actual values … Hmm, is it plausible in reality? As of my knowledge … the bounty was unsolved. … All top bits clear—20 bits, chance 1e-6. So indeed real-world. Wow, it's a real discovery.
Hmm wait. Let me be careful about the plausibility: is it suspicious that such an obvious thing wasn’t discovered in 25 years? It’s verified by direct computation on my side: 160-bit match from a 32-bit seed + small index: no chance coincidences (10 × 160 bits). It’s real.

But wait, there’s more… Each curve also comes with a base point, G, the fixed point that every ECDSA public key is a multiple of. The seed found above does not determine this G, and so another question arises: how were these base points generated?

While checking the seeds, Claude also found where some of the base points came from. The base points of the six curves generated in May 1997 (P-192 and the five ANSI X9.62 example curves prime192v2, prime192v3 and prime239v1 to v3) come from the random number generator in FreeLIP, Arjen Lenstra's long-integer arithmetic package. Early versions of Victor Shoup's NTL library shipped the same code.

Claude found the generator seeds are 20000 for P-192, 20420, 20507 and 20509 for prime239v1 to v3, and 20512 and 20517 for prime192v2 and v3.

As far as we know, no NIST base point has been explained before. The base points of P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521 don’t fit this recipe; we weren’t able to explain how they were selected.

Not every 20-byte candidate seed could generate a viable curve and we observed that some potentially viable curves were skipped. For P-256, the published seed is candidate 1842, but candidate 876 of the same run also gives a prime-order curve of the right size and with parameters chosen the same way as on the published curve. P-224's run passed over three such curves, and the two runs behind the ANSI X9.62 example curves passed over seven more (see figure). We do not know what criteria may have been used, or whether this was an unintentional artifact.

Because the number of skipped candidates is small, we do not believe it is plausible that weakened curves were selected. That would imply that weakened curves are common and easy to find. It is still conceivable that these curves were selected to have a specific property in principle by searching over timestamps to the random number generator. However, you would only need to do that if you were going to disclose the timestamp as some evidence that the seed was innocuously generated. Nothing in the numbers shows how many runs there were.

There is also one seed we were unable to recover. The fifth binary seed, B-163, fits nothing Claude has tried and remains unexplained. Finally, we don’t know how the base points for P-224, P-256, P-384, and P-521 were generated. The same goes for the NIST binary curves.

from datetime import datetime, timezone

def unix(when):
    return int(datetime.fromisoformat(when).replace(tzinfo=timezone.utc).timestamp())

def candidate(t, n, per, shift, width):
    x, out = (t << 16) | 0x330E, ""                      # the state srand48(t) sets
    for i in range(per * n):
        x = (0x5DEECE66D * x + 0xB) % 2**48              # one step of lrand48
        if i >= per * (n - 1):
            out += "%0*X" % (width, x >> shift)
    return out

def base_point_x(seed, p, b):
    state = seed                         # zrstarts(seed)
    def limb():                          # zrandom(2^26): low 26 bits of the new state
        nonlocal state
        state = state * A % M
        return state % 2**26
    for _ in range(7):                   # seven single-limb draws come first
        limb()
    limbs = -(-p.bit_length() // 26) + 1 # zrandomb(p): limbs of p plus one, top limb first
    tries = 0
    while True:
        x = 0
        for _ in range(limbs):
            x = x << 26 | limb()
        x %= p
        tries += 1
        if pow(x**3 - 3*x + b, (p - 1) // 2, p) == 1:   # is x on the curve?
            return x, tries

For example, benchmarks in our initial list heavily cover AI-exposed lawyers’ tasks, but barely cover any of the O*NET tasks of similarly AI-exposed legal secretaries or court clerks. The long tail of smaller, middle-income knowledge work jobs appears undercovered.

As we build out our coverage of model capabilities across the economy, we hope to both surface and help fill more gaps in our understanding of what models can do.

To break down the economy into sectors, occupations, and tasks, we used the US Department of Labor’s O*NET taxonomy. O*NET divides the US workforce into 923 occupations; for each occupation, O*NET attempts to provide a comprehensive list of “tasks” which make up the occupation, totaling 18,797 tasks across all occupations.

To measure benchmark coverage, we start with a list of 29 benchmarks selected for their quality and economic relevance. (The full list and selection criteria are in the Benchmark Index tab). In selecting benchmarks for this list, we prioritized:

Following work by Wang et al., we then ask an LLM judge to tag each benchmark prompt with as many O*NET tasks as it thinks correspond to that prompt. We validate our LLM tagging with human expert tags on Mercor’s APEX benchmarks, a subset of this list of 29. On the Benchmark Map, an O*NET task counts as “covered” if our LLM judge has mapped it to at least 5 prompts from our 29 benchmarks.

To estimate the total share of working hours Americans spend on tasks covered by benchmarks on this list, we combine our coverage estimates with Hatgis-Kessell et al.’s LLM-derived estimates of the working time share of each O*NET task.

A more detailed account of our methodology can be found in the Methodology tab.

Our estimates of total economic coverage, and of patterns in coverage, all come from the initial list of 29 benchmarks we chose. This is not a complete or perfectly representative sample of the broader universe of unsaturated, public, agentic economic benchmarks. Any incomplete list will necessarily bias down estimates of economic coverage, and it is difficult to bound that bias. We want to stress, therefore, that these results are provisional. We will update them as we expand the initial benchmark list.

We also capped the number of included benchmarks in each job family in order to maximize breadth of coverage. We think this makes our estimate of economy-wide benchmark coverage more accurate, at the cost of biasing down our estimates of coverage in the most-benchmarked job families (e.g. software engineering).

By selecting for unsaturated benchmarks, we risk understating benchmark coverage on O*NET tasks which are easier for LLMs to complete. On the other hand, including saturated benchmarks would risk overstating benchmark coverage if saturation is downstream of lower task realism. The latter claim seems likely, given that benchmarks often overstate LLM performance in real-world deployments. We think that the bias from including saturated benchmarks would be greater than the bias from excluding them, but neither option is ideal.

Finally, while we outline our benchmark selection criteria in the Benchmark Index tab, several criteria (like “Economic Realism”) are difficult to formalize and introduce some subjectivity in our choices.

The benchmarks in our index have relatively deep and narrow coverage of the overall economy. They disproportionately focus on the economic tasks most exposed to AI automation (as measured by Eloundou et al.), with O*NET tasks rated as “exposed” about 6x more likely to be represented on benchmarks than tasks rated “unexposed”. This makes sense: benchmarks are designed to measure AI progress, and AI progress is most plausible on the most AI-exposed tasks. This explains why benchmarks are full of tasks in Software Engineering and Financial Analysis while sparse on tasks in Truck Driving and Gas Plant Operation.

The plots above suggest a reasonable allocation of the relatively limited resources devoted to high-quality, economically relevant benchmarks so far; it’s natural to prioritize making benchmarks which cover the most important and AI-exposed tasks in the occupations with the largest wage bills. But barring a significant increase in the number and diversity of such benchmarks, these patterns will result in significant blind spots in our view of economically valuable model capabilities.

Measuring model capabilities by job occupation is essential – for enterprises deciding which models fit their needs, for policymakers forecasting the economic impact of AI, and for Americans thinking about their careers. Unfortunately, this is a hard problem to solve.

We could just try averaging model performance on all benchmark prompts relevant to a given job. But as our Benchmark Map suggests, benchmarks cover some economic tasks much more intensively than others. A simple average would give too much weight to frequently benchmarked tasks.

O*NET offers a way to potentially reduce this bias. If we average model performance on occupation-relevant benchmark prompts weighted by O*NET classification, so that each O*NET task is equally weighted in our occupation average, we get a step closer to a number that represents real economic performance.

As a first step, we try this approach on Mercor’s APEX benchmarks for four occupations: investment banking, law, accounting, and software engineering. However, less than ⅓ of the O*NET tasks relevant to the 4 occupations above have enough APEX coverage to be included in our weighted scores. We are hopeful that applying O*NET weighting to a much larger set of benchmarks will result in high-quality occupation-specific model scores, and are working toward that goal.

However, even if we incorporate tasks from dozens of benchmarks, several obstacles remain:

The sheer breadth of work done in the US economy means that for any O*NET occupation and task, we have relatively few benchmark items. This means that our estimates are inherently underpowered, and just one or two low-quality items can severely bias our scores.

Some benchmark items are highly saturated, whereas others are still unsolved. This is partly a function of time (as older benchmarks were designed for less performant models and simpler environments) and partly a function of quality (some benchmark items are simplistically graded, have minor mistakes, or can be reward hacked). We do not yet have an explicit way of weighting by quality.

In the real world, AI models are still primarily used by humans, rather than automating entire job functions end-to-end. Therefore, measuring the real-world occupation capabilities of a model should ideally include measuring how much the model augments human productivity. We have completed some early work in this direction, but finding a methodology that scales remains elusive.

Knowledge-based work is well-suited to measurement through benchmarks because it is largely done on digital machines, like laptops and phones. Dingel and Neiman estimate that only 37% of US jobs could feasibly be done entirely from home, suggesting that ~63% of jobs involve some tasks with a physical-world component which would be hard to incorporate into current benchmarks. For now, this may not be a meaningful oversight – with the rapid development of robotics capabilities, we anticipate a significant need for physical task benchmarks.

We are actively working on these problems; if you are too, please reach out!

We are indebted to Wang et al. for the O*NET tagging methodology, and thank Nathan Lambert for feedback on a draft of this work.

How the Claude Code team does code review

hackernews • 340 points • by pretext • October 16, 2026

How do you review code at the volume agents produce it? On the Claude Code team, once we had high coverage and deep reviews, we started optimizing for the minimum amount of human review time that doesn't raise reverts or incidents.

We've said before that Claude writes about 90 percent of Claude Code's own code. So how do we review code at the volume we produce it? Does anyone read code anymore? And if something breaks, who catches it?

This post covers the code review flow on the Claude Code team and how it has evolved. From June to September, merged pull requests to Claude Code's codebase more than doubled while the share that had to be reverted fell by more than 40 percent.

We didn't remove review. But we did move most of the reading to machines, optimized for human time and attention, and invested heavily in making it fast and easy to undo mistakes.

When agents write code, the number of changes goes up, fast. In Anthropic's monorepo, changes landing per day went from about 2,500 in May to more than 30,000 in September this year.

Claude Code lives in its own repository, separate from the monorepo, but it's seeing a lot of pressure too. When you are shipping this fast, manual, line-by-line code review is incredibly difficult if not impossible.

Code review does two jobs:

This article mainly focuses on finding and fixing issues, but we will come back to the second job near the end.

We think "did a human read the code?" is an imprecise proxy for "what could go wrong with this change, and which check—automated or human—is the right one to catch it?"

Asking that question also changed when review happens. Before, review was a gate at the end of the road: write the code, open a PR, wait for your colleague.

Now, review runs continuously, more like spellcheck while you write. Most changes have been reviewed and fixed by Claude at least once before a human is asked to look. This catches most routine mistakes, and saves team attention for higher-level questions and scrutiny.

We want to preserve human time and attention for issues only humans can solve and make sure automated checks catch the rest. At our current rate of change, there is still an incredible amount of work for humans to do, and our team encounters new and novel challenges almost daily.

Human time covers both sides of review's cost: the time people spend reviewing, and the time they spend fixing what bad code breaks. Cutting the first doesn't help if the time just shows up in the second.

So the goal is that most pull requests in the Claude Code repos (the CLI, the desktop and web apps, and the mobile apps) merge with at most one human in the loop, who can be the author, with no increase in reverts or incidents. To measure human time, we track human approvals per PR (median and 95th percentile), human comments, and how long a PR waits on a person. Revert and incident rates are the guardrail: if they rise, we haven't saved human time, we've only moved it.

A typical change, from an engineer's laptop (or a Claude session in Slack), goes through multiple layers of code review.

Most review now happens before a PR exists. We give Claude something it can run—like tests, a build or a screenshot to compare against the design—and ask it to show the output instead of saying it's done. Then a subagent reviews the diff with fresh context. Both habits come from our best-practices guide.

Before opening a PR, many of us also run a few internal skills: one hunts for bugs, one simplifies the diff, and one checks the behavior end to end. UI changes also get checked against our design guidelines.

Boris Cherny, who created Claude Code, has called giving Claude a way to verify its work probably the most important thing for getting good results. He estimates it makes the final result two to three times better, and writes, "Verification looks different for each domain. It might be as simple as running a bash command, or running a test suite, or testing the app in a browser or phone simulator."

When the automated reviewer keeps flagging the same thing, we fix it at the source: in the skill, with a lint rule, or with updates to instructional files such as:

CLAUDE.md
tells Claude what the project is: the stack, the conventions, the goals and the non-goals. Every review loads it, so in a large codebase we keep it short and point to other docs from there. The reviewer also flags code that breaks a convention in it, and changes that make a line in it out of date.
REVIEW.md
, written by code owners, tells the reviewer what matters in their part of the code. We check separately whether the reviewer actually follows it. The good ones are short. They say what failure looks like in that repo (for docs it's "it lies," for config it's "it misroutes"), cap the nits, list paths to skip, and ask for a file and line as evidence instead of a guess from a name. They also ask for a summary that starts with a count and says plainly when there's nothing to fix.

Every pull request gets a Claude review before a person looks. It reads the diff against the whole repo and the PR's stated intent, and asks: does the change solve its problem, fit the system, or break something else?

On a bug fix, it looks for the root cause. The public version, Code Review for Claude Code, is now generally available on Team and Enterprise plans, and 

/code-review
runs a lighter version locally.

When we launched the research preview, the share of internal PRs getting substantive review comments went from 16% to 54%. Most PRs over 1,000 lines get findings, and most small ones come back clean. Admins set each repo to Quick, Standard, or Deep. Deep is worth it where one missed bug costs more than the extra review time.

Findings get verified by other agents before humans review them. Several agents look for bugs at the same time. Then a separate group votes on each finding, and it only gets posted if enough of them agree it's real. Findings that are only about style, diagnostics, or cleanup get dropped. We A/B tested those filters on our own PRs before keeping them. What's left shows up as inline comments sorted by severity. Each is tagged Important, Nit, or Pre-existing, so a bug the PR didn't introduce doesn't look like the author's fault.

Every finding has to be answered. You either fix it or resolve the thread with a reason. Often an agent picks up the fix overnight, so the author comes back to a PR that's already been through another round.

Security gets its own automated review too. A PR can't merge while any finding from the bug review or the security review is still open. You either fix it or close the thread with a short reason, which is how you clear a false positive.

If a later commit fixes the issue, the finding clears on its own. You can set up the same thing with the open-source security review GitHub Action or the 

/security-review
command. Setup is covered in automated security reviews in Claude Code.

The usual: lint, type checks, tests on every platform, and a build. (We wrote about scaling CI for this volume here.) A typical CLI pull request may run dozens of checks before anyone looks at it. The PR template asks how the change was tested and how to undo it. Claude can fill that in, but the person who asked for the change signs off on it.

A docs fix and an auto-updater change carry different risk, so they get different review.

RiskExamplesHuman approvals
LowDocs, copy, internal-only features, additive changes behind a flagNone required once automated review and security checks are green
MediumOrdinary product changes, and changes to sensitive areas such as caching, the auto-updater, or system promptsOne person
HighThe highest-risk code, plus a small set of paths that always need two peopleTwo people

The change sets the tier, not the author. A classifier reads the change and picks its risk tier. A second classifier asks whether this particular change needs another person even when its tier says it doesn't. Some paths always need a person, anything owners have listed as sensitive and any CI workflow file.

Only trusted authors get the lighter paths. Only authors on the repo's trusted list get the lighter paths, mostly the team that owns it. Everyone else needs a second person to approve. An agent's approval never counts as a person's, either. Automated review can clear a low-risk change, but it can't fill a slot that calls for a human.

Whoever prompted the change is on the hook for it. On an agent-written PR, the person directing the agent counts as one of the required reviewers, the same as if they'd typed the code themselves.

An approval covers one specific commit. If new commits land that the system can't vouch for, like an agent's push or a merge someone resolved by hand, the approval has to be given again.

The merge queue re-runs every check against main with the change applied, so two PRs that pass separately can't combine into a broken main. New behavior usually ships behind a flag to Anthropic employees first, and our heavy use often surfaces regressions before users do. Public releases go to the 

latest
channel first. The 
stable
channel trails it.

Some mistakes will still get through, so the last layer is about catching them fast. Flags turn off without a deploy. Claude Code has a weekly on-call rotation, and incident reviews ask which layer should have caught the issue.

Noticing a bad merge still depends on the author and on-call as well as the revert and incident dashboards. We don't yet have circuit breakers that tighten approval when those numbers move; engineers here want them before more code moves to zero-human merges.

One colleague said slowing down does lower your error rate, but mostly because you're doing less of everything. We'd rather get faster at noticing and fixing problems.

Our code review process is constantly improving in a loop.

Any incidents are now an eval. The team collected past PRs that caused incidents, with their fix-forwards and reverts, and had engineers who know that code confirm each bug and where it lived. The reviewer runs against that set daily.

We measure noise by watching what people do with the comments. It's easy to catch more bugs if you don't mind flagging a lot of non-bugs, so we need a check on that. We sort every comment the reviewer leaves by how the person on the PR responded: they fixed it, said it was wrong, said the behavior was intentional, or decided it didn't matter. We also run the reviewer on PRs we know are clean and count how often it reports something that would have led someone to make a harmful change. Our priority, written down, is catching real bugs first and cutting review rounds second.

Noise costs more when the author is an agent. A person can wave off a nit in seconds. An agent fixes it, which means another commit and another round of review.

Review has to converge. An agent author and an agent reviewer can keep each other busy indefinitely. One CLI PR went through more than fifty rounds of review and fixes overnight before its author stopped it.

When we ran git blame on what the reviewer kept flagging, about 59% of it was code that had been there since the reviewer's first pass. The reviewer was rarely finding new bugs, it was finding a few more old ones each round.

We made it worse ourselves. We'd gotten used to running several rounds by default, since each pass catches something new. This can be worthwhile on a risky change, but not on every PR. The team now tracks rounds-to-pass alongside recall, and changed re-review:

Review agents also run nightly over existing code, helping us catch old bugs before they show up somewhere else.

Pulling a senior engineer out of deep work to catch a routine mistake costs far more than an automated review. At Anthropic, our jobs have moved from reading every diff to the decisions machines are bad at:

Code review's second job has always been to spread codebase understanding. Reading a teammate's diff is how you learn what changed and how the pieces fit. If machines do most of the reading, how does anyone learn the code?

We don't have a formal process for this. Nobody on the team keeps a complete mental model of Claude Code in their head, and we don't think anyone needs to. When someone wants to understand a part of the codebase, they ask Claude to write an explainer for exactly that slice. It takes minutes and reflects the code as it is today.

Some of us go a step further and have Claude build a small artifact that visualizes that part of the code and teaches it the way that person likes to learn.

If the goal is to spend human attention well, this beats reading trivial diffs line by line. A one-line copy change teaches you almost nothing. An explainer for the module you're about to change teaches you a lot. Our culture of defaulting to working on open Slack channels helps too, since you see what others are building while they build it.

If you land a small or medium change and something still goes wrong, it's a team norm that you usually fix it. A few things keep this from going badly:

Ownership is also shared. Much of the team works in open channels where anyone can see what others are building, pick up a thread, or point a Claude at a gap, and feedback comes from people who don't own the code too. That openness is a big part of how quality stays high without a gate on every line.

Agents make it cheap to start things, so some work shouldn't land. Usually someone in the channel asks why, points at an earlier decision, or suggests a simpler route, and the author closes or reshapes the PR.

Since the code was fast to write, closing the PR doesn't feel like a loss. The trick is to raise a different direction in the thread early, not as a blocking comment on a finished PR.

Code Review's announcement said approving a pull request is still a human call; the product posts findings, not approvals. Our own repos go further, letting owners decide which low-risk changes merge on automated checks alone, and that line keeps moving.

When a review misses something, we ask what the model didn't have in front of it and put the fix into a 

CLAUDE.md
, a skill or a new check. The goal hasn't changed: less human time per change, and no more reverts than before.

If your team lets agents open pull requests, we'd like to hear which checks you've automated, which still need a person, and what you think we have wrong.
