Lean verification of the Riemann Hypothesis

Joint statement by the Lean and Mathlib communities, endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative.

On November 20, 2026, Levent Alpöge released a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis as a Lean library comprising 17 million lines of code.

We have full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct, and we congratulate him on this achievement.

Our rationale is outlined below.

The codebase depends directly only on Mathlib. Over the past day, the Lean and Mathlib communities have rallied to verify the proof's correctness. We hereby certify that both the statement and the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis are correct. The four criteria in https://xenaproject.wordpress.com/2026/11/12/and-the-proof-is-checked-in-lean/ are all satisfied.

  • The statement of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct. This statement is part of Mathlib: RiemannHypothesis and has been carefully vetted by experts in Lean and number theory.
  • Alpöge’s library compiles correctly. By now, dozens of people have reported correct compilation of the codebase, reproducing the same raw proof export file. The library compiles without errors or build failures.
  • The code proves the correct statement. Crucially, the community has added a file that isolates the statement of the Riemann Hypothesis and imports only Mathlib. That statement was compared against the theorem proved by Alpöge using two independent tools (
    comparator
    and 
    con-leche –compare
    ), both confirming that the theorem proved in Alpöge’s library is exactly Mathlib’s statement of the Riemann Hypothesis.
  • The Lean proof is valid. The exported raw proof has been checked by several proof checkers, including Lean’s official kernel, the 
    con-leche
    and 
    con-ron
    checkers, and several independent checkers contributed by third parties and implemented in different programming languages. The checkers 
    con-leche
    and 
    con-ron
    come with a formal proof, relative to set theory, that they cannot accept a false proof, thus addressing concerns that Lean’s logic itself might have flaws that the proof could exploit. Pill to treat pancreatic cancer hailed as 'biggest breakthrough in decades'

hackernews • 1100 points • by rafaelc • November 20, 2026

A pill that can shrink and potentially eliminate pancreas tumours is being hailed as the biggest breakthrough treatment for the cancer in decades.

In one in 12 of the patients given the drug, daraxonrasib, during a trial, the cancer became undetectable, and the tumours stopped growing in almost all of the 61 patients. The trial participants had recently been diagnosed with advanced pancreatic cancer that had spread to other parts of the body.

Though doctors have said the pill cannot yet be considered a cure, and they do not know how long it can keep the disease at bay, they have said it is encouraging news because it appears it will offer patients more time, even if it does not cause full remission.

Half of all people diagnosed with pancreatic cancer die within three months, and only about 3% of patients survive for five years when the disease spreads to other organs. It has the lowest survival rate of all common cancers in the UK and has a similarly poor prognosis globally.

Patients were followed for a year and a half during the trial on average and some did not see the cancer return during this period. Longer studies will be conducted to see if any of the cancers can be put into remission. During the trial, tumours stopped growing in 90% of patients, just over 60% of patients had their tumours shrink or disappear, and for about 8% of patients tumours became undetectable on CT scans.

A year after starting treatment, 76.6% of patients were alive; usually 40-45% of patients given chemotherapy alone survive a year.

Dr Wungki Park, a gastrointestinal medical oncologist at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in New York, who presented the findings, said “this is the closest to cure”, adding that he had never seen so many tumours disappear in patients whose cancer had spread.

He presented the findings at the EORTC-NCI-AACR Symposium on Molecular Targets and Cancer Therapeutics in Barcelona. “We are beginning to see not only a higher percentage of tumour response rates, but also deeper responses and a near doubling in the duration of disease control, which may eventually translate into longer survival,” he said.

“The median follow-up is 18 months. Just imagine, in an average of 18 months your disease just disappears on the CT scan … that’s closest to complete remission … it’s incredible.”

Daraxonrasib is a KRAS inhibitor treatment, which works by locking on to and shutting off certain mutations found in most pancreatic cancers. These mutations drive tumour growth.

There are hopes the treatment could work for dozens of other cancers because they can have similar mutations.

Michelle Mitchell, Cancer Research UK’s chief executive, said more research was needed, but added: “This is the biggest leap forward we’ve seen in decades for pancreatic cancer patients and could offer real hope to people facing one of the deadliest and most difficult-to-treat cancers.

“These results raise the exciting possibility that this breakthrough could eventually pave the way for treating other cancers that carry Kras gene mutations. More trials are now needed before this treatment can be assessed for use on the NHS and despite this advance, pancreatic cancer survival is still very low.”

My monitor was cutting my download speed by 75%

hackernews • 608 points • by soheilpro • November 21, 2026

My Monitor Was Cutting My Download Speed by 75%

Somehow I am one of those few very lucky people to have the opportunity of owning a 2.5 gig symmetrical line for my internet. But one day, when I wanted to brag to my equally nerdy friends, shock and horror: 600 Mbps down? Luckily my upload speed was still 2.4 Gbps, so I didn’t lose all my bragging rights, but that’s weird, right? If anything, you’d expect the opposite!

My ISP is pretty cool. It’s run by this one guy (Hey Keith!), and I know I can always message them and they respond promptly, as they did this time. We tried to run through a few debugging steps, they checked on their end, and everything was normal. I tried my work laptop too, same speed.

I didn’t have time for debugging, because I needed to travel 2000 km to the other side of the continent for a family emergency.

But as soon as I got back, I got curious again. What could it be? In retrospect, I now know when it started: when I moved all my connections onto my Studio Display XDR. I wanted to have a one-cable setup, and the monitor offered me that. I’ve had it for half a year now, and I don’t think I’ve ever run a speed test through it. 600 Mbps was more than fast enough for me to never realise I wasn’t getting three quarters of the speed I was paying for 🤦

The setup#

  • MacBook Pro M3 Pro
  • 2026 Studio Display XDR, with everything plugged into it so switching laptops is one cable
  • UGREEN USB-C to 2.5G Ethernet adapter, plugged into the display
  • UniFi Dream Router 7, the same one from my WiFi 7 debugging post

What I thought was interesting#

Pings to other wired computers during a download would fail 10% of the time over Ethernet through the adapter plugged into my screen, and 0% of the time over Wi-Fi. What made it stranger was that there were no dropped frame errors in macOS’s logs at all. This was the big hint for me: the adapter was dropping them before the computer could know.

I then plugged the adapter straight into my Mac instead of the monitor, and ran an iperf3 server on my UDR 7: 2.3 Gbps. So the cable, the router and the adapter are all fine? What about fast.com? Perfect speeds there too! 2.2 down and 2.3 up when connected straight in. As soon as I connected it to the display, it was back to 600 down again.

So it’s the display, right? Well, I would find out: sort of yes, sort of no, which is why I wanted to write this blog post. I couldn’t find anything online explaining the exact cause, and it’s quite a cool reason.

The driver and flow control#

The adapter uses a Realtek RTL8156B, which on macOS binds to Apple’s generic USB networking driver. But Realtek ship their own driver on their website, so I thought I’d give that a go! I installed it and, to my surprise, it almost doubled the download speed to 1.1 Gbps, but that was still nowhere close to 2.5. First hint that it couldn’t just be the monitor’s fault.

Then I tried flow control. I had never truly looked into this before, and I think it’s quite neat to know about if you were also ignorant of it like me. Basically, when a device receives Ethernet frames it can’t keep up with, it just drops them. With flow control (it’s something your router or switch has to support), it can send the sender a “pause” frame, and the sender then buffers the data on its side, instead of the receiver (the adapter in this case) on its side. I turned it on in my UniFi console and guess what, download jumped to around 2 Gbps, with upload still at full speed! Yay! Well, not so fast…

I don’t like it always being on, because it can add latency to other systems like my gaming PC. Can you work around it? For sure, you can disable it on those computers, but it’s janky, and it’s another thing I need to remember when my Sauerbraten game starts lagging. I need fewer things to remember, considering I am 30 in a couple of months. My memory is not what it used to be.

But the upload was always fine#

Yeah, isn’t that weird? First of all, it looks like the port can indeed do 2.5 in both directions. I already knew that, because I get much more than that from my external SSD through the monitor’s Thunderbolt port. So the issue is not the monitor again? But it has to be!

Well, I had another idea. How about I connect to my Linux box, which is also on 2.5, and send perfectly smooth UDP traffic at a fixed rate with flow control off? Then I can check the chip’s own counter of dropped frames:

Sent Lost behind the display Frames/s reaching the Mac behind the display Lost direct on the Mac
1.46 Gbps 0% 123,800 0%
1.75 Gbps 0.05% 148,600 0%
1.94 Gbps 0.1% 165,000 0%
2.03 Gbps 2.7% 168,000 0%
2.12 Gbps 7.2% 168,000 0%
2.20 Gbps 10.5% 168,000 0.001%
2.28 Gbps 13.5% 168,000 0.02%

So basically no drops whatsoever when connected straight to my Mac, but loads of drops after about 168k frames per second (roughly 2 Gbps). Remember that number. That number comes from the adapter’s own counters: however much I sent, the number of frames it managed to hand over to the Mac each second flattened out at about 168,000, and everything above that was dropped.

Why did this not happen on my SSD? What was the only difference I could find? Bursts!

When the Mac reads data from a USB 3 device, the device sends some packets, then has to wait for the Mac to say “lgtm, send more!”. The SSD enclosure sends 16 packets before needing a go-ahead message back. The RTL8156B in my Ethernet adapter sends 4.

Here is a diagram of my best understanding of why this is an issue. I made this diagram 100 times, and I think I can probably improve it more, but if it helps you understand the issue, I have done my job.

As you can see, the speed of packets coming into the adapter is 2.5 Gbps. That’s what the router pushes in. The speed going out is around 2 Gbps. The reason is that if you add up the average time it takes to get the go-ahead through my monitor’s hub, which is around 7.6 µs, to the average time it takes to send 4 KB, around 8.2 µs, you can find what the maximum speed is.

Well, if the speed of the drain from my adapter to my Mac is half a gig slower than the speed of the input, then you can imagine the buffer getting filled more and more until it overflows. Those are dropped packets, which result in a hard backoff when the router resends them, so you drop loads in speed. And that’s the reason for all of this.

OK, well, what now?#

I have a cupboard full of USB-C Ethernet adapters. Don’t ask.

The problem is they’re all 1 gig adapters, so any of the tests I made would not tell me anything, because the issues don’t start at 1 gig. They start at around 2… If I run my 2.5 gig adapter at 1 gig speeds, I get 1 gig downloads with the official driver. If I run it at 1.5, I get 1.5. If I run it at 2, well, I get 1…

So I thought I would do what any human would do, and I spent more money on a new adapter. Another UGREEN, but this time it’s one that promises 5 Gbps and uses the RTL8157 chip instead: UGREEN 5G USB-C Ethernet adapter. My reasoning was that if this one could get me better speeds, it would help me understand how. And that it did!

With this adapter, I dropped 0 frames:

Sent Frames/s reaching the Mac Dropped by the adapter
1.5 Gbps 124k 0
1.9 Gbps 157k 0
2.1 Gbps 173k 0
2.3 Gbps 187k 0
2.4 Gbps 194k 0

Why? Well, looking through what each adapter tells me about itself:

      
$ python3 -c "import usb.core; e = usb.core.find(idVendor=0x0bda)[0][(0,0)][0]; print('packet size', e.wMaxPacketSize, 'bMaxBurst', e.extra_descriptors[2])"

# old adapter (RTL8156B)
packet size 1024 bMaxBurst 3

# new adapter (RTL8157)
packet size 1024 bMaxBurst 7

# bMaxBurst counts the packets after the first one, so 3 means 4 packets and 7 means 8

I can see that instead of sending 4 1 KB packets per burst, it would send 8! And the speed of the link was also 2x faster, so those 8 KB would go 2x faster to the Mac. That means the time spent was 8 times lower to send the same data, and while the wait for the go-ahead was identical, the theoretical speed from the adapter to the Mac, including the wait for the go-ahead, was a maximum of 4.5 Gbps. That’s much bigger than the 2.5 coming into the adapter, so the buffer would never fill up, packets would never drop and everything was great, even if the “go-ahead” response was still much slower than when connected directly to the Mac.

Final thoughts#

I think the big takeaway from this is that all the things you think are quite easy to understand, such as transfer speeds, have so many cool intricacies that it’s worth diving deep every so often. Who would’ve thought that the speed of the link between the adapter and the Mac was defined not only by the link speed itself in Gbps, but also by the time it took your Mac to reply, by the amount of packets, by the speed of those packets, by the number of packets in a burst, and by the size of those packets?

See you next time, when I will spend just as much time on another trivial thing that we can all learn from!

LookAtMyQuant is a quantization compiler. It measures how much each stored piece of a model hurts the output when compressed. It then picks a code for each piece so the whole model fits a size budget with the least damage.

On Gemma 4 E2B, its GGUF has 12× lower KL than Q4_K_M at the same file size. The method and every result are on the site.

The code is under the MIT license.

LookAtMyQuant 2 takes Qwen3.5-4B to 1.23 GB, 2.3 bits per weight. The file solves 330 of 540 exact-answer math problems. Prism ML's Bonsai 2 Ternary 4B solves 328 at 1.20 GB, and the original solves 359. Its KL from the original on held-out text is 0.45 nats, against 0.59 for Bonsai.

runtime_mlx/drafter_q35.py
adds speculative decoding. A small drafter proposes the seven tokens after the model's latest one in a single pass. The model then checks all eight positions in one pass over its coded weights (

On an M5 Max, over 30 held-out prompts of up to 256 tokens, generation with the drafter was 2.0 times as fast: 176 tokens a second against 88, timed in alternation in one process on 2026-11-11. A check kept 4.3 tokens on average. Over eight everyday prompts it was 1.5 times as fast. The gain follows how predictable the text is: 2.5 times on algebra, 2.0 on code, 1.2 on chat and 1.05 on a story, and one of the 30 held-out prompts ran slower with the drafter. The text matched plain greedy decoding on every prompt. These are speeds after the first token; reading the prompt takes about 0.1 s either way.

Two later changes are in this code. Each was measured against the version before it on 40 paired generations: proposing from the 96,033 most common tokens was 6.0% faster, and picking the winning token inside the packed product was 5.3% faster. Linear mode also enables self-calibrating adaptive fallback by default (

adaptive=True
; pass 
--no-adaptive
to disable): when rolling acceptance drops below the measured break-even ratio of drafted-check latency to serial-step latency (accounting for cache flush and drafter context replay), it pauses drafting for short exact serial bursts and resumes once acceptance recovers, preserving bit-exact greedy parity while avoiding slowdowns on low-acceptance prose.

Methodology

We gathered details about Anthropic’s data center commitments from Epoch AI’s tracker, Anthropic’s announcements and filings, announcements and filings from partners (including Amazon and SpaceX), and reporting from The Information, Semafor, and others.

We first identified the source for each data center’s power. For projects with on-site gas (Monarch, Nexus, Colossus 2) or new grid gas (including indirectly contracted projects such as the Schahfer expansion for the AWS New Carlisle site and Entergy Mississippi’s gas plants for AWS’s Madison and Ridgeland facilities) we used emissions factors from permits where available, or permit values from comparable facilities. For grid-connected facilities without associated new generation capacity, we assume these sites will be powered by marginal, dispatchable grid capacity. We used EPA’s AVERT tool to model which power plants would meet the new incremental load from Anthropic’s facilities based on each site’s expected power use and region. AVERT uses historical EPA data for these power plants to model the expected short-run marginal emissions rate of CO2 and criteria pollutants (NOx, PM2.5, etc.). We use AVERT because we are interested in the short-term impacts from new data centers coming online in the next several years.

To calculate the amount of power used by each site, and in turn the total quantity of air pollutant emissions, we assumed power usage effectiveness (PUE) ranges from 1.1–1.2 for all sites (typical values for hyperscale data centers are between 1.05–1.3), and power utilization ranges from 60–80%. See LBNL’s 2024 Data Center Energy Usage report for more details about how these parameters vary in typical hyperscale data centers. For our mid-case, we assumed a PUE of 1.15, a power utilization of 70%, and that all 9.6 GW of identified compute capacity is online in 2028.
