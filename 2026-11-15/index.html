Lovable apps expose passport details and more than 200K private messages

hackernews • 859 points • by armcat • November 14, 2026

Lovable: Many Apps Built on the Platform Expose Sensitive Data

AI makes it easier to build your own web applications, but handling sensitive data still requires special care. We examined 159,041 Lovable projects and found many cases where personal and confidential information was accessible without logging in.

About Lovable

Lovable is a Swedish AI startup founded in Stockholm in 2023 by Anton Osika and Fabian Hedin. The platform turns plain-language descriptions into web applications and is designed to be accessible even to people with little or no programming experience.

Lovable grew out of Osika’s open-source project “GPT Engineer”, released in 2023. The project evolved into the GPT Engineer App before being renamed Lovable in late 2024 and launching publicly that November.

The Promise

Lovable can build impressively sophisticated web projects with AI, from static websites to full applications with user management and database connections. But as soon as an application handles confidential information, one question becomes crucial: Is that data protected against unauthorized access?

In its marketing, Lovable repeatedly emphasizes that no technical knowledge is needed to build projects. This can give the impression that the platform takes care of security too. Yet Lovable’s Terms & Conditions place responsibility for applications and data handling on the customers themselves.

That leaves a troubling gap between the promise of an easy-to-use, all-in-one service and the responsibilities customers are expected to take on.

Against this backdrop, we investigated how well sensitive data in Lovable applications is protected, in collaboration with Handelsblatt. Lina Knees reported on the findings on November 2, 2026 (in German).

Research Question

We wanted to find out whether sensitive data in Lovable applications was accessible without logging in. We looked separately at data sent when a website first loaded and additional content we checked by making further read-only requests with the same API key.

The result: We found many clear examples of sensitive data left unprotected.

Methodology

We focused on the database service Supabase. Its integration is relatively easy to analyze, and we believe it is the most widely used database service among Lovable projects. Lovable’s own database features, introduced in late 2025, are still less widely used. Similar misconfigurations may occur there too, but we did not examine them in this analysis.

Using datasets from the open web archive Common Crawl, we identified and analyzed 159,041 Lovable projects from December 2025 to September 2026. Of these, 34,545 used Supabase.

24,664 websites sent database records from Supabase as soon as they loaded, without requiring a login or any other interaction. That alone is not a security issue: the data may be intended for public display. But by making additional read-only requests, we were able to retrieve more database content. We found records containing email addresses in 10,560 tables across 7,403 of the 24,664 websites (30%).

Limitations

Email addresses can help identify records containing personal data, but they may also be public contact addresses or test data. At the same time, this approach misses personal records that contain no email address. These effects partly offset one another, though we could not establish to what extent. The 30% figure should therefore not be read as a confirmed data leak rate. It applies only to the 24,664 websites we examined more closely. Our sample, drawn from Common Crawl, is also not representative of all Lovable applications.

We retrieved only parts of the datasets rather than downloading them in full. The figures below reflect the records we actually downloaded; the affected datasets may contain more. We are not publishing details of how to access them, as doing so could make further access easier.

Data Leaks

We found many serious cases of exposed data, including:

  • A German lifestyle forum exposed more than 80,000 profiles containing full names, email addresses, dates of birth, and gender information. More than 200,000 chat messages were also accessible.

  • Several event organizers and restaurants exposed tens of thousands of records relating to ticket and food sales, with no login required. The records we inspected included email addresses, phone numbers, postal addresses, payment information, and ticket codes.

  • A property management company exposed AI-generated summaries of internal emails containing sensitive information about tenants, property owners, and business partners.

  • In a particularly serious case, a service provider exposed more than 1,000 records containing identity document details, including passport numbers, expiration dates, and nationalities. These highly sensitive identity details could be used for identity theft.

  • Other accessible content included tens of thousands of contracts, invoices, personal photo albums, and internal WhatsApp communications.

  • We also found numerous newsletter subscriptions, waiting lists, and contact and support inquiries. In one case, 280,000 emails sent to a provider’s customers were accessible.

Depending on what it contains, exposed data like this could be used for phishing, ticket fraud, or identity theft. We could not establish whether any of it had been misused.

Handelsblatt contacted Lovable with the findings of our investigation.

Implications

Our analysis shows that many Lovable applications leave even highly sensitive data unprotected, with potentially serious consequences for the people affected.

Access control mistakes can happen in any application, whether written by people or built with AI. With Lovable, database integrations follow recurring patterns, making it easier for attackers to look for similar misconfigurations across many applications. An approach that works against one app may then work against others with little extra effort. Users without a technical background also face a harder task spotting and preventing these flaws.

An easy-to-use development platform does not remove the need for an expert review of access permissions before an application handles confidential data.

Automated checks are no substitute for a professional security assessment.

Before an application handles real customer or business data, its access permissions, interfaces, and separation between user accounts should be reviewed. These checks should be repeated after significant changes. If the necessary expertise is not available in-house, bring in qualified support.

Why get an expensive Pi when I have an old phone?

hackernews • 568 points • by casi • November 10, 2026

I’m working on a project that needs a high resolution camera, GPS, storage, and a microcontroller to control some lights. (More on the project soon!) I’ve been designing PCB adapters around a Raspberry Pi, an Arducam 64MP camera, and a GPS module. Every time I add another part, the bill grows.

I’m also trying an ESP32-P4 board, which will get its own post soon. But the alternative I keep coming back to is an old smartphone.

I have a Redmi 13C 5G and a Huawei Mate 30 5G. Phones like these turn up second hand on Taobao for roughly ¥300–500 (£33–56 / €40–67 / $45–75). The Redmi gives me a camera that saves 50MP JPEGs; the Mate 30 has a 40MP main camera and a telephoto lens. Both already contain GPS, storage, a clock, a battery, mobile data, and a touchscreen. Their USB-C ports give me a route to the PCB that will handle the lights and other sensors.

These are illustrative November 2026 listings. The more fully equipped Pi total uses the cellular HAT’s GPS instead of buying the separate GPS HAT; its modem is 4G, while the phones are 5G models. Neither side includes my custom light controller PCB, a SIM or data plan, or an enclosure. The conversions use 10 November 2026 mid market rates of approximately ¥1 = £0.111531 / €0.133643 / $0.14991, rounded to whole units in the table.

The phone is already becoming more than a price exercise. I’ve unlocked and rooted the Redmi, tested its camera, and got it sleeping and waking for scheduled work. I can plug it into my computer and have Codex configure and test the pieces I need. The Huawei is harder to root, but I can still run a kiosk app on it. I’ve made a stand to photograph the same target with the Redmi and Arducam side by side; I want to see what detail each camera actually captures. A 50MP JPEG file does not, by itself, settle that question.

The open question is supply. Finding two good used phones is easy; finding a few hundred matching ones, with healthy batteries and predictable camera behaviour, would take more work. I feel increasingly confident that it’s possible.

This could be useful well beyond the current project. For digital artworks and installations, we’ve often used a Pi or a Mac mini. Imagine instead posting a client a configured phone: plug it into the display and it runs. With mobile data and remote access, I can fix something without asking a gallery for Wi-Fi privileges or an open port.

There’s something fun about taking a device made in enormous numbers, giving it a second life, and finding that most of the hardware I was about to assemble is already inside it.

A family tree of software job titles

hackernews • 344 points • by umerf • November 14, 2026

For a few years I was ‘Head of DevOps’. Before I got that job title I’d done much the same work under other names, and since then I’ve watched the same work get called SRE, and then it seemed to morph towards platform engineering. If you’ve been in this industry for any length of time you’ll have seen titles come and go like this, and you might have wondered where they all came from.

So I (with the help of Mr Claude) built this family tree of software job titles, from the 1940s to now.

It’s here: ianmiell.github.io/sw-job-title-family-tree

What It Is

There are 104 titles, from ‘Computer’ (a person, not a machine) at the top to ‘AI Agent / Context Engineer’ at the bottom.

Time runs from top to bottom, in five-year rows. The titles sit in seven coloured columns by family:

  • Development
  • Analysis, architecture and leadership
  • Operations and infrastructure
  • Networks and security
  • Data and AI
  • Quality and test
  • Support and customer-facing

A solid arrow means the title grew out of its parent. A dashed arrow means influence, or a common career move. Landmark titles are in bold.

On the site you can click a family to see just that column (here’s Data & AI), or click a title to get its history, background and connections (here’s SRE).

The whole thing is one Graphviz file and one JSON file of descriptions. Each title is one line in the 

.dot
file, and the picture is rendered with:

$ dot -Tsvg job_title_family_tree.dot -o job_title_family_tree.svg

Adding a title is three small edits: a node in its family’s column, an entry in the row for its five-year period, and its parent arrows.

A word on the dates. They’re roughly when a title became common, not its first recorded use, and they’re my best estimates, not sourced citations. Some are well known (SRE at Google in 2003, ‘DevOps’ coined in 2009, ‘Data Scientist’ in 2008). Plenty are a ‘c.’ and a shrug.

Six Things I Noticed

Drawing it out made a few patterns hard to miss.

1) Coding Started Out as the Clerical Bit

In 1947, Goldstine and von Neumann’s ‘Planning and Coding of Problems’ split the work of using a computer in two: mathematicians planned the problem, and coders translated the plan into machine instructions. Coding was treated as lower-status clerical work, and much of it was done by women.

It turned out that coding eventually became the hard, creative part, and the coder merged into the programmer.

Go back further and it’s stranger still. Several of the first ENIAC programmers were recruited from the Army’s human computers, people whose job was to compute by hand. So the job gave its name to the machine, and its people to programming.

2) Titles Break Apart, Then Join Back Up

The webmaster turned up around 1993, and was often a single person who wrote the HTML, ran the webserver (often a box under a desk), answered the email, and kept the site up (sometimes by kicking it, more often by plugging it back in after kicking it).

By the mid-2000s the title had all but gone, as it was unfashionably ancient. The code went to web developers, the servers to sysadmins, and the content to editors and designers.

Then the web developer split into front-end and back-end around 2005, and the two joined back up again as ‘full-stack’ around 2008.

This is the centralisation/distribution see-saw I’ve written about before, playing out in job titles.

3) Some Titles Don’t Last

The prompt engineer made headlines in 2023 with a few eye-wateringly well-paid job listings. As the models got better, the skill became part of other jobs, and on the tree it gets absorbed into the AI engineer almost as soon as it appears.

The scrum master is a slower version of the same story: demand peaked in the 2010s, and many organisations have since folded it back into engineering management or delivery roles.

The newest title on the tree is the ‘AI Agent / Context Engineer’ (c. 2025). The description for it currently says “time will tell whether it stays a separate title”. I’m not taking bets.

4) Names Get Recycled

‘Systems Engineer’ is two different jobs on the tree. At IBM in the 1960s it was a pre-sales role, working with customers to design installations and get them running. In the 1990s, thanks largely to Microsoft’s MCSE certification, it meant a more senior, design-oriented sysadmin. Same title, different branch, no relation.

‘Configuration management’ started as a 1960s US defence discipline for tracking hardware changes, became software version control in the 1970s, and then came back decades later as the name for tools like Puppet and Chef.

5) The New Thing Is Often an Old Thing

The ‘forward deployed engineer’ (FDE) is one of the hottest titles of 2025, as OpenAI, Anthropic and plenty of AI startups hire FDEs to get AI working inside their customers’ organisations.

Palantir built its business around them from about 2010: software engineers embedded with a customer, often on site, building whatever is needed to make the product work for that customer’s problem.

Which sounds a lot like IBM’s systems engineers of the 1960s, who worked with customers before and after the sale, designing installations and getting systems running. (It also sounds a lot like old-style consulting, but I would say that.)

6) The DevOps Debate Is There

Ben Treynor Sloss defined SRE as “what happens when you ask a software engineer to design an operations team”. DevOps was meant as a culture of developers and operations working together, but by about 2011 ‘DevOps Engineer’ had become a job title, usually for whoever did the automation. On the tree, the arrow between SRE and DevOps points both ways. Platform engineering has both of them as parents, and was sold around 2022 as ‘the successor to DevOps’.

The Dev/Ops divide has never been resolved. It just keeps getting new names.

Why Bother?

When I’m asked what the difference is between a DevOps engineer, an SRE and a platform engineer, the honest answer is always a little history lesson. This is the shortest version of that lesson I’ve managed so far.

The dates are best guesses, and I’ll have missed titles, mangled some lineages and drawn arrows people will argue with. If you know better, let me know, or raise a PR on the repo.

Which brings me back to the coders.

In 1947 coding was the clerical bit, and the real work was in the planning. Wind forward eighty years and you’ll hear much the same said about coding again, because AI. Last time, the coders turned out to be doing the hard part.

I’ll leave you to draw that arrow yourself.

We're Asking an Appeals Court to Require a Warrant for ALPR Searches

hackernews • 254 points • by nobody9999 • November 15, 2026

Today, along with the ACLU, EFF filed an amicus brief in the Sixth Circuit court of appeals in United States v. Sturdivant, a case where police warrantlessly tracked a suspect’s movements using Automated License Plate Reader (ALPR) data. Our brief details the vast, interconnected network of ALPRs that constantly track drivers around the country and argues that this tracking is a “search” under the meaning of the Fourth Amendment.

Sturdivant involves a series of robberies in and around Cleveland, Ohio. Using surveillance camera footage, police identified the make, model, and color of the robber’s car, but they did not know its license plate number. After running an open-ended search of two ALPR databases, Flock and ELSAG, officers learned a car belonging to the defendant had been tracked near sites of the robberies, eventually leading to his arrest. Faced with a motion to suppress the ALPR evidence, the district court held that Mr. Sturdivant had no reasonable expectation of privacy in his license plate number because it could be observed by members of the public, and that therefore he had no Fourth Amendment interest in not having his car tracked in public places.

But ALPRs are simply not comparable to observing an individual license plate on a passing car. As we’ve explained in many amicus briefs and in our lawsuits directly challenging ALPR use in San Jose, California and Wisconsin, ALPRs give law enforcement “a virtual time machine to view suspects’, or anyone else’s, past movements (and much more) with just the click of a button.” Just last month, a federal district court agreed and held that ALPRs are a “form of indiscriminate mass surveillance” that can violate the Fourth Amendment.

We hope the Sixth Circuit will reverse the lower court in this case and hold that ALPR searches require a warrant.

Court denies motion to dismiss DRAM price fixing case

hackernews • 231 points • by ybathaee • November 15, 2026

Key Points

  • Judge Noël Wise denied in full Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron's joint motion to dismiss a proposed class action alleging they conspired to cut supply of conventional DRAM, such as DDR5, and shift capacity to HBM.
  • The court held the case was not a rerun of DRAM II, the Ninth Circuit's 2022 affirmance of dismissal of an earlier DRAM conspiracy suit against the same companies.
  • The judge found that long-term HBM lock-ups, refusals of long-term conventional DRAM contracts, Micron's Crucial closure, fab delays, the failure to expand despite record prices, near-identical customer vetting and the companies' prior cartel history supported a plausible inference of conspiracy.
  • The court gave no weight to three of the plaintiffs' arguments: investor statements about "supply discipline," which it found too similar to statements the Ninth Circuit's DRAM II decision had already held insufficient; executives' meetings at industry conferences, since DRAM II all but forecloses relying on them unless plaintiffs identify what was actually exchanged or agreed there; and the three-company market structure, which it called neutral.
  • Answers are due Dec. 7, 2026, and the initial case management conference was reset for Jan. 12, 2027.

A federal judge on Nov. 14 refused to dismiss a proposed class action accusing Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron of conspiring to restrict the supply of conventional dynamic random-access memory, or DRAM, the standardized memory chips used in computers, phones and servers.

U.S. District Judge Noël Wise of the Northern District of California denied the companies' joint motion to dismiss in full, finding the complaint plausibly alleged an agreement in violation of Section 1 of the Sherman Act. The allegations, accepted as true at this stage, have not been proven.

The companies argued the case was a rerun of DRAM II, in which the Ninth Circuit in 2022 affirmed dismissal of claims against the same three companies over a 2016–2018 supply restriction. "The Court finds that it is not," Judge Wise wrote.

The suit was filed June 25, 2026, by 17 consumers and small businesses from five states. It concerns conventional DRAM, which the complaint describes as "standardized memory built to industry specifications and sold for ordinary purposes," including DDR5 generation modules built for computers.

According to the complaint, the three companies controlled more than 91% of global DRAM revenue as of the third quarter of 2025. They allegedly cut production starting in late 2022 and, beginning in 2023, shifted capacity to high bandwidth memory, or HBM, a specialized DRAM product for artificial intelligence accelerators that "requires approximately three to four times the silicon wafer area per gigabyte compared to conventional DDR5 DRAM."

By the third quarter of 2025, conventional DRAM prices had risen 171.8% in one year, according to the complaint, which alleges a 697% increase over 18 months. Yet "not one of the three Defendants expanded or announced plans to meaningfully expand commodity DRAM production capacity," the complaint alleges.

Without direct evidence of an agreement, the plaintiffs needed plus factors, additional facts suggesting a preceding agreement rather than conscious parallelism, in which competitors independently make similar moves.

Judge Wise rejected the companies' argument that timing gaps undercut parallel action. SK Hynix and Micron announced cuts three weeks apart in late 2022, she noted, and Samsung's lag of about four and a half months, until April 7, 2023, was "not such an unreasonably long delay that it defeats an allegation of parallel conduct on a motion to dismiss."

She found the complaint plausibly alleged that the three companies acted in parallel when they cut supply, shifted production from conventional DRAM such as DDR5 to HBM, and wound down DDR4.

Under Ninth Circuit precedent, action against self-interest must be "so perilous in the absence of advance agreement that no reasonable firm would make the challenged move without such an agreement." Judge Wise wrote that a pivot to HBM by itself "might not be sufficient," but that "the related conduct tips this factor over the edge to weigh in Plaintiffs' favor."

According to the complaint, all three signed long-term contracts locking in HBM supply, Samsung and SK Hynix reportedly refused long-term agreements with major conventional DRAM customers, and Micron announced on Dec. 3, 2025, that it would close Crucial, its 30-year-old consumer brand. Those moves, which she called "difficult if not impossible to unwind," plus the failure to expand despite record prices, "are the sort that at the pleading stage, it appears no reasonable firm would make without a prior agreement," she wrote.

She applied similar reasoning to allegations that Samsung and Micron delayed or halted new DRAM plants, citing the Ninth Circuit's 1990 Petroleum Products decision that "Changes in refinery capacity are not readily reversible." "The Court views the commitment to build or not build manufacturing capacity as analogous," she wrote. She also counted the companies' alleged same-quarter 2025 decisions to wind down DDR4 as a plus factor, citing the complaint's allegation that "older, slower DDR4 came to cost more per gigabyte than newer, faster DDR5."

Judge Wise found that near-identical customer-vetting practices all three companies allegedly adopted in January 2026 "strongly support a plausible inference of conspiracy." She rejected the companies' "obvious and non-conspiratorial explanation" of booming AI demand, noting that the vetting began after the AI boom and the HBM pivot were underway.

The court gave no weight to three other arguments. Judge Wise held that investor statements, including Micron's 2022 reference to "supply discipline," did not distinguish the case from DRAM II. Though the conference-meeting allegations went beyond those in DRAM II, she wrote that DRAM II "seemingly all but forecloses a trial court from giving any weight to trade conference meetings" absent specifics, and she called the market's oligopolistic structure "neutral in the analysis."

According to the complaint, the companies and two other manufacturers formed a criminal cartel to fix DRAM prices from 1998 to 2002; Samsung paid a $300 million fine, Hynix paid $185 million, and Micron received immunity for cooperating as a whistleblower. Judge Wise, like the DRAM II court, rejected the argument that the now-20-year-old history should not count.

At the hearing, the companies quoted the late Judge Posner's line that competitors in concentrated markets "watch each other like hawks." Judge Wise wrote: "But Plaintiffs' allegations go beyond hawks watching each other; their contentions are more akin to hawks flying in formation – an activity that hawks ordinarily do not do."

The plaintiffs are represented by Bathaee Dunne LLP. Samsung is represented by O'Melveny & Myers LLP, SK Hynix by Cleary Gottlieb Steen & Hamilton LLP, and Micron by Gibson, Dunn & Crutcher LLP.

Judge Wise ordered the companies to answer by Dec. 7, 2026. Because the court had reset argument by about three weeks, she also granted the companies' unopposed administrative motion to continue early case deadlines and reset the initial case management conference for Jan. 12, 2027, at 9 a.m.
