Fingerprints are the new cookies

hackernews • 464 points • by royfish • October 11, 2026

You’re still the ….

Now open it on your phone or some other device and your nickname will be different and just as consistent.

Is it different in incognito? Good. But it’s always the same? Bad news.

Imagine this:

Most websites you visit get paid to show ads and indirectly run my script that lets me know you’re the …. I can use it to decide what ads you’ll see. But more importantly, if you’ve made an account on that website, I can offer to buy your data - whatever they can share under their privacy policy: your name, activity, conversations with AI…

Pretty soon I’d know more about you than your friends do - perhaps more than you know yourself.

“So what?” - you say.

“I have nothing to hide. And I prefer my ads to be relevant… sometimes they show me exactly what I need! And the data is anonymised anyway…”

See, the thing is, it’s not about you at all. It’s not really about the ads anymore either. Nobody cares about you. And if the police or your government happen to care, I’ll gladly take their money too.

I look at all this data and I don’t see people. I see patterns. Patterns emerging out of patterns. A living, digital fractal state of the economy, society and human psyche. The crystal ball of the 21st century and it’s made of silicon! That’s the product.

And to the highest bidder it goes!

What does that make you then? A gear? An ox? A volunteer?

Fingerprints are the new cookies

Remember the big fuss about third-party cookies? It was so big that the EU had already rolled out one of its greatest inventions - consent banners.

If EU ships faster than tech companies, that can only mean the tech companies are sitting on their hands. And one of those tech companies happens to hold two-thirds of the browser market.

What was the play?

Let’s give it up for Google - the biggest ad company on Earth!

ACT I. PRESSURE - “PRIVACY IS PARAMOUNT TO US, IN EVERYTHING WE DO.”

It’s 2019 and the cookies are still hot. It’s become bad PR and Safari and Firefox already block them.

A year later Google pledges: we will destroy cookies!! And establish a new, privacy-first reign.

ACT II. DISTRACTION - “INSTEAD OF DEPRECATING THIRD-PARTY COOKIES, WE WOULD INTRODUCE A NEW EXPERIENCE”

5 years later: people are now preoccupied with far greater worries than cookies, and the solutions Google has developed (Privacy Sandbox) kinda suck and have raised antitrust concerns so nobody uses them.

“You know what, let’s just keep the cookies. We’ll mask your IP address* though!” (* but only when incognito while authenticated) (** in the end they didn’t even deliver on that)

ACT III. ACCELERATION - YOU CAN NOW USE DEVICE FINGERPRINTING IN OUR AD PRODUCTS

Now there’s a better way to know who’s who!

How does fingerprinting work?

Nice, let’s nerd out properly.

You visit a website. Your browser sends a request to the server and the server returns code for the browser to run.

The request already reveals information about you; what browser you use, the language it’s in, the OS that runs it and your IP address, which the server needs to send you the response. That often reveals your location.

Then the code runs.

See, we’ve made a lot of civilizational progress in making browsing safe. Long gone are the days when getting a virus just by visiting a website was a daily concern. The thing is, the definition of safety varies depending on who you ask. The broad consensus ends at isolation: a website’s code can’t see the data stored by other websites, and it can’t see your files or other applications without permission. Chrome and Edge stop here. Then you have Safari (especially on iOS/macOS 26+) and Firefox extending that definition to privacy, but they still don’t fully protect you from fingerprinting. Brave and Tor are the leading privacy-first browsers, with up and coming Mullvad Browser (Mullvad being, in my opinion, one of the best VPN providers out there).

The code runs on your hardware, which can be measured in ways that produce consistent and unique readings. There’s not much that can be done about this really, so there’s only two established solutions:

  • feed a little bit of noise here and there. It’s called farbling and that’s what Brave does to make your fingerprint inconsistent.
  • make everything look the same, always, so there’s no telling browser users apart. Tor and Mullvad adopt this approach.

Ok, let’s create a fingerprint

So there are two sources for data that can make up your fingerprint; the network request and the code environment.

When you look at network, the data is generally either fragile or generic:

  • the IP address; in a ‘happy path’ where one person always uses the same IP address, it might be the only data you need. But commonly people do a lot of browsing on mobile networks, they share the WiFi with others, they use VPNs or their home network’s IP address is rotated by the ISP. It’s fragile as such.
  • headers like 
    User-Agent
    or 
    Accept-Language
    ; reveal your browser, OS, versions and the language set.

When you can execute code on the device, you have ways to measure its hardware qualities (without any permission prompts!) and these are surprisingly stable:

  • GPU; behaviour, vendor, driver
  • CPU; behaviour, vendor, number of threads
  • memory size
  • display data; resolution, pixel ratio, refresh rate
  • audio processing
  • device model

And on the software level; installed fonts, extensions, system info…

Millions of devices use the latest Chrome, there are millions of Macs with the M4 chip and there are millions of browsers in French.

But when you look at devices that run the latest Chrome in French on an M4, that number now drops significantly. Add to it hardware measurements and it gets so low it becomes practically unique.

The fingerprint doesn’t need to be truly, globally unique - it just needs to be unique enough to tell people apart in your sample size.

A ZIP code (inferred from IP), a birth date (you’ve given away on some platform) and sex (you fill it in everywhere) together are enough to uniquely identify over 60% of Americans.

You can always add another variable to decrease the potential pool of candidates.

Information Entropy

Have you ever played Akinator? You think of a character, a genie asks you some twenty questions, and somehow it knows. That’s fingerprinting in a nutshell, except your browser answers the questions for you. Once you understand information entropy, Akinator’s magic turns into a simple trick.

Let’s simplify with that game where you have to guess the character on a board through questions like “Is my character a man?”. If I say “yes”, and half of the characters on your board are men, you’re now considering only half of the total candidates. Now we can say that my answer gave you 

log₂(100 / 50) = log₂(2) = 1
bit of information, because it cut the pool of candidates in half.

The bits you get from one answer are called its surprisal: the rarer the answer, the more it tells you.

  • a trait shared by 1 in 2 people is worth 1 bit (is a man),
  • 1 in 8 is worth 3 bits (has a mustache),
  • and 1 in 128 is worth 7 bits (has one eye).

Surprisal quantifies the value of the answer, and entropy quantifies the value of the question. If 1 person in 128 is one-eyed, then asking about it will yield ‘no’ 127 times in 128.

Answer Probability Surprisal Entropy contribution
Yes1/128
log₂(128 / 1) = 7 bits
 
1/128 x 7 = ~0.055
No127/128
log₂(128 / 127) = ~0.0113 bits
 
127/128 x 0.0113 = ~0.011

Summing up the contribution of each possible answer, the question itself is worth ~0.066 bits of entropy - that’s the average amount of bits you gain from an answer.

When we combine traits that are independent we simply add up their bits or multiply the ratios, so a man with a mustache and one eye would mean 11 bits, or 1 in 2048. Alas, as Thomas Bayes would have you know, nothing is ever so simple in practice. A mustache is already a strong indicator of a man, and a man is probably more likely to have one eye. The traits are not independent so the bits don’t really add up.

Some interesting applications of this type of reasoning:

  • In medical diagnostics: A positive result from a 99% accurate test for a disease known to affect 0.01% of the population is about 100 times more likely to be wrong than you are to have the disease. You need more evidence!
  • In cryptography: A password made out of four random words is as strong as eight random keyboard characters (but much easier to remember!)
  • In forensic science: singling out one person in a city of a million takes about 20 bits. A witness description that matches 1 person in 1,000 provides only 10 bits, which still leaves a thousand possible suspects.

So what can I do about it?

Test your browser:

  • coveryourtracks.eff.org shows you how unique your fingerprint is, with entropy for each measured data point.

If you’re using Chrome you’re basically walking around with an ID on your forehead.

Safari and Firefox offer some level of protection, but you’re still identifiable by advanced trackers.

Brave, Tor and Mullvad Browser are the only major browsers at the moment that implement advanced protections against fingerprinting.

If you don’t feel like switching browsers, I get it. I am a big Arc fan myself and unfortunately it’s discontinued and will never have any privacy features. There are extensions like JShelter - they disrupt fingerprinting scripts by intercepting browser API calls. But they also break some websites. Same problem applies to Firefox’s Resist Fingerprinting feature.

The ironic truth is, privacy extensions make you more unique, since fewer people have them. So a smart tracker could detect that, ignore the signals disrupted by the extension and focus on the ones that are not.

No extension can be as effective as engine-level protection built into the browser. So if you don’t want to be tracked, you gotta commit.

It's now less "two groups of people speaking past each other" and more a sharp funnel. - Napkin math somewhere around 6B people (~75% of the population) have barely come in contact with LLMs at all. - Around 1-2B (~20%) are casual and infrequent users of free-tier ChatGPT-like products. This group sees derpy chatbots and treats them a bit like a better Google search, a writing aid, or etc. Maybe an agent tries to book you a flight. Somewhere around 20M people (0.2%) see first-hand that large, complex projects that used to take them weeks/months can now be completed by agents with a prompt. Building apps, copying apps, translating apps, decompiling apps from binaries... This has all happened very quickly and recently - less than 1 year ago, I was writing code manually by hand, typing memorized computer code commands into a code editor character by character, occasionally pressing Tab to autocomplete a little chunk of code. - And finally we get to the ~5,000 people (~0.00006%) with access to frontier-grade systems internally. The external world has seen the preview. It looks like swarms of thousands of agents collaborating over weeks on software mega projects: minting zero days, running cyber attacks and defenses at machine speeds, discovering new science, advancing the frontier of mathematics. Things that would have taken top professionals in the industry years of work. Meanwhile, human review and comprehension are starting to fall behind. For example, people are still involved in the "archeology" of the OpenAI-HF incident from many months ago. Mathematicians may be poring over the 722 manuscripts on frontier mathematics for a while. The funnel is driven by a combination of factors: 1. The impact scales with ambition, problem size, and horizon. A question with a paragraph answer barely stresses the system. This aspect primarily drives the area (e.g. math&code) dimension of the funnel. 3. Access. Free-tier, paid-tier, internal. So this is the weirdness of the moment. The world's biggest accountancy firm helped in falsifying documents to hide Roman Abramovich's wealth and evade tax, BBC Panorama has found.

Deloitte Cyprus said it "acts in compliance with applicable laws".

The findings are being revealed following a joint investigation between BBC Panorama and the Bureau of Investigative Journalism.

Until 2005, Deloitte - now the biggest of the "big four" firms which dominate the accounting industry - owned MeritServus. After they split, the two companies worked closely together with Abramovich's associates to manage his global fortune.

The leaked documents from MeritServus reveal some of their practices.

Our first example of backdated paperwork comes in 2006, after a new law in Cyprus meant that company accounts had to be made public. The new transparency law threatened to expose the secrets of Abramovich's vast wealth.

Deloitte Cyprus had prepared draft accounts for 2003 - but had not yet finalised them - for his Cypriot company Amiscan Investments. The accounts detailed $672m of luxury property.

A mansion in West Sussex, a luxury flat in London and an estate in the south of France were all included - plus helicopters, private jets and a small fleet of boats the oligarch owned at the time, including the $147.5m Pelorus.

To keep them secret, Abramovich's associates and Meritservus decided to rewrite history. They drew up a plan to circumvent the transparency law by transferring the assets to Amiscan's parent company in the British Virgin Islands (BVI), where they could be kept hidden. And they backdated the change by three years to make it look like the asset transfer had happened in 2003.

Deloitte Cyprus reviewed the backdated documents and said they were "adequate" to allow them to remove the assets from Amiscan's accounts.

When the 2003 accounts were signed off by Deloitte in 2006, the homes, planes and yachts disappeared at the stroke of a pen. Only $75,000 of assets remained.

Deloitte signed off the accounts even though the leaked documents show it knew they were completely misleading.

Lord Sikka, professor of accounting at the University of Sheffield, said it was a "violation of a statutory duty" and "pretty scandalous" to misrepresent transaction dates and not to publish true and fair accounts.

Panorama also found evidence that backdated documents had been used in a tax evasion scheme.

This involved another one of Abramovich's Cypriot companies, Kravin Investments, which had almost $2bn in the bank in 2003.

It had been earning large amounts in interest, so Roman Abramovich's company should have been paying tax on that interest to the Cypriot authorities.

But his advisers had another idea to rewrite history.

An email from MeritServus in August 2005 to Abramovich's Russian associates said a plan had been devised after a meeting with Deloitte. "If you agree, it will be necessary to change the financial statements and the accounting records accordingly for 2003 and 2004," the email read.

MeritServus informed the oligarch's Russian associates, that Kravin's "Provisional Tax Assessments for 2005" would be filed showing the company earning no income from the money.

A month later, an associate in Russia emailed in response, "I agree".

MeritServus then created a series of backdated documents which falsely purported to show that Kravin was only holding and investing the funds on behalf of its parent company in the BVI.

This made it look like Kravin did not actually own the money, so it would not be liable for tax in Cyprus.

Deloitte wrote in a September 2005 email that "certain changes will have to be made to the accounts (bank account balances, interest income, profit and loss account)" because of the backdated changes to Kravin's assets.

So Deloitte was apparently advising on the falsification of financial records. The evidence suggests it allowed Abramovich's company to evade millions in tax.
