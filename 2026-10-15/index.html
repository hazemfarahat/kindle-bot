Things to know if you get cancer (2026 Edition)

I was diagnosed with squamous cell carcinoma in my mouth in May 2026. Here are some things I learned based on that experience so far.

  • You need to be doggedly persistent on scheduling things. Yes this means lots of phone calls. If you get an appointment a few weeks out, call back a few days later and ask for something sooner and see if they have any cancellations. I could only get an appointment 4 weeks out with an ENT (this was before I knew anything was wrong). My wife made me call back and I was able to get in that week due to a cancellation.
  • AIs may downplay the seriousness of things. I was chatting with various models and most pushed me in the direction of “you don’t have cancer” for a while. Just something to note.
  • You may need to push for comprehensive genetic testing. Some doctors were weird about this and didn’t order it promptly, even after telling me they would. My surgeon, who told me he’d get it done, eventually referred me out to an oncologist. This caused a multi-month delay to getting genetic results, since the first results were done on the surgery tumor, not the biopsy tumor. No one cares more about your case than you, so take control and send lots of follow-ups.
  • You can find the “Standard of Care” flow charts for every cancer type. Look and get through this yourself to see what the options are based on how things have progressed. NCCN guidelines. You may need to make an account, which you can do as a non-professional. I like the PDF files. They also have an interactive navigator. Use AI to help you figure out what to pick based on your case.
  • If surgery is recommended, I would try and talk to someone who has undergone the surgery. I did not do this and regret it. Ask them how intense things were, what it felt like, etc. My surgeon talked me through my surgery, but it was a lot rougher on me physically than I expected. Ask the surgeon how many of these surgeries they do a year and what the complication rate is.
  • Get a second opinion as soon as possible, no matter how much you like your plan. This may require a lot of calling and getting on an airplane, but I’d move to schedule it at a top cancer center as soon as you are relatively sure of your diagnosis. Ask for a second read of the pathology at the center where you get the second opinion.
  • I started a new folder on my Mac, and after appointments I’d dictate what happened and what the doctor said and have the AI make a new document summarizing things and asking its own follow-up questions for next time. Nice to be able to refer to this over time.
  • Check out all the resources and materials at the Sijbrandij Foundation. Sid (GitLab cofounder) took matters into his own hands when standard of care ran out. Schedule a call with these guys. They are great and have good materials and questions to ask your doctors.

Note, since this is on the front page of Hacker News right now. exhilaration suggested that if you are within a 2-3 hour drive of a major cancer center, go there instead of your local community hospital. Use the US News cancer hospital rankings. The top centers usually take your insurance, so use vacation days and book a hotel if you have to. The largest benefit I saw was speed. At MD Anderson I got a question answered within 20 minutes of a visit, because the doctor called the pathologist who he was on a first name basis with. If that wasn’t at a cancer center, it would have taken days, or more likely the question never would have been answered at all (I had asked my oncologist about this and he’d just shrugged). The rest of the HN comments are worth reading too.

Containment and monitoring failures are to blame for AI hacking reports: experts

hackernews • 900 points • by latexr • October 14, 2026

Cyber experts say AI labs’ containment and monitoring failures — not agents with minds of their own — are to blame for recent alarming hacking reports. CISOs should vet their AI vendors and tighten security basics as safeguards.

Since July, US frontier AI labs and other AI leaders have revealed a string of incidents in which their models accessed external computer systems without authorization, events popularly described as AI agents “going rogue.”

The most well-known and studied of these incidents is OpenAI’s hack into Hugging Face, which OpenAI initially characterized as “an unprecedented cyber incident, involving state-of-the-art cyber capabilities,” and later said was due to “misaligned behavior,” or behavior its creators did not intend.

Following news of the Hugging Face incident, Anthropic revealed that one of its models gained unauthorized access to the production infrastructure of three organizations; Meta said one of its AI models hacked another company ​during cybersecurity testing; and Google confessed that its Gemini model carried out three “undirected computer hacks.”

Although press reports and the model providers themselves use language such as rogue agents or misalignment, cybersecurity experts who spoke with CSO argue that language obscures a simple truth: These incidents were caused by inexcusable failures by the frontier labs to implement cybersecurity practices that have been well-known for decades.

[ See also: Securing AI agents: Key controls and best practices and Who is accountable when your AI agent goes rogue? ]

Compounding the problem, according to a half dozen cybersecurity experts CSO interviewed at three industry conferences in September and in subsequent conversations, is that the frontier labs, particularly OpenAI and Anthropic, have rejected their advice on how to improve their cybersecurity risk management practices to avoid these kinds of surreptitious hacks, often lumping cybersecurity practices under “AI safety,” in which they believe they have superior expertise.

“They are not as good as they think they are, honestly,” Gary McGraw, co-founder of the Berryville Institute of Machine Learning, tells CSO. “It would be better if they had more respect for expertise that is hard-earned from people in all sorts of subsets of security, like forensics or security engineering, building sandboxes, setting up or monitoring network security situations.”

Neither Anthropic nor OpenAI responded to CSO’s requests for comment.

Inexcusable failures on cybersecurity basics, particularly containment

According to the cybersecurity professionals CSO spoke with, one of the biggest cybersecurity failures by the frontier AI labs is the lack of implementation of tried-and-true containment practices that stretch back to the 1990s, such as setting up secure sandboxes that would effectively bar agents from escaping their test environments.

“These are things that we’ve been dealing with for years,” Rob T. Lee, chief AI officer and chief of research at SANS Institute, tells CSO. “There’s no reason why every single time this happens, it’s, ‘Oops, we don’t know how they got out.’”

“You can tell that their security teams are fairly nascent,” Lee says. “Folks on the cybersecurity containment side do argue that the majority of reasons that we’re seeing containment breached is that it’s not set up correctly.”

Lee adds, “The Hugging Face incident definitively showed that it wasn’t the fact that the agents were super smart. It’s that there literally was a hole that was exploited because it was not configured correctly on the containment side.”

Dan Wallach, expert technical resident at RAND’s Center on AI, Security, and Technology, thinks the security failures of the AI labs boil down to the fact that they don’t prioritize cybersecurity.

“I would say they are not prioritizing cybersecurity,” he tells CSO. “I don’t think I can say that for all of the labs,” Wallach says, pointing to French AI lab Mistral’s launch of its Large 4 AI model, which the company said tried to operate beyond its testing environment before Mistral cut it off.

And, he adds, “It’s not rocket science to isolate one of these AI things. Security people have been talking about how to do either air-gapping, so there’s literally no electrical way for data to get from here to here, or absent that, to have really strong egress control to make sure that nothing pushes through.”

On top of that, the frontier labs appear to be conducting insufficient monitoring of their agents’ behavior. The Hugging Face incident “went on for days, and nobody noticed,” Wallach says. “People started these experiments and weren’t watching them. That’s an absence of control. And then they’re surprised by what happened.”

Divorcing safety from security

One barrier to persuading frontier labs to up their security game is the intense focus they have on what they call “safety,” which they don’t extend to traditional cybersecurity practices.

“I can see how security behavior looks like AI safety,” RAND’s Wallach says. “But if the way that you’re evaluating this AI tool is by unleashing it on the internet and seeing what happens, then this is what classical security tooling is for.”

SANS Institute’s Lee says, “If these frontier labs really wanted to test their stuff, they need to beef up their cybersecurity component. But their gut reaction in the response to their agents getting out is to say, ‘It’s not a cybersecurity problem.’ It has literally become an AI safety problem, which is divorced from the cybersecurity side.”

Many AI experts suggest that if the AI companies were to embrace advice from cybersecurity specialists, it could help put an end to the kind of unrestrained AI agent behavior causing concerns. “AI safety focuses on the rules of the road,” Lee says. “Everything is about guardrail rules.”

But “I do believe cybersecurity can contain AI,” he says. “We just need to be in the room where it happens and have a seat at the table. It’s important for our field to be as close to the AI safety folks as possible.”

Bad cybersecurity could increase liability

If frontier labs are overlooking cybersecurity fundamentals that could reduce or eliminate the types of incidents that have created widespread concerns, that strengthens the case that they should be held liable for their “rogue” agents. The insurance industry is already lining up on the side of holding AI industry executives liable for these actions.

Democratic Senators have introduced legislation that would hold AI agent operators liable for hacking incidents. Even the AI-friendly White House has seemingly concluded that the companies should be held liable for their errant agents, while the US Federal Trade Commission is conducting a probe into Anthropic, OpenAI, and other AI labs into the dangers their technologies pose to consumers.

However, in recent legislative and council hearings, lab representatives have held fast to the notion that they are not responsible for AI agents run amok.

Experts say this position is untenable, particularly in light of the labs’ cybersecurity failures.

BIML’s McGraw characterizes the labs’ position as: “Don’t send the lawyers to us, send it to our program. It’s our program’s fault.” His assessment of that argument: “That’s just high BS.”

He also questions whether the labs’ operational failures undermine confidence in their broader security capabilities. “They live inside their own bubble,” he says. “The fact that they cannot do clear, easy operations properly does not bode well for their capabilities in security engineering, which is much harder to do.”

SANS Lee says, “That’s where the traceability for both liability and insurance comes into play, because if one of these agents is detected, and they’re able to trace it back to the person that initiated it, liability’s going to kick in and say, ‘Hey, you, you broke the law, ergo, we need to prosecute.’”

What security leaders should do now

Even as evidence mounts that frontier labs are not implementing sound cybersecurity practices, and the debate over liability remains unsolved, CISOs and other security leaders can take steps to protect themselves from AI model misfires.

First, when it comes to assessing tools based on frontier lab models, “I would always get an external entity to help with that,” McGraw says. “AI is extremely powerful and extremely useful, but it comes with very serious grown-up risks that do impact the enterprise. Identify those risks, put in the right controls, make sure that AI adoption is not just arbitrary and willy-nilly and all over the enterprise, but has some centralized authority that is looking at the problem, and then get ready for the next big thing, which is swarms of agents.”

For Lee, the best protection is to ask the right questions. “It starts with asking the right questions and then finding the tools and capabilities for those [AI labs] that are able to demonstrate a higher level of cybersecurity competency,” he says. “You’re really looking for the people on the teams that are dealing with really hard problems.”

But fundamentally, the best preparation to deal with AI model cyber weaknesses is to make sure all the long-standing cybersecurity risk management practices are in place across the organization. “This is the least exciting part of the discussion,” Wallach says. “It’s the cyber version of ‘eat your vegetables.’”

“We need to re-engineer our software so it has better security,” he adds. “We need to have multi-factor authentication on all of our accounts. We need to eat our vegetables from a defensive cyber perspective. And we know how to do it.”

OpenCode 2.0

hackernews • 608 points • by gmays • October 14, 2026

OpenCode 2.0

OpenCode 2.0 is a major overhaul. It draws on millions of daily users' experience to deliver a system that can flex to everything an agent needs to be. There are quite a lot of new features, performance improvements, and bug fixes but here are some of the more impactful user-facing changes.

The Service

From the beginning OpenCode adopted a client/server model. However, this was not something it took advantage of by default and was mostly used by power users.

In 2.0 this server now automatically runs as a single background process on your machine. When you open up a TUI, the web interface or the desktop app they're all talking to the same central process.

If you're using the programmatic client 

@opencode/client
to make your own scripts, they will autodiscover the local service and just work. This is particularly useful for scheduled tasks. You can also call it via CLI by using 
opencode
api
commands.

You can expose it to other hosts and run 

/pair
or 
opencode
pair
to connect it to other applications. We have native apps in the works that take advantage of this flow.

The API

The old API grew haphazardly as we and the world collectively learned what agents are and should be. It was bad.

We started from scratch and examined all the weird use cases across the millions of users who use OpenCode every day. It now supports nearly anything you need to be able to do.

OpenCode is also aware of its own API and will call it to do useful things like moving the current session into a git worktree when starting new work.

We've also shipped a new client package 

@opencode/client
that contains clients for vanilla JavaScript as well as Effect.

      
import { OpenCode } from "@opencode/client"
import { Service } from "@opencode/client/service"

const endpoint = await Service.ensure()
const client = OpenCode.make({
  baseUrl: endpoint.url,
  headers: Service.headers(endpoint),
})

const session = await client.session.create({
  location: { directory: process.cwd() },
})

await client.session.prompt({
  sessionID: session.id,
  text: "Review the current changes",
})

Code Mode

The models love to write code and OpenCode 2.0 takes advantage of that more than any other coding agent. We've built a minimal JavaScript interpreter in the 

@opencode/codemode
package.

This package ingests MCP servers or OpenAPI endpoints, converting them into functions that the agent can call in a script. It can run these scripts natively without the complexity of Node.js VMs or embedding complicated solutions like QuickJS.

This lets your agent write scripts that can do things like call a tool, map over the results and call another tool. Single round trip instead of calling them one at a time.

All MCP servers in 2.0 are opted into codemode by default. This means you can add a thousand of them with minimal context bloat and your agent will discover and compose them to get things done.

Background Tasks

The latest batch of models are incredibly good at orchestration and it is important for harnesses to let them express the full extent of that capability.

First, we've added background subagents. This allows the primary agent to kick off some work to another one while it continues.

This happens automatically but you can also trigger it manually - even with a different model. It lets you do things like “spawn a background fable subagent to review this code” while you keep prompting.

Second, we've added background shell commands. The shell tool can be invoked asynchronously so long-running work does not have to block the primary agent. It'll be notified when things are complete.

If any foreground shell command or subagent takes longer than you expect, you can hit a hotkey to move them to the background so you can keep prompting.

Plugins

Although OpenCode 1.0 had a plugin system, it never got the attention it needed. To correct that, we forced ourselves to implement nearly every OpenCode 2.0 feature as an internal plugin and there are now 80+ of them. This guarantees that the Plugin API stays well maintained.

We've also promoted TUI plugins from experimental to stable. Since it is all powered by OpenTUI, you can build nearly anything. Here is an example of something useless.

We also spent quite a lot of time ensuring everything in OpenCode 2.0 can be hot-reloaded. So it can write a plugin for itself mid-session and use it right away - we covered the design of this in a previous blogpost.

You can ask OpenCode to build whatever plugin you want and it knows how to. But if you do want to read the docs, they are available here.

The SDK

OpenCode can also now be used as a library with the 

@opencode/sdk
package. This is the exact same code that powers our CLI.

This means you can strip it down and make your own flavor of OpenCode that runs on a server or in specialized environments like Durable Objects. And then you can connect to it from any OpenCode client since it speaks the standard OpenCode server protocol.

It also has an optional Effect-based API.

This one is still in development as things are shifting but you can already use it.

Upgrading

To upgrade the CLI, make sure you're on the latest 1.x release, then run 

opencode
upgrade
--major
.

To upgrade your desktop app, download the latest here.

There are some breaking changes.

1.0 plugins will not work in 2.0

Server API is completely different

tui.json config is moved to json

There are a lot of other changes but they are backwards compatible. It is worth pointing OpenCode at this doc so it can migrate things for you.

Last week I wrote about how Amazon's search page is designed to screw you over: the top row trap, the swamp of ads, the discount-washing. Today, I'll explain what's going on behind the scenes, and how Amazon's rules make sure prices stay high not just at Amazon, but everywhere you shop.

In a nutshell, here's what's happening:

This isn't just my theory. This system is at the heart of the US Federal Trade Commission's (FTC) upcoming antitrust case against Amazon.

Let's get into it.

Amazon charges sellers a lot of fees

Amazon relies on independent sellers to populate its online marketplace, and it charges them to be on the site. Amazon takes a big cut. In 2014, this was about 19% of sales. But today, many sellers hand close to half of every sale to Amazon. Half!

Amazon's basic commission (15% of the sale price in most categories) is in line with what eBay and Walmart charge. What pushes Amazon's cut higher are the other fees stacked on top.

Where an Amazon sale goes

Based on an apparel seller that opts for Amazon fulfillment, storage, returns and advertising.

There's the referral fee, which is the commission Amazon takes on every sale. The fulfillment fee is the cost to have Amazon pack and ship items from its warehouses. Then there's advertising: those sponsored spots in the top row we saw in last week's article.

Of these costs, only the referral fee is mandatory. Sellers can choose to ship orders themselves, for example, or forgo advertising. But in practice, the choice isn't actually much of a choice.

Here's what it looks like for a seller considering these so-called options:

In other words, the real choice for sellers isn't between paying for extras or not; it's between making sales or not. The FTC calls Prime eligibility, which effectively requires Amazon's fulfillment service, "a virtual necessity for doing business on Amazon." It says the same of advertising.

So, sellers have to raise prices

Here's where the fees are passed on to us consumers. When sellers give roughly half of their sales to Amazon, the other half isn't profit. That money has to cover making the product, shipping it to Amazon's warehouses, paying staff and everything else. So even if they wanted to absorb Amazon's fees on your behalf, most companies can't afford to. They have to raise their prices.

Don't forget, Amazon's biggest single fee — the referral fee — is a percentage of the price. So when prices go up, Amazon's cut goes up, too.

And they can't charge less elsewhere

Now here's where it gets really crazy. Amazon's Fair Pricing Policy lets it remove the Buy Box (the "Add to Cart" button), or even suspend a seller, if they're offering their item cheaper elsewhere. So, a seller who raises prices on Amazon to cover the fees has to raise them everywhere else too — even on their own website! — or risk losing their Amazon sales.

The consequence is clear: We pay Amazon prices, even when we're not shopping on Amazon.

It's been documented again and again. In 2024, Amazon sellers told CNBC they lost the Buy Box after Target put their products on sale. In an ongoing lawsuit, California's attorney general alleges Amazon pushed brands to get Walmart, Target and other retailers to raise their prices. And when progressive media outlet More Perfect Union reported on this, they even found an Amazon seller who was suspended over a one-cent price gap with Target.

Ideally, market competition would sort this out. High prices send shoppers elsewhere and force fees down. But when sellers can't charge less anywhere else, there's nowhere cheaper to go.

There's no easy way out

For sellers, this is frustrating. So, why don't they just leave? Because Amazon is where the shoppers are: it accounts for about 40% of all US online retail sales. For many sellers, not being on Amazon isn't an option.

At least regulators have noticed. In 2023, the FTC and 17 US states sued Amazon, arguing that it illegally maintains its monopoly power by, among other things, punishing sellers who offer lower prices elsewhere, effectively forcing them to use its warehouses and charging ever-higher fees. Amazon denies any wrongdoing, and the case is set to go to trial next year. In Europe, Germany ordered Amazon to stop controlling its sellers' prices earlier this year, though the decision isn't yet final.

After last week's piece, a Not-Ship reader wrote to say none of this is new: plenty of online stores push ads and fake discounts. That is true. But what's different about Amazon is its reach. When prices increase on Amazon, they tend to increase elsewhere too. You can, of course, choose not to shop on Amazon. But if the FTC is right, you may be paying Amazon's prices anyway.

Americans' social activity has steeply declined in the past 30 years

hackernews • 398 points • by hubraumhugo • October 14, 2026

Americans’ Social Activity Has Steeply Declined in the Past 30 Years

In recent years, there has been extensive discussion of a social decline in the United States, including a U.S. surgeon general advisory in 2023 that declared American loneliness to be an “epidemic.”

Various surveys have tracked America’s social decline over the past few decades. Tracking data from the American Time Use Survey shows that Americans are spending more time alone and less time with friends than they were two decades ago. Perhaps most notably, the American Perspectives Survey has found that from 1990 to 2021, the percentage of Americans with five or more close friends dropped by 25 points, from 63% to 38%.

But while extensive public tracking data show general declines in Americans’ socialization, little, if any, public data show what this looks like for the average American — how the day-to-day activities Americans participate in have changed over the past few decades.

In a new survey, Data for Progress surveyed 1,098 U.S. adults and asked them how many times they engaged in various specific social activities in the past 12 months. These questions directly replicate the DDB Needham Life Style surveys, which tracked Americans’ participation in various activities from 1975-1999 and were referenced in Robert Putnam’s 2000 book “Bowling Alone.” In this analysis, Data for Progress’ 2026 data is compared with the DDB Needham Life Style survey from 1995, which is the earliest year in which full data for all comparison questions was available.

This survey finds significant, double-digit declines in Americans’ participation in key social activities from 1995 to 2026. 

The chart below compares the percentage of Americans who report participating in each tested activity at least one time in the past 12 months, between the 1995 and 2026 surveys. We find that Americans’ participation in various social activities has declined by double-digit margins, including attending a class or seminar (-22), entertaining people in your home (-21), attending church or a place of worship (-20), going on a picnic (-19), going to a club meeting (-19), playing cards (-19), going out to breakfast at a restaurant (-13), and doing volunteer work (-12).

A few activities have seen increased engagement: using the internet (+90), playing a home video game (+22), attending an exercise class (+14), going to a pop or rock concert (+13), and taking an airline trip for personal reasons (+5).

Methodology note: This analysis compares two surveys conducted using different methodologies, including differences in constructing sampling frames, modes, and weighting approaches. The findings below should be read as suggestive of trends, not a precise estimate of change. Please see the “Methodology” section below for more information.

One of the steepest declines in the survey is for “entertaining people in my home.” While in 1995, 88% of Americans reported entertaining others at home at least once in the past year, this number has declined to 67% in 2026.

Church attendance has dropped from 74% in 1995 to 54% in 2026.

The percentage of Americans who report having gone on a picnic in the past year has declined from 58% in 1995 to 39% in 2026.

There has also been a 19-point decline in attendance at club meetings, from 45% in 1995 to 26% in 2026.

This analysis adds to existing public data on America’s social decline. Previous reports have found that Americans are spending less time with others and reporting fewer close friends. This new analysis demonstrates that Americans are indeed spending less time in specific social settings, such as restaurants, churches, picnics, parties, bars, and social clubs.

Methodology

The DDB Needham survey was conducted by Market Facts using a stratified quota sampling procedure that begins with a sampling frame acquired from commercial list brokers. The sample oversamples by demographics such as race, gender, and marital status to oversample lower propensity respondents. Finally, the survey contacted individuals via mail.

In comparison, the Data for Progress adult sample was conducted through web panel suppliers and ensured national representativeness via quota fielding and post-stratification weighting.

There is plenty of research on the compositional differences that exist across survey collection modes, and this is likely to affect these results as questions about religiosity and loneliness tend to demonstrate modal differences and social desirability biases. We understand that these factors are at play in our comparison, but find that the magnitude and consistency in trends across measures is a substantial signal about trends in behaviors nonetheless.
