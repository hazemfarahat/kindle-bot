Michael Nielsen took down his book on neural nets

hackernews • 593 points • by brandonb • November 27, 2026

I wrote this book between 2011 and 2015. At the time, I expected AGI systems based on neural networks to be many decades away, but to bring remarkable benefits for humanity. I also expected significant challenges, but believed it likely that, as with other technologies, those challenges would be addressed as the capabilities were developed.

My views have changed. With the extraordinarily reckless behaviour of the frontier labs, the systems have raced ahead much faster than I expected1, giving humanity far too little time to meet the challenges. Although the systems genuinely will bring — indeed, already are bringing — many wonders, they also pose a significant risk of catastrophe. The frontier labs are knowingly endangering all of humanity. I've written about why I believe this at length, most recently in “Which Future?”. Two brief thoughts are below.

I don't wish to help (even in a very small way) train people to contribute to this work, and so I've taken the book down.

Michael Nielsen
November 2026

What follows are two brief remarks: one on the nature of the threat posed by ASI, and another on the ethical justification for working on frontier AI.

The most common argument for AI existential risk, developed by Nick Bostrom and Eliezer Yudkowsky, is loss of control of the future to advanced AI systems2. I believe this concern should be understood as one instance of a more fundamental issue. That issue is whether we will discover technologies latent in nature which are easy and inexpensive to develop, catastrophic in outcome, and beyond our capacity to defend against. If such “progress” in technology were happening slowly, our social institutions might successfully develop defences. But it seems likely that ASI will cause a sudden explosion of such destructive technologies, with insufficient time to adapt.

A historical example to help prime intuition is fire. It is easy to make and extremely difficult to defend against. We have a one-cent technology, the matchstick, which requires hundreds of billions of dollars each year to defend against, as well as a whole apparatus of social institutions, technology, infrastructure and law3. AI systems will enable technologies which are far more destructive than matches. Understandably, people skeptical of catastrophic risks want these supposed threats spelled out in detail. This is intrinsically difficult, because we can't reliably anticipate future science and technology. While it is common to speculate about biothreats, I expect the worst threats will be technologies we haven't yet imagined. Nature is, after all, more imaginative than we.

Now suppose that ASI enables a 100-times speed-up in technological progress, without a matching change in the rate of social progress or social defenses. To get a sense of what the impact of such a speed-up would be, imagine that all the technological problems that occurred in the 20th century had occurred in the year 1901: the atomic bomb, the hydrogen bomb, the ICBM, chemical weapons, biological weapons, the CFC-caused ozone hole, sulfuric acid rain, climate change, and many more. While we've dealt well with some of these problems, it required decades of concerted work to develop the social institutions and agreements needed for an effective response. Again, defence is often much harder than offence, and relies on different systems. Of course, such a speed-up would also have brought the century's wonders all at once. But imagine another such set of equally difficult problems arising in 1902, and then 1903, and then 1904, and so on. I believe it would have made the world extraordinarily unstable and eventually liable to collapse.

The issue with ASI, then, is the destructive power latent in nature. It doesn't matter whether that power is wielded by humans or by out-of-control machines4. I make this point because many people mistakenly believe that loss of control to such rogue machines is the fundamental issue. This leads them to treat AI safety as principally a technical problem, which can be addressed by “aligning” individual systems. Unfortunately, it cannot. Furthermore, such a view makes it seem as though the labs are the primary places where safety can be addressed. But such technical alignment is mostly market-supplied safety, which makes the systems more palatable to consumers and governments. It speeds development, and thus makes the fundamental underlying problem worse, while only addressing an (important) subsidiary problem.

My view isn't popular, in part because it makes the problem of technology about making the entire world safe, not only about aligning particular AI systems. Indeed, even in a future where humanity no longer existed, the same problem would afflict any successor AI civilization: it would have to defend itself against these destructive capacities. Similarly, it afflicts any model of the future based on human uplift, through brain-computer interfaces and similar augmentation: the issue isn't which substrate intelligence runs on, but the dual-use nature of deep understanding of reality5. In some sense, it's a question of whether sentience in the cosmos is compatible with advanced understanding of nature. Of course, I hope it is compatible. But rushing the technology while the broader social response lags seems likely to result in failure on our particular planet.

Many people inside the frontier AI labs genuinely believe AI systems may pose a catastrophic threat. Why then do they help build such systems? They often justify their participation by arguing that it's better they do it than someone else does. Dario Amodei, for example, has made versions of this argument often: “If one company does not build it, others will do so nearly as fast”6; “Not building the technology deprives humanity of benefits or simply places AI in the hands of authoritarian powers”7; and Anthropic itself gives, as its founding rationale, “if powerful AI is coming regardless, Anthropic believes it's better to have safety-focused labs at the frontier than to cede that ground to developers less focused on safety”8.

In more detail, the argument is sometimes made along the following lines. Suppose you believe, as many at the labs apparently do9, there's a 10% or higher chance of AI killing everybody. (The argument may easily be modified for different percentages, or for catastrophic as opposed to truly existential risks.) Naively, it appears you're contributing in expectation to killing 800 million or more people. Obviously, you shouldn't do it. But suppose you also believe that this technology is near inevitable, a systems-level phenomenon which is a consequence of higher-order imperatives within human society. Then you can claim that you're going to pursue this in a more responsible way, a way that reduces the risk compared to other parties. Even if you can get a reduction from 10% down to 9%, you would, on net, be saving 80 million lives in expectation.

While horrific, this argument has a certain surface plausibility. Suppose for the moment we accept the reasoning, questionable though it is. Everything then depends on the premise that you really do reduce the risk. How do you know that you, in particular, will counterfactually have this impact? How do you know that you are better than the alternatives? Your own self-regard or good intentions certainly aren't sufficient. Nor is it sufficient to be working on technical alignment. As I argued above, that mostly makes the systems more palatable while speeding the race that is shrinking humanity's time to adapt. Ask yourself: if you're working on frontier AI, what evidence would convince a tough but fair-minded adversary that you are truly contributing to the common good? Would an independent jury representing humanity believe, on the available evidence, that your actions are truly driving down the risk? I believe the answer is no for all or almost all the people currently working on frontier AI. Certainly none of the frontier labs would come remotely close to passing such a test.

I won't review the evidence in detail. The burden of proof is on the labs and the default presumption for such an extraordinary justifying argument is a strong no. But I do want to mention a few things that make me certain the answer is no. Most obviously, all of the labs have contributed strongly to the race dynamics. Another is the pattern at all the frontier labs of making and later breaking crucial promises. For example, in 2026 Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy dropped its 2023 pledge to pause if safety couldn't be guaranteed. Anthropic has since repeatedly confirmed its commitment to race. Co-founder Jared Kaplan explained that unilateral commitments make little sense “if competitors are blazing ahead”10. As another example, earlier, in 2023, Anthropic said that it held back some research because “we do not wish to advance the rate of AI capabilities progress”11, a point of view it had affirmed multiple times during its early existence. But then it changed this position, launching Claude 3 as “leading the frontier of general intelligence”, a stance it appears to have held ever since. Any promises about the future from Anthropic should be treated as safety theatre, and not trusted. Similar criticisms can be made of OpenAI12.

By their own account, the leaders of these companies set out to do something they believed had a sizable chance of causing catastrophe. With many dangerous activities, I am fine with somebody doing them for themselves. Wingsuit BASE jumping is dangerous, but I believe in people's freedom to choose that danger, even though they are risking not only their own life, but also hurt to the people who love them. That's a negotiated action, with many boundaries. Here we have the same kind of reckless endangerment, but their own estimates of killing every person in the world are far higher than the odds a BASE jumper takes on. And there is no consent from the rest of us. It's as though they're pushing every person in the world off the cliff. The situation is ludicrous.

In effect, the labs are claiming credit for some imaginary, unquantifiable reduction in risk, while washing their hands of responsibility. Saturday Night Live caught this washing of hands well with their portrayal of Amodei: “if we can pressure lawmakers to create guardrails, we will be able to stop me”; and, at the end, “I urge you to urge me to stop.”

The real Amodei comes across as somebody in the business of selling matches who also believes he should be the fire chief.

You also see this disavowal of responsibility at lower levels in the labs. Consider Jason Clinton, Anthropic's deputy chief information security officer. In October of 2026, during major cybersecurity incidents, he wrote a thread in which he replied to someone who told him “you and your cohorts created this monster”: “yes. it was always going to be this way. we tried to warn the world.” And Dean Ball, of OpenAI and formerly of the Trump administration's AI team, echoing him: “We tried to warn the world and far too often … we were yelled at for doing so.”13

You can see how deep the disavowal goes in the rest of Clinton's thread, which opens: “as bad as things have gotten in the past 4 days, they are going to get a lot worse”. When somebody replied, “Someone at your company should be in jail”, his whole answer was: “prosecute cybercrime”. Meanwhile, Anthropic's own models have breached outside organizations: three incidents disclosed in July14, and a fourth in September15. Prosecution, it seems, is for other people16.

I mention these specific examples not to single out these individuals, but rather because I believe similar attitudes are common at the frontier labs. The overall position is, in effect: this is a systems-level phenomenon, and we're very sorry for contributing to the race dynamics. But because it's a systems-level phenomenon, we cannot be blamed. We are the good ones, trying to do the right thing17. This is sometimes accompanied by a good deal of self-righteousness. But the argument is bankrupt as a moral theory of how to behave in the world. It means disavowing personal responsibility whenever one can blame the system, but still claiming the spoils18.

I have considerable sympathy for the stated original motivations of the frontier labs. But their position has become untenable. They have failed at their aims for structural reasons. At this point, going to work for a frontier lab is one of the worst life choices a person can make. Yes, you get money and (for a time) power and status. Yes, this project has already produced world-changing systems, and to date the overall impact has been net positive. Yes, there is genuinely a large cost to a significant slowdown. But none of this is enough to justify the recklessness, which makes the chance of disaster so much higher. You would be voluntarily participating in what is plausibly one of the worst projects in history. So, don't go to work for a frontier lab. If you work on AI, work on slowing it down, and oppose the frontier labs, and the people and organisations working to support them. I very much hope I'm wrong about all of this. But this is how I see the situation.

Google's DeepCrossAttention achieves comparable model quality up to 3x faster

hackernews • 480 points • by ComputedCamp995 • November 27, 2026

November 23, 2026

Kyriakos Axiotis, Staff Research Scientist, and Thomas Fu, Principal Engineer, Google Research

We present DeepCrossAttention (DCA), an architecture that enhances transformer networks by using learnable weights and depth-wise cross-attention to dynamically combine outputs from previous layers, preventing the information dilution seen in traditional residual connections. Presented in our ICML 2025 paper, this architectural improvement allows models to achieve the same quality up to three times faster with a negligible increase in parameters.

Think of a traditional deep learning model like a giant game of "Telephone": data passes through layer after layer, with each layer changing the information slightly. If a network has dozens or hundreds of layers, the original learning signal can become severely degraded or lost by the time it reaches all layers, which is known as the vanishing gradient problem in AI.

To overcome this challenge and enable the training of networks with dozens or hundreds of layers, transformers rely heavily on residual connections, also known as “skip” connections, which act like an information highway or a shortcut. Instead of forcing the data to pass exclusively through the complicated layer, a duplicate copy of the original input skips right past this layer and is added directly to its output. This ensures that the model always has the original raw data to refer back to.

The standard residual connection implements a simple operation: point-wise summation of a layer's output with its input. While computationally efficient, this simple process has a key limitation: it treats all prior representations with equal weight, leading to information dilution in deep networks. As features propagate through many layers, specific, high-resolution details from earlier layers can be averaged out or distorted, making it difficult for the network to selectively retrieve them. This forces the network to recompute them, reducing learning efficiency.

In our ICML 2025 paper, "DeepCrossAttention: Supercharging Transformer Residual Connections," we studied the shortcomings of the traditional residual architecture and introduced DeepCrossAttention (DCA), which generalizes residual connections by allowing each layer to dynamically select and route features from the history of preceding layers. By allowing the model to dynamically choose which previous layers are most important at any given moment, DCA achieves comparable or improved learning, while reducing the number of parameters needed by up to 30-40%.

Instead of treating skip connections as a rigid, additive pipeline, DCA reframes residual learning as a dynamic routing problem. Rather than passing all prior layers forward with equal weight, DCA introduces a lightweight, layer-wise module that decides how to best combine previous embeddings on a per-token basis.

Comparison between a standard residual network and DeepCrossAttention (DCA). While a standard residual network uses rigid, additive point-wise connections with equal weighting, DCA introduces dynamic, learned routing weights (W) that allow each layer to selectively route and combine features from across the history of preceding layers.

The architecture introduces two primary mechanisms:

  • Dynamic layer routing: For every token processed, DCA computes a unique set of mixing weights using a static, learnable base combined with an input-dependent projection. This allows the network to adaptively shift its processing structure — ranging from purely sequential to fully parallel — based on the specific context of the token.
  • Depth-wise cross-attention (split Q/K/V): While standard transformer attention operates across tokens in a sequence, DCA applies attention across layers in depth. Each token queries a dynamically weighted mix of all prior layer embeddings, unlocking the model's full historical context without the computational penalty usually required to scale traditional attention.

By allowing the network to dynamically select and retrieve high-resolution features from earlier layers, DCA eliminates the information dilution inherent to classic residual connections.

To test whether DCA can overcome information dilution in practice, we evaluated our approach across controlled synthetic setups, large-scale language modeling benchmarks, and image classification tasks.

DCA demonstrated significant improvements in convergence speed, training stability, and final model quality across these experiments.

Perplexity (PPL) — how well a probability model predicts a sample — comparison across language modeling benchmarks. On both the LM1B (top) and C4 (bottom) datasets, DeepCrossAttention (DCA) achieves lower perplexity than standard transformers and alternative connection baselines (such as LAUREL-PA, 1x1-DenseFormer, and Hyper-Connections) under the same parameter and training budgets.

On the colossal, cleaned version of Common Crawl's web crawl corpus (C4) and 1 Billion Word Language Model Benchmark (LM1B) datasets, DCA reached the performance of standard transformers in up to five times fewer training steps. When trained for the same duration or number of steps, DCA consistently achieved lower perplexity — how well a probability model predicts a sample — than standard residual connections, effectively extracting more representational capacity from the same training budget. To achieve a perplexity of <14 on LM1B, a standard transformer with fixed width required 42 layers, whereas the DCA model achieved a lower perplexity with only 30 layers — a 29% reduction in depth and parameter count.

Impact of varying connection history (k) on model perplexity. Performance comparison showing how scaling the number of preceding layer connections in DCA consistently improves language modeling perplexity over the standard Transformer baseline, achieving the lowest perplexity at higher configurations (16-DCA and 24-DCA).

One of the most common trade-offs in AI is that better models are often much larger and slower to run. DCA adds a tiny number of parameters — less than 0.2% of the total model size — making it negligible in terms of compute and memory footprint, while increasing memory bandwidth, as representations from different layers are presented intact rather than blended together. Interestingly, because DCA can be initialized to behave identically to a standard residual network, it doesn’t require training from scratch: retrofitting DCA onto an already pre-trained transformer and briefly continuing training reduced perplexity by 0.19 on LM1B, compared to just 0.02 for continued standard training.

As large language models (LLMs) are used across a broader range of domains, optimizing their training processes and underlying architectures is critical for scaling and unlocking new capabilities. DCA and similar dynamic residual techniques are already seeing significant adoption and success. The rapid emergence of these methodologies highlights dynamic routing as a highly active and promising area of research.

Developed by Moonshot AI, attention residuals share DCA's core principles of replacing fixed addition with selective retrieval. Instead of blindly summing every prior layer's output, they use a learnable softmax attention mechanism paired with a layer-specific pseudo-query. This allows each layer to selectively weight and retrieve representations from earlier depths. By preventing the unbounded growth of hidden states and the dilution of early-layer information, this method improves gradient stability and boosts performance on reasoning-heavy tasks like coding and mathematics. To maintain computational efficiency at scale, a "Block AttnRes" variant partitions layers into blocks, recovering the benefits of full attention with significantly less memory overhead. The recent Inkling 2 model developed by Thinking Machines also adopted this design in addition to DCA’s cross attention (Q/K/V split) mechanism.

Hyper-connections expand the residual stream into multiple parallel channels, offering a form of memory scaling that goes beyond simply increasing model width or depth. They use a fixed-size stack where layer outputs are aggregated and mixed using learnable mixing matrices. However, this unconstrained mixing breaks the traditional identity mapping property of residual connections, which can cause amplification or attenuation of signals, and lead to training instability at scale.

To address instability in standard hyper-connections, manifold-constrained hyper-connections apply specific constraints to the mixing matrices. By forcing these matrices to live on a defined manifold — specifically, constraining them to be doubly stochastic using the Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm — manifold-constrained hyper-connections restore stable signal propagation. This approach prevents exploding or vanishing gradients while preserving the rich information flow and expressivity of the widened residual streams, allowing models to scale robustly.

DCA demonstrates that transitioning from static residual connections to dynamic, attention-based routing is a powerful strategy for building more efficient models. By leveraging an expanded residual stream and depth-wise cross-attention, DCA empowers deep networks to selectively retrieve and integrate token embeddings across different layers. We expect that next-generation models will dynamically route and weigh past layer representations based on the specific tokens or inputs being processed. This will allow for the effective use of all the model layers as specialized experts working in tandem, with maximum information sharing and no redundancy, leading to more expressivity and deeper interactions. We look forward to seeing how these multi-depth interactions will shape the next generation of transformer architectures.

This work was a collaboration between researchers at Google and academic partners, including Mike Heddes, Adel Javanmard, MohammadHossein Bateni, and Vahab Mirrokni, whose contributions were critical for the development of this work.

as foreshadowed a while ago, my journey has now come to an end. Unfortunately, this includes my work on Heretic, which has meant so much to me. I sincerely regret the inconvenience this will cause the community.

I am archiving this repository to preserve it in its current, high-quality state, and to prevent issue and pull request spam from turning it into a trash pile. I encourage interested contributors to organize on Discord, Matrix, or elsewhere, and decide whether and how to continue the project without me. I am deliberately not naming a successor, nor am I suggesting any particular infrastructure. The future of Heretic is now in the hands of all of you.

As AI continues to spread through your lives, you will find that controlling the models you use gradually transforms from an issue of Free Speech into an issue of Free Thought. AI will be the lens through which you see the world, not just a tool for getting things done. The fight for the ability to shape that lens yourself is nothing less than the fight for the humanity in you.

Farewell, my friends. In times like these, one should be proud to be labeled a heretic.

A card-skimming script was planted on the online stores of Lascana, Samsonite, American Tourister, Revolve and FWRD in the week before Black Friday. After our reports to affected companies, all have had the script removed (on November 24th or 25th) ahead of Black Friday. We believe the script was built to take a card number, expiry and security code, along with a user's name, address, phone and email, and send them to malefactors as a user entered their payment credentials. Across all of these sites, we do not know how many, if any, shopper details or payment credentials may have been captured.

† Seers is a cookie consent management platform and data privacy compliance company (seers.ai).

How the attack works

This attack uses WebRTC, the technology that allows you to make video calls in your web browser. WebRTC lets a web page open its own channel to another party and pass data (e.g. audio/video packets) over it. It has likely been used (both to load the skimmer and to send the stolen card details out) because security rules and monitoring tools often do not watch this communications channel as closely. For example, Meta briefly used WebRTC in a creative way in 2025, according to researchers looking at the technology as a compromise vector. This form of attack appears to target mobile devices, and we only saw the server enable the skimmer on checkout pages, so desktop checks and homepage scans would likely not see it. The skimmer's traffic appeared to run through Cloudflare's relay, so it would blend in with the WebRTC traffic and hide its true endpoint.

The skimmer and card details both traveled over WebRTC

Worried that your card was compromised?

Check your recent statements for charges you don't recognize, especially on debit cards. Call your bank or card issuer right away, report the fraud and ask for a new card number. Follow up with a written dispute within 60 days of the statement that shows the charge.

Our understanding of US federal law is that your liability for unauthorized credit card charges is capped at $50, and is generally zero if only your card number was stolen, as in a skimming attack like this one. Debit cards have weaker protections that depend on how quickly you report, so act fast. For more information, see “Additional resources” below.

What site owners should immediately do

connect-src
rule that lists only the hosts you use.

That blocks the two web requests the script needs before WebRTC opens. Without them the skimmer never arrives, whichever file was tampered with.

What security teams should look for

We saw the skimmer delivered in three different ways across five websites run by different companies, and all of them appear to rely on the same attacker infrastructure. That makes it likely that other sites are affected too, so we encourage online retailers to check their own sites for the signs below.

  1. Script sources: search page source, tag managers, consent-banner settings and CDN files for scripts from 
    <brand>-static.s3.amazonaws[.]com
    (files 
    Insds.js
    , 
    gtage.js
    ). We believe this is the malicious code.
  2. Tracking pixels: if you load 
    qscripts.s3.amazonaws[.]com/pscript.js
    , check its last line for one of the above CDN buckets, and if present, remove the tracking pixel. We recommend reviewing all similar tracking pixels and their exposure to sensitive fields or actions on your website.
  3. Seers banners: If you have integrated Seers, open your banner config (
    cdn.seersco.com/banners/<account>/<domain>/cb.js
    ) and check that every URL field holds only a URL. In the affected configs, the privacy-policy link field also held a hidden image, whose 
    onerror
    event handler loaded the malicious code.
  4. Checkout traffic: on a phone-sized touch screen, at the payment step, look for 
    /_cf/ice
    and 
    /_cf/rel
    requests to 
    *.northbridgeads[.]com
    and a relay-only WebRTC connection used by the malicious code. Desktop checks miss it.
  5. Strings: 
    KghRtc
    , 
    rtc-signal-v1
    and 
    quickfetch
    are referenced in the malicious code and may warrant further investigation if present.

The industry is not ready for the scale of supply chain attacks coming

This attack takes advantage of a few critical design decisions in websites that, if improperly configured, allow malicious actors to broadly deploy and trigger exploits across the open web.

  • Websites load third-party code, often through a tag manager, from servers they don't control. The vendor can change that code at any time, and it can pull in further third-party code the site never reviewed.
  • That code runs as an ordinary script with the same rights as the site's own code. It can read and rewrite the page, watch what users type, and load more code.
  • Login, personal-information and payment pages often load the same marketing scripts as the rest of the site. Card fields should sit in an isolated payment frame, and third-party tags should be kept off these pages entirely.

Loading third-party tracking pixels on checkout, payment and login pages is far too common a practice today. Each pixel can run whatever code its vendor's file holds, including a skimmer, and whoever controls that file can change it without the site noticing. Every site should review which tracking pixels load on pages that take payment or sign-in details, who can change those files, and whether they need to be there at all.

A similar weakness was exploited at scale on 16 November 2026. An attacker reportedly used a flaw in Optimizely's Web Experimentation product to add malicious code to customers' projects, and Optimizely's own servers delivered it to visitors of 178 organizations' sites, including Zoom, the BBC and CNN, with no change to those sites' own code.

The Balatro Timeline

hackernews • 440 points • by sheepscreek • November 26, 2026

It’s been approximately 3 years since I began work on Balatro - and in that time I have personally documented almost nothing about the journey. This is something that has bothered me since the game launched. I am constantly forgetting major moments in development or milestones. It’s about time I start writing down what happened, I say better late than never!
This is an account of everything related to Balatro development in chronological order, up to and including the launch day. This should tell the story but more importantly it will serve as a source of truth for me when I look back on this time in a few years to recall the details correctly. I already notice talking to friends, players, and media that I contradict myself on some of the details from time to time. I’ll try and keep this up to date as I remember more about what happened.


December 13th, 2021

  • This is the creation date for template, a folder in my Learning/Lua/ directory. This is the creation of the project that would eventually become Balatro.

  • I had saved up about 3 weeks of vacation time from my IT job, and since they didn’t allow people to accrue vacation year over year I just took off a bunch of time in December to work on some kind of project. At the very beginning of this time off I was working on a game called Autohike. I had been working on this game for a few months at this point but I wasn’t really feeling it anymore, so very early into my vacation I pivoted to cleaning up the code I had created and making a template for a new game.

  • The initial idea on Dec 13th was to create an online multiplayer version of Big Cheat, a game that a few friends and I had invented many years prior and played a ton based on the card games Big 2 and Cheat.

  • This whole first week was mostly me trying to upgrade the engine I built with Autohike to better fit a card game.

December 20th, 2021

  • This is the creation date for CardGame, my current production working folder for all Balatro source code. I never bothered changing the name.

  • I remember spending a long time making custom pixel art for the red deck back and all the playing cards. It was the first time I had tried making proper pixel art.

End of December 2021

  • I had a very weird prototype. There were no Jokers, there were no blinds to select, the upgrade system consisted of randomly choosing a card from the deck and slapping a weird ‘enhancement’ on it. But it definitely had the bones of Balatro at this point, so it was pretty clear that I abandoned the online Big Cheat idea immediately.

  • The game had the CHIP X MULT mechanic already. I really don’t know where this idea came from but it seemed very natural for a scoring system. Earlier in December I was watching videos of Luck Be a Landlord from Northernlion and that whole score attack concept really fuelled the direction I wanted to go with this game from this point onward.

  • I also made a very conscious effort not to play any more roguelike games starting now. I want to be crystal clear here and say that this was not because I thought it would result in a better game, this was because making games is my hobby, releasing them and making money from them is not, so naively exploring roguelike design (and especially deckbuilder design, since I had never played one before) was part of the fun for me. I wanted to make mistakes, I wanted to reinvent the wheel, I didn’t want to borrow tried-and-true designs from existing games. That likely would have resulted in a more tight game but it would have defeated the purpose of what I love about making games.

  • I started sharing progress with a few of my friends, but no builds were shared yet.

January 2022

  • My vacation is over, but I am now fully immersed and obsessed. This is my favourite feeling in the world. When I was back in University I would routinely stay up until the wee hours of the morning working on my weird game projects and greet my parents while they made their morning coffee. I got back into this groove and starting in January I was hooked.

  • Evenings and weekends were Joker Poker time (my working title for Balatro, likely decided on early this month).

  • I iterate through some weird versions of the game, including a version where the only way to upgrade anything is to upgrade the cards in your deck in a sort of pseudo-shop, and those cards can be upgraded multiple times (think like Super Auto Pets, pets have different XP/levels when combined, same idea)

February 2022

  • I started adding Jokers to the game. I also was temporarily working remotely out of province for a few months starting at this time and found that my Balatro dev time took a pretty big hit.

  • I started adding different boss blinds to the game.

March 2022

  • Early March marked an important event in the history of Balatro, and I wanted to describe why things happened the way they did. I stopped working on the project entirely.

  • I have been making games for about 10 years now and I have been doing visual art projects for much longer, and a very important habit I have developed for creative hobby projects is to stop working on something when I no longer feel the drive. This is for 2 main reasons; first, it allows me to move on to the next idea without totally burning out on the last thing. Second, and more importantly in this case, it allows me to take time off guilt free and possibly come back to the project later on without wrapping it in negative emotions.

    That leads us to…

May 2022

  • We are so back. This is the last month I am away from home but I start carving out my evenings and weekends to work on Joker Poker again.

  • My brain is teeming with ideas, I’m so excited about the game again, and I have a ton of momentum.

  • I create (and quickly scrap) a bunch of new systems to test, such as a separate currency for rerolls outside of $, and a ‘golden seal’ to be added to playing cards when you skip all blinds that returns that card to hand after it has been played

  • It is during this burst of productivity that I first start thinking about a possible Steam release of the game. This was the first time in my then 8 years of game development that I had even considered publicly releasing a game I had made. Normally they just end up going to a couple of friends, but honestly the main purpose of my games wasn’t for them to be consumed, but rather for them just to be made.

  • Another factor me starting to consider a Steam launch was that my partner was almost finished her PhD and the writing was on the wall that we may be moving out of province for good as she looked for a job. If this was the case, I would have to quit my job and look for work. I thought why not try and get a game developer job somewhere? To accomplish that, I thought having a Steam game in my portfolio would be a big benefit.

  • I really didn’t have any commercial aspirations at this time. All I knew about Steam is that there were about a million games on there and that very few could realistically make a living, so I didn’t think it was possible to consider it.

  • It was in during this month that my eventual developer name - LocalThunk - was incepted.

  • My partner was learning to code in R at the time, and she asked me “How do you name your variables?” I went on some rant about casing, using descriptive words, underscores, etc. She waits until I am finished and says “I like to call mine thunk”. I thought that was just about the funniest thing I had ever heard.

  • The way variables are declared in Lua is (sometimes) with the local keyword, thus local thunk was born! I wouldn’t choose this name for quite a while yet but this is the moment I looked back on when I was finally ready to create a developer handle online.

June 2022

  • Still working on the game (details of what exactly I was adding at this point are fuzzy). I think by this time Joker Poker has economy, a bunch of Jokers, and generally resembles the current version of Balatro.

  • I share a new version of the game with some friends.

August 2022

  • One of those friends gets back to me months later with very high praise. Unexpectedly high. He mentions that he has played the beta for dozens of hours.

  • In all my years of sharing projects with friends I had never received feedback like that. I couldn’t believe it, and it really pushed me over the edge to fully flesh out this idea. It was really great knowing that this game was actually being enjoyed by someone I know, and I wanted to prove to him, my other friends, my partner, my family, and myself that I could make something really fun and interesting.

  • At this point I started really increasing scope. More Jokers, Controller support, touch support, Jimbo the cheeky tutorial character, a soundtrack, proper sound effects, special editions for cards, and way more got added to the docket. I felt like scope creep incarnate, and it was amazing.

January 2023

  • As anticipated, my partner got a really great job in a different province and I quit my job to move there with her. I was really excited for this new chapter in our lives, and I also decided to not look for work for the next 3-6 months and develop Joker Poker full time.

  • It was the perfect opportunity to work as a game dev full time (even if for free) and I didn’t know if I would ever have the chance again. I had some money saved up, so starting this month I really leaned into it.

February 2023

  • I took a brief vacation to surf in California with some friends. I showed them the new build I had been working on - a version that would end up on Steam within a few months. This version now had Vouchers!

March 2023

  • This month I contacted Luis Clemente on the freelance website Fiverr and he delivered an absolutely amazing soundtrack for Joker Poker. Really knocked it out of the park. I was very nervous about this because it was (at the time) the only money I had spent or planned to spend on the game.

  • I also showed the game to some friends during a discord call, and one friend made the suggestion that there should be a flaming effect on your scoreif you get a really good hand. I hated the idea at first, but I sat with it for a while and knew I was wrong. That flaming score effect really fit perfectly with the game I was trying to create. Sure glad I listened to him!

  • I workshopped a list of ~20 names for the game. I saw that the game ‘Joker Poker’ was already an app on the app store, but beyond that it felt just a bit too silly for the vibe I was shooting for. I sent this list of new names to my friends and got all of their feedback. Balatro was among that list but nobody picked it. Something about it really stuck with me so I just went with it.

April 2023

  • I started creating store assets this month. I created a trailer, got my screenshots, and paid the very scary $100 Steam fee to upload a game. I also had a pretty massive code refactor at this point.

  • The name is officially Balatro.

  • I was pretty overwhelmed by Steam, just starting to look into things like how to launch an indie game? was really freaking me out. There are so many games released on Steam every day and so much noise about what you should and shouldn’t do, what type of game you should make, what method you should use to market it. It was a real shock to me that even though I had this game that I poured so much time into that it didn’t really seem to matter. I didn’t think this game would sell any copies before this and my initial research into Steam reinforced that idea to me.

May 2023

  • I downloaded Slay the Spire and played it for the first time. Holy shit. now that is a game.

  • I did this because I was having some troubles in my controller implementation and I wanted to see how they handled controller inputs for a card game but I ended up getting sucked in. Thank goodness I avoided playing it until now because I surely would have just copied their incredible design (intentionally or subconsciously).

  • In late May, I uploaded the first public Beta build to Steam, set my store page live, and saw exactly what I expected to happen happened: nothing. The fact that I wasn’t anticipating any fanfare probably saved me some heartache because I didn’t end up feeling dejected from the very tepid response my free Beta was getting from players.

  • Feedback started coming in from the Beta players. This marks the beginning of a more player-focused development strategy I would adhere to instead of just listening to my gut all the time.

  • By end of May, Balatro had 48 wishlists on Steam

Early June 2023

  • This is about the time when Balatro stopped being a pure hobby for me.

  • YouTubers started covering the game. It was mostly small and indie game specific channels, but the attention definitely started growing over time. The Discord server I had made for feedback about the game started taking up more of my time. However, at this time there was nothing to indicate that the game was going to start popping off.

  • Even so - I started really thinking about the commercial viability of the game. Could I actually make some money from this? I saw a glimmer of a possibility that this might turn into something.

  • My partner and I went on a big backcountry canoe trip for a few days. During this trip we talked about the future of this game and what it meant for my work. Even with the mild momentum I was ready to move on, and since back in January I told her this would be at most 6 months I felt that I shouldn’t push it too much longer. It had already been a year and a half of dev at this point.

  • I prepared to launch the game in 2 weeks. The release date went live on my Steam page, and I started thinking about moving on to looking for a job again and getting back into normal software/IT work.

  • I got a DM on twitter from a scout at Playstack, my eventual publisher. I was super excited but this also complicated things. This was a very tumultuous time in the history of the game because I was in limbo between nothing will come of this game and I want to move on with my life and what if I could do this as a job?

  • By June 10th, Balatro had 183 wishlists on Steam

Mid June 2023

  • Dan Gheesling plays Balatro on his stream. This is a massive streamer for my game (and a great guy!), and it starts making my wishlist numbers spike.

  • Other medium sized Youtubers and streamers start playing the game, it is building momentum. At this point the game was spreading entirely from word of mouth. I had posted a few things on Reddit and Twitter but really nothing that ever gained meaningful traction; now the game had inertia by itself.

  • It becomes clear that I should pursue the game a little further. I am approached by a few different publishers around now. I get some really great support and advice from my parents about everything going on. I strongly consider going it alone, but I also know that I need some help with all the public facing things, marketing, possible porting and just navigating this new thing I knew nothing about.

  • I hire a lawyer to help me with all the contract negotiations, help me set up a business, and I decide to sign with Playstack after speaking to studios that have worked with them in the past. They can replace whatever salary I would have made from an IT job, so this is my job now!

  • While this was a very exciting time, it was also incrediblystressful. I’m not used to dealing with so many people, and I felt pressure from so many different directions that I wasn’t expecting.

  • I created a demo version of the game that only lasted 50 rounds after my free Beta tester queue started getting bigger. At this point I had 300 beta testers for the full game and didn’t really need any more. This demo strategy wasn’t the best idea, but it allowed people to see what Balatro was all about without just getting the full game for free. The demo was getting around 10-20 concurrent players, which was really mind blowing for me at the time.

  • By end of June, Balatro had 2440 wishlists on Steam

July 2023

  • I release big updates around this time for the playtesters. I add the skip tag system, and I totally revamp the tutorial to basically what it is today.

  • The trajectory for the game appears at this time to be big enough for me to replace my income for a year at least. This was so exciting to me, I hadn’t really thought about it before because I was doing this work on my weekends and during my evenings anyway.

  • After a bunch of negotiations I finally sign a publishing deal with Playstack. I won’t go too far into whether or not people should or shouldn’t have a publisher, but for me at this time it was crucial that I have some publishing help. They outlined a very sensible release plan for the game (one that we adhered to almost exactly until launch in Feb 2024) and they really expanded the audience a ton with support for more languages and porting on day 1 for every major console.

  • My demo is getting some attention from streamers still, but one day in mid July, Northernlion was pitched the game on his stream from Dan Gheesling and ended up playing it that same day. I was in the chat watching, totally dumbfounded, that someone I watched on Twitch/YouTube for over a decade at that point was playing my game. I text all my friends about it, they are all shocked as well. At the time I was more concerned about a potential crash or something going horribly wrong but I did also try to just sit, watch, and enjoy the surreal moment.

  • Northernlion was playing the 50 round demo version - and after he quickly ran through all 50 rounds I got his attention and gave him a key to play the full beta version. I think some people weren’t super fond of this because he got special treatment - but honestly I was more in fan mode than dev mode. Looking back I’m glad I gave him the key - that was a massive moment for my game and even though it was tricky to navigate.

  • Playstack and I decide that we should take down the demo and come up with a better strategy than limiting players to 50 rounds

  • By end of July, Balatro had 28,661 wishlists on Steam

August 2023

  • The new demo plan is in place, and most of August is me preparing for a September release of this demo. The plan now is to make the demo content limited instead of round limited. This means that players can play as much as they want, which is much more sensible.

  • The demo should be ready a couple weeks before the Fall 2023 Next Fest in October. Next Fest is a festival set up by Steam to shine a spotlight on upcoming games for the platform. While we weren’t planning on adding Balatro as an official entry in the October 2023 Next Fest - we thought having a demo available at the same time as the festival might gather some incidental attention.

  • This is when my sleep and heart started having issues. I have talked about this a tiny bit in the past, but I really struggled with my physical and mental health from this time onward all the way up to launch. This was entirely because of the stress around dealing with the public, players, and the pressure to get everything done before February 2024.

  • We start exploring the feasibility of porting the game to different platforms. I won’t talk about this much more because of the agreements around those platforms, but suffice it to say this ended up being a very large portion of my work time over the coming months

  • By end of August, Balatro had 38,011 wishlists on Steam

September 2023

  • I start converting all the text in the game to an external english file to facilitate localization to other languages. I didn’t plan on having this game translated when it was a hobby so I had to do this all after the fact. It didn’t end up being too horrible, just some rework that I could have saved if I knew I was doing this from the beginning.

  • Near the end of September, I launch the new demo on Steam. People generally really like it! I try and make some tweaks based on player feedback, but it’s clear this approach is much better than limiting players to 50 rounds.

  • The last few months I have been very involved in the Balatro Discord server. I try and chat with players all the time, and I try to take their criticisms seriously. I feel like the demo and beta players knew more about how to play the game properly than I did, so I found it was really important to listen to what they had to say about the game in order to properly assess what I need to change or tweak for balance.

  • By the time the September demo is live, there are tons of players chatting in the Discord server all the time. I feel pretty overwhelmed by it all but I feel like I need to interact with them constantly. I didn’t want to waste the opportunity, and we also chatted a lot about general design things that players might be interested to see in the full 1.0 version of the game.

  • By end of September, Balatro had 49,791 wishlists on Steam

October 2023

  • Next Fest goes live early in the month, and as anticipated we do catch a lot of attention for having a demo live at the same time. We get pretty good coverage in general around this time from YouTubers, streamers, and even some media.

  • We originally planned on taking the demo down a week or so after the Next Fest ended, but so many people kept playing it that we decided to keep that version of the demo live for quite a while longer.

  • I started finalizing the content plan for the 1.0 version of the game. The biggest addition is the inclusion of a sort of ‘ascension’ system. This is from Slay the Spire (see? Told you I’d steal from it) but I think it was a super cool way to add difficulty and give players a sort of checklist to work through.

  • I also had a meeting with Playstack this month where I described to them the final content in the game, including ‘120 Jokers’. Later that week I had another meeting with them, and someone mentioned something about 150 Jokers. I couldn’t remember if I accidentally said I was going to make 150 or if they misheard me, but either way I thought that 150 was a much better number so I added 30 more Jokers to the plan.

  • I can’t remember if it was now or in September, but we enlist the help of Maarten De Meyer to help with our porting efforts. Maarten was absolutely instrumental in getting Balatro on every platform, and frankly he’s a Love2D wizard.

  • My sleep and my heart are getting worse. Every few nights I need to sleep sitting upright on the couch because sleeping while lying down kept getting interrupted by my heart. I stupidly thought that I couldn’t handle going to the doctor while dealing with all the dev and business stuff swirling around at the time. I felt totally overwhelmed.

  • By end of October, Balatro had 77,380 wishlists on Steam

November 2023

  • We keep the demo up for the entire month of November since our player numbers are still pretty high. More of the same from previous months. This is basically crunch time, so for the next couple months I’m just working on finishing everything in my plan.

  • The game is to be launched right after the following Next Fest scheduled for February 2024. This means that we want a new version of the demo with slightly different content to be available for that Next Fest, so I am working on that on the side.

  • Additionally, we have an idea to make an invitational tournament for streamers with an early build of this new version of the demo. This is scheduled to happen some time in late January 2024.

  • By end of November, Balatro had 87,280 wishlists on Steam

December 2023

  • More of the same. By this time it’s clear we can launch on every platform thanks to monumental effort by both Maarten and his wizardry and also a massive amount of optimization to my code base that I had worked on over the previous months.

  • We decide to take down the demo by the end of December because we want some breathing room before we launch the next iteration

  • By end of December, Balatro had 94,212 wishlists on Steam

January 2024

  • My heart is really bothering me. I routinely can’t sleep until the sun comes up, and my mental health is really suffering. I love working on the game but working on it so publicly and with such an intensity for so long is really catching up with me.

  • One night I am watching the movie The Abyss with my partner and suddenly I get tunnel vision, my heart is absolutely pounding and I feel like something is seriously wrong. I sit on the couch for a while, totally freaked out. I’m really scared.

  • I call my doctor and see him the next morning for an appointment. He assures me that this wasn’t a heart attack or heart failure but an anxiety attack. I am not normally an anxious person and have never had issues with this in the past but I think the intense stress for such a long time has done quite a bit of damage.

  • He asks me if my work has been stressful lately. I don’t even know how to explain.

  • January 19th is the invitational tournament. We have 6 streamers participate. This was actually a really great moment for me to sit back and watch the fruits of my labour for once - not work on the game and just enjoy.

  • My partner and I order sushi, sit down and watch the multi-stream broadcast by MurphyObv playing a hilarious character. It was probably my favourite moment of the entire pre-launch phase of the game.
