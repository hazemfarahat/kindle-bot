An Italian farmer found some unknown seeds in his attic and planted them

An Italian Farmer Found Some Unknown Seeds in His Attic and, With Nothing to Lose, Planted Them. The Result Was Almost Biblical

When a seven-eared stem emerged from the ground, it seemed like a miracle. What Claudio Grossi had unearthed was an ancient grain that proved to be an answer to some modern problems

Sometime in the early 1990s, Claudio Grossi, a 40-year-old farmer in Parma, in northern Italy, was cleaning his attic when he turned up an old sack full of seeds that he couldn’t identify. The bag, he was sure, had been left by his late great-grandfather, who’d started the farmstead around 1915. Grossi had no idea how long the seeds had been sitting there, deprived of water, soil and sun. Dry and cracked, the kernels looked so old in the palm of his hand that he doubted anything would ever sprout from them. He carried the bag down the stairs, shook the seeds out into a pile of manure and then forgot about them.

To his surprise, the seeds soon sprouted a few different types of wheat. One was particularly striking: At six feet high, its plants were about twice as tall as commercial wheat. But the most remarkable feature came when it developed ears—the grain-studded parts at the top of the plant. All of the wheat types Grossi had previously seen grew only one such ear. But the tall wheat grew a full seven ears, always in the same pattern: a long one with six shorties bunched up around it like little fingers. Even the kernels themselves were bigger than those of average wheat. And unlike the typical pale-yellow wheat kernels, they came in a rainbow of colors—yellow, black, red, white and even green, sometimes all on the same stem. Grossi, a grain farmer his whole life, had never seen anything like it. “I thought it was a freak of nature,” he says. He couldn’t find a mention of it in agricultural books.

For Grossi, a practicing Catholic, the next insight came from a different book altogether. Leafing through a Bible at church one Sunday, he found a passage in the Book of Genesis describing a stalk with seven ears, in a dream that Joseph interprets for the Pharaoh: “And he slept and dreamed the second time: And, behold, seven ears of grain came up upon one stalk, rank and good. And, behold, seven thin ears and blasted with the east wind sprung up after them. And the seven thin ears devoured the seven rank and full ears.” To Grossi, that sounded just like the mysterious crop in his manure pile.

“I connected the dots,” Grossi says. That grain must be really ancient, he thought. And if people were farming this odd-looking wheat in biblical times, why does no one today? 

Back on his farm, Grossi harvested the grain, milled it into flour and baked a loaf of bread in his kitchen. Compared with commercial wheat, the dough didn’t rise well, but as the loaf sat in the oven, it filled the kitchen with a surprisingly nutty and delicious aroma. Once finished, it had a rich, satisfying taste. “You get these notes of sweetness that you can’t find in regular commercial flour,” he says.

Did you know? More miracles of grain in the Bible

  • In 2 Kings 4:42-44, Elisha multiplies 20 barley loaves in order to feed 100 men—anticipating Jesus’s “Feeding of the 5,000” in the gospels. 

  • In 1 Kings 17:8-16, Elisha helps a widow at Zarephath to outlast a drought and famine with only a handful of flour and a little oil. 

  • In Judges 7:9-15, a Midianite soldier recounts a dream in which a barley loaf tumbles into the camp and overturns a tent. Gideon, the judge and military leader, recognizes the dream as a sign that God will deliver the Midianites into Israel's hands. 

  • In 2 Kings 4:38-41, during a famine, someone gathers wild gourds and cuts them into a pot of stew. The diners discover that the stew is dangerous and cry out to Elisha. He calls for flour and throws it into the pot—and the stew becomes miraculously safe to eat. 

Grossi discovered it had other advantages: It was hardier and more self-sufficient than commercial varieties. It fared better during droughts, growing so tall that it overshadowed the weeds. It didn’t need fertilizer or herbicides. Indeed, it seemed so superior to its industrial cousins that he thought of it no longer as a freak of nature but as a wonder. He dubbed it grano del miracolo—“miracle grain” or “miracle wheat.” 

Grossi’s discovery came at just the right time. In Italy in the 1990s, a burgeoning slow-food movement aimed to preserve traditional farming practices and promote natural diets.

Grossi hoped that miracolo could ride this slow-food wave, becoming both a portal to the past and a passage to a future. Perhaps, he thought, miracolo could help other farmers bring back ancient grains, leading the movement that is changing how we make bread, a staple of the human diet. And today, as climate change makes droughts, heat waves and plant disease outbreaks more frequent and intense, a grain that has shown it can fend for itself would help keep that bread on the table.

Farmers began cultivating wheat about 10,000 years ago, in the Fertile Crescent—the area of the Middle East known as the Cradle of Civilization. Thousands of years ago, the hardy and adaptable plant took hold in Europe and Asia. Compared with our modern crops, these ancient wheats were much taller, outperforming other grasses in the field.  

Over the millennia, humans bred different varieties of wheat, some yielding less grain and others more. As human populations grew, the strains of wheat that brought plentiful harvests became more desirable. That pattern accelerated amid the upheavals of the early 20th century. By the early 1920s, Italy was still recovering from World War I and unable to grow enough grain to sustain itself; the country was importing roughly a third of its wheat. When fascist leader Benito Mussolini became prime minister in 1922, he launched a campaign to “free the Italian people from the slavery of foreign bread,” by pushing for wheat varieties that yielded greater harvests. The Italian government promoted and planted these “elite” varieties, then distributed the seeds to farmers. Named la Battaglia del Grano, or the Battle of Wheat, this campaign became a global model for agrarian development. 

In the United States, wheat harvests were then still plentiful, but not for long: By the early 1930s, amid unprecedented droughts, American farmers found the land was exhausted. The result was the Dust Bowl, during which the previously fertile soils of the Great Plains turned into a desert. American farmers had to look for more drought-resistant varieties that still yielded enough grain. 

The midcentury Green Revolution, a movement launched by the American agronomist Norman Borlaug, focused on selective breeding and boosted crop production. This work spread worldwide, feeding countless people. The wheat that was created was indeed more plentiful, yet it came with trade-offs. The petite plants couldn’t always outcompete weeds and were susceptible to disease, so growers used weed killers, fungicides and fertilizer to sustain the crops. Little did they know that these practices would only weaken the health of the soil—while making the wheat even less resilient to changing climate and extreme weather events.

In the 1990s, Grossi sensed that anxiety over wheat harvests was building. That’s why, after he grew wheat from those seeds in the attic, he set out to put the hardy ancient grain back on the table. First, he realized he’d have to solve a big problem: Bread made from the wheat leavened poorly, rising only to fall again. In the early 2000s, he began discussing this challenge with an old local baker who shunned modern machines and still kneaded his dough by hand. The baker was intrigued. The two men started experimenting.

They tried letting the dough rise in smaller batches, using wicker baskets that were taller and helped the dough keep its shape. Soon, the two found another secret: Less kneading made miracolo leaven even better. “The dough was rising, and it was holding its shape,” Grossi says.

One new fan of the bread was Beniamino Perobelli, a student in food science at the University of Parma, who tasted the miracolo bread at a local farmers market and dedicated his undergraduate thesis to investigating its nutritional value. That work bore out what Grossi had already intuited: This wheat was truly ancient—and truly special. 

Working with the lab of Teofilo Vamerali, then a professor at the University of Parma, Perobelli determined that the grain was Triticum turgidum—literally “swollen wheat” in Latin. Known in English as “poulard wheat,” it had originally evolved in what today are Turkey, Egypt and other nearby Mediterranean countries. And searching agricultural references, Perobelli found that it had been grown in Italy, too, until farmers shifted toward higher-yield commercial varieties. That solved the mystery of the seed bag stashed away in the attic: It had survived because Grossi’s grandfather saved some of the heritage grains.

The food scientists found that, compared with modern wheats, grano del miracolo was richer in phosphorus and iron. Moreover, to their surprise, they found that T. turgidum had less gluten than modern wheat. And it packed a lot of lysine and tryptophan, the essential amino acids that serve as proteins’ building blocks. It also had more arginine, an amino acid that children need to grow. “The richness of microelements, minerals, and the greater presence of proteins other than gluten were very pleasant surprises,” says Perobelli, now a corporate social responsibility and sustainability manager with Rigoni di Asiago, an Italian company that produces organic fruit spreads, honey and more.

For Grossi, Perobelli’s findings offered scientific proof of his grain’s goodness. He persuaded Molino Grassi, a local organic grain company, to offer the flour to artisanal bakers. Over the next few years, Grossi upped production, so Molino Grassi could sell the flour to restaurants and consumers in Italy and abroad. 

By the 2010s, traditional recipes were enjoying a renaissance. So were artisanal bakers and pasta makers, who also became more discerning about which grains they used, says Francesco Sottile, an expert in biodiversity of agrosystems at the University of Palermo. 

Ancient grains aren’t necessarily perfect. For example, Grossi has found that miracolo’s height can be a liability—which may be why it fell out of favor with Mussolini. Heavy winds, rains and hail can make the tall stems flip over before the grain ripens, ruining harvests. But Grossi circumvented the problem by planting miracolo with shorter wheat varieties that provide structural support. “I’m quite happy because I developed something that’s like a discovery,” he says.

Relying on a limited number of wheat cultivars, Sottile argues, leaves humanity vulnerable to agricultural disasters: prolonged droughts, extreme heat, new diseases or pests that develop resistance to our chemicals. Diversifying grains reduces the risk—even if some varieties succumb to environmental pressures, others will stand.

Sottile shared an example. “I’m based in Sicily,” he says, where 2024 saw “one of the worst droughts in the last 35 to 40 years.” It was so bad that many commercial wheat growers abandoned their fields in April of that year, realizing they wouldn’t be able to reap the seeds they sowed. Sottile was heartened to see a better fate among smaller-batch farmers who grew heritage grains, more resilient to extreme weather.

“Most of the farmers who were adopting ancient grains, they were able to collect, to harvest,” Sottile says. The world needs more crop biodiversity, he adds—including more ancient grains like grano del miracolo. By the same token, we need more people like Grossi, who, much like the ancient grain he sows, have the tenacity to make a miracle happen.

#How the vulnerability works

When a Linux server accepts a TCP connection, the kernel tracks the half-open handshake in a lightweight "request socket," then creates a full socket once the handshake completes. If the connecting peer's IPv4 header carried options, the kernel keeps them to echo in its replies. The bug: the kernel handed those saved options from the request socket to the new connection without copying them, then freed them immediately when the connection went away, while another CPU could still be reading them to build a handshake reply. Reading freed kernel memory is a use-after-free.

The peer needs no login, and it all happens inside the kernel's handshake handling, before any application code runs. The CNA's scoring notes say the stale data can leak kernel heap memory back to the peer, steer kernel writes out of bounds, or crash the machine. Anthropic says a lost race "only fails the handshake without crashing the whole system, so a determined attacker can retry indefinitely until they succeed."

#How to fix

#Update the kernel and reboot

Install your distribution's fixed kernel as soon as it ships, then reboot: a kernel update doesn't take effect until the machine boots into it. Reboot container hosts and Kubernetes nodes too. Appliances and cloud images that bundle a kernel need their vendor's update.

On Debian, SUSE Linux Enterprise Server 16.0 and the 15 LTSS releases, openSUSE Leap 16.0, Oracle Linux's UEK kernels, and Amazon Linux 2 and 2023, the fix is out. #If you can't patch yet

Red Hat's bulletin and AlmaLinux now publish nftables rules that drop incoming IPv4 TCP packets carrying IP options. Red Hat says its rule "reduces exposure to this vulnerability but cannot fully prevent it." The following is Atlas's interim recommendation, the same rule as AlmaLinux's, based on the CNA's scoring notes, which say the attacking peer supplies IPv4 Record Route or Timestamp options in its SYN. TCP traffic almost never carries IPv4 options legitimately, so dropping TCP packets that have them should cost nothing:

1nft add table ip cve_2026_984322nft add chain ip cve_2026_98432 pre '{ type filter hook prerouting priority -300; }'3nft add rule ip cve_2026_98432 pre meta l4proto tcp ip hdrlength gt 5 counter drop

ip hdrlength gt 5
matches any IPv4 header longer than the 20-byte minimum, which only options produce. The rule runs before connection tracking and doesn't touch IPv6 or non-TCP traffic. We tested it in a Debian 12 container: it dropped a SYN carrying a Record Route option, and ordinary TCP connections still worked. We haven't tested it against an exploit, and it doesn't fix the kernel. It lasts until reboot, which is fine if the reboot is into a fixed kernel; remove it with 
nft delete table ip cve_2026_98432
. If your edge firewall can drop IPv4 packets with options, doing it there protects every host behind it.

If you manage the firewall with iptables, this rule does the same job from the 

raw
table, which also runs before connection tracking:

1iptables -t raw -I PREROUTING -p tcp -m u32 --u32 "0>>24&0xF=6:15" -j DROP

The 

u32
match reads the IPv4 header-length field, and a value of 6 to 15 means the header carries options. We tested it with iptables 1.8.9, on both its 
nf_tables
and legacy backends, in a Debian 12 container: it dropped a Record Route SYN sent to the host and one routed through it, and ordinary TCP connections still worked. Remove it by running the same command with 
-D

-I

xt_u32

Red Hat's rule hooks 

input

prerouting
, so it also drops packets the host routes on to containers. On October 31 we tested it against two pairs of network namespaces. It dropped a Record Route SYN routed between one pair, and never saw one bridged between the other, as AlmaLinux warns: "Traffic between containers on the same host may bypass it." AlmaLinux also says not to use it on networks that rely on CIPSO security labels, which travel in IPv4 options.

Turn off multipath TCP if you don't use it. That removes the setup Anthropic's exploit needs, though not the bug. AlmaLinux's steps:

1echo 'net.mptcp.enabled = 0' | sudo tee /etc/sysctl.d/90-disable-mptcp.conf2sudo sysctl -w net.mptcp.enabled=03sudo ss -Mlnp    # listeners opened earlier stay MPTCP: restart their services

New MPTCP sockets then fail, and Go programs fall back to plain TCP when they start. The setting is per network namespace, and each new one starts with MPTCP on, so a container keeps it unless you set it there too (Docker:

). We tested the steps and the Docker flag on October 31. For a single Go program, 
GODEBUG=multipathtcp=0
in its environment turns the default off, but the Go Security Team calls disabling MPTCP program by program "a fragile mitigation."

Until you can patch, keep MPTCP listeners off the internet, and put the hosts that have one at the top of the patch list.

Jepsen: ParticleDB 1.1.4

1 Background

Modern applications often spread state across a variety of systems: databases, caches, queues, data warehouses, search indices, and so on. Coordinating these systems is challenging: caches are often stale, the analytics platform might lag days behind the primary database, the search index might lose data, and transactional updates across disparate systems remains an open research problem.

ParticleDB1 aims to unify these workloads in a single engine with a special focus on AI agents and applications. It offers transactional SQL, a key-value store, vector search, live analytics, and user-defined functions called Particles.2 To integrate with existing systems, clients can connect through a variety of interfaces, including the Postgres and Redis protocols, as well as its own gRPC and HTTP2 APIs. Written from the ground up in Rust, ParticleDB claims to be orders of magnitude faster than Postgres while preserving the same safety characteristics.

ParticleDB was built by Orkes to support their workflow orchestration platform, Conductor. Conductor was initially built to store data in Redis and Postgres, but to improve performance, Orkes opted to write their own database. ParticleDB 1.0.0 offered a limited feature set specifically for Conductor’s queries, and was deployed as the backing database for some of their Conductor clusters. A few Orkes customers followed suit. In preparation for a broader release, Orkes undertook a major refactoring of ParticleDB, adding a custom wire protocol, more datatypes, additional OLAP queries, and more. While Orkes and their customers continued to run 1.0.0, they soft launched the refactored versions on particledb.ai. Orkes engaged Jepsen to test these versions of ParticleDB, beginning with 1.1.4.

While ParticleDB could run as a single node, it also offered a replicated cluster using the Raft consensus protocol. This replication feature was available only in enterprise cloud deployments. The builds Orkes provided did not support replication, so this report covers only single-node behavior.

1.1 Safety

ParticleDB’s home page advertised that ParticleDB was “formally proven” with a TLA+ proof. However, during our collaboration no TLA+ specification was available. It is unclear what properties might be proven, or whether they described ParticleDB as built.

2 Test Design

We wrote a test suite for ParticleDB using the Jepsen testing library. We ran ParticleDB versions 1.1.4 through 1.1.18.11.rc-22 on individual Debian 13 nodes, both in containers and EC2 VMs. Our test harness could simulate process crashes by killing ParticleDB, simulate the loss of un-

fsync
ed writes using LazyFS, and truncate or corrupt disk files. We ran ParticleDB using each transaction mode and all three WAL sync modes.

We first tried ParticleDB’s official Java SDK. While the documentation indicated that the library was available as a Maven dependency, neither the JAR nor source code were actually available. Instead, we began with the official Postgres JDBC client, version 42.7.11, and later added support for the ParticleDB Java SDK, versions 1.0.0 through 1.0.2.

2.1 Workloads

We used several Jepsen workloads from other SQL databases, and designed new ones specifically for ParticleDB.

The 

rw
workload performed randomly generated transactions involving reads and writes of integer values identified by unique integer keys. We stored each value in a single row and accessed them both via primary and unindexed secondary keys. Keys and values were encoded as a variety of datatypes, including integers, decimals, and strings. We assumed that writes followed reads within each transaction, and that individual keys were sequentially consistent, and used these assumptions to infer constraints on the version orders and transaction dependency graphs. Jepsen’s transactional checker, Elle, then searched for cycles in that dependency graph, as well as a variety of non-cyclic phenomena like G1a (Aborted Read).

The 

append
workload also performed randomly generated transactions over rows identified by unique integer keys, but the values were lists of integers. Transactions could read one of these lists by key, or append a unique integer to a list by key. We encoded each list as a comma-separated 
text
field, and used SQL 
concat
to append to them. Rows could be selected via primary key or unindexed secondary key, or via a join to an indirection table. From there, we used Elle to infer version orders and dependency graphs, and searched for a variety of cyclic and non-cyclic phenomena.

When these workloads revealed consistency violations within a single transaction, we designed an 

internal
workload to stress internal consistency. This workload performed a series of randomly generated transactions involving 
INSERT
, 
UPDATE
, 
DELETE
, and 
SELECT

To investigate tantalizing anomalies in the

 workload. This workload used Clojure’s test.check generative testing library to produce random tables and simple SQL statements over them, then executed those statements against both ParticleDB and PostgreSQL. If ParticleDB’s behavior was not equivalent to PostgreSQL’s, the compare workload would search for a simpler sequence of statements that could reproduce the same divergence. Although our SQL generator was relatively simple—just a few basic datatypes, literals, arithmetic, comparisons, boolean logic, transactions, and some simple 
LIMIT

OFFSET

Drawing on these successes, we devised a hybrid

It then checked each transaction independently to ensure that each of its statements observed results consistent with those preceding. For example, if a transaction executed 
DELETE ... WHERE 2 < c
, then observed a row with

3 Results

We found 96 issues with ParticleDB, which we present in several categories below. All but three of these issues occurred in healthy, single-node instances of ParticleDB. Busy readers may wish to jump to the Discussion section, which summarizes our findings.

3.1 Availability and Performance

Under normal operation, ParticleDB was slow, deadlocked, and crashed without warning.

3.1.1 Abysmal Performance With 
table-2pl

In ParticleDB 1.1.4 through 1.1.18.4, 

--txn-mode table-2pl

For example, take this test run with a single node running 1.1.18.4, transaction mode

High latencies (#62) appeared improved by 1.1.18.6, which could execute transactions with sub-100 millisecond latencies for at least a few seconds.

3.1.2 Mystery Crash (#81)

While 1.1.18.6 improved latencies, it also crashed frequently. Crashes occurred spontaneously with various transaction modes, WAL modes, and client interfaces; there was no obvious cause. Nodes would run for a few hundred to a few thousand seconds before exiting. No error messages were printed. These crashes (#81) no longer appeared in 1.1.18.11.rc-18.

3.1.3 Deadlock After Two Transactions (#27)

In ParticleDB 1.1.18.8, submitting as few as two concurrent transactions to a healthy, single-node deployment could cause it to deadlock. This occurred both using the Postgres and ParticleDB interfaces. The node would log an error message like 

[DEADLOCK] 1 cycles detected at SystemTime ...

3.2.3 Malformed Responses for Prepared Statements (#2)

In ParticleDB 1.1.4 through 1.1.18.9, any attempt to execute a prepared statement using the Postgres JDBC driver caused an exception like

3.8 Transactions and Concurrency

We found several problems with ParticleDB’s transactions and concurrency control. All of these occurred in single-node instances without fault injection.

3.8.1 Nested Transactions Destroy Ongoing Ones (#44)

ParticleDB 1.1.4 through 1.1.18.9 allowed clients to begin nested transactions, instead of warning that a transaction was already ongoing. Any writes performed by the enclosing transaction would be lost, both within the nested transaction and after commit.

3.8.7 A Thousand Tableaus (#38, #66, #68, #69)

Perhaps owing to the single-threaded issues discussed above, ParticleDB 1.1.4 through 1.1.18.9 exhibited frequent, profound transactional anomalies in essentially every way we could measure. 4 Discussion

ParticleDB 1.1.4 claimed to be a Postgres-compatible database which offered Snapshot Isolation with its

and

serializable-ssi

Instead, we found a library of confusion.10 In versions 1.1.4 through 1.1.18.11.rc-19, basic functionality like transactions (#3), prepared statements (#2), and Postgres auth (#1) were unusably broken. The server spontaneously deadlocked (#27, #71) or crashed (#81) every few minutes. Integers stood in for booleans (#30, #50), comparisons to 

NULL
threw errors (#13), and you could assign multiple values to the same column (#5). Large floating-point numbers quietly became infinite (#56); 
Infinity
, 
NaN
, and 
NULL
arithmetic were broken (#36, #92, #15, #21); and out-of-range values were improperly stored or clipped to fit (#32, #11). In ParticleDB, 
0.0
was not equal to 
0
(#63), but 
0.90...
wasn’t not equal to 
5
(#80). Strings allowed too many (#52, #64) and too few (#55) characters. A 
WHERE
clause with floating-point operations would silently fail to match any rows (#47), 
WHERE 4 = 5
returned rows with primary key 
5
(#18), 
WHERE a = 1
matched the row 
{a: 2}
, and 
WHERE TRUE
matched nothing (#35). Null (#8) and unique (#9) constraints did nothing, and primary keys could be duplicated (#29), null (#57), or both. Primary keys failed to conflict with rows that did exist (#90), and conflicted with rows that didn’t (#82). Joins returned incorrect column names (#54), coerced values to strings (#76), failed to return rows (#59), and confused similarly-named columns (#77). It was impossible to delete rows within a transaction (#26, #45), rows with integers set to floats (#23), or with floats set to integers (#84), or those containing 
NaN
s (#78), or fixed-width strings (#74).11 Rows from dropped tables could re-emerge in mangled forms (#46). Columns could be set to values of the wrong type, silently altering or corrupting data (#7). Inserts ignored provided columns, creating rows best described as “deeply cursed” (#16).12 Updates could create new, shambolic rows where none existed (#24, #25). Strings sometimes turned into eight-bit integers (#37), and primary keys occasionally became 
NULL
(#72). Aborted transactions could commit, or fail with their effects still visible (#43). Queries sometimes missed (#48), duplicated (#89), or returned the wrong (#67) rows. Tables with a 
text
primary key were mostly invisible (#41). Special values were sometimes inalterable (#91). Entire columns occasionally vanished for no obvious reason (#65). Uncontended statements on freshly created tables observed nonsensical results or conflicted (apparently) with themselves (#31). Each transaction and WAL mode exhibited essentially every transactional anomaly our tests could detect: write loss, internal consistency violations, G0, G1a, G1b, G1c, G-Single, G-nonadjacent, P4, A5A, G2, and real-time variants thereof (#38, #66, #68, #69, #73, #70, #96, #109, #110, etc.).

These behaviors occurred in single-node instances of ParticleDB, without fault injection, often with just a single client. When we killed ParticleDB, it allowed internal anomalies, P4 (Lost Update), and G-single (#107), even with the new 

serializable
transaction mode. Corruption or truncation of WAL files caused partial data loss (#108). Simulating misdirected reads or writes could cause total data loss (#112).

As we concluded our engagement, version 1.1.18.11.rc-22 had just become available. It abandoned the

After we finished testing, Orkes updated ParticleDB’s documentation to claim that every transaction mode discussed here was “experimental”. This claim is difficult to support. To the best of Jepsen’s knowledge, none of the ParticleDB documentation described these modes as experimental or unsupported during our engagement. Orkes’ unpublished operator guide did describe one mode, 

serializable-occ

Orkes appears to have reworked ParticleDB’s approach to consistency. It now supports the standard SQL isolation level statements for

Jepsen has not evaluated versions of ParticleDB after 1.1.18.11.rc-22, and cannot speak to these isolation levels or concurrency control modes. However, Orkes claims that all issues from this report have been addressed as of version 1.1.19-rc.13.

As always, Jepsen takes an experimental approach to safety verification: we can prove the presence of bugs, but not their absence. While we make extensive efforts to find problems, we cannot prove correctness.

4.1 Impact

Orkes believes that the issues we found in 1.1.4 were likely due to an extensive refactor of ParticleDB in spring 2026, and that Orkes and their customers, who continued to run an earlier version, were likely unaffected. Near the end of our collaboration we briefly evaluated an internal build of version 1.0.0. While it exhibited some string and arithmetic issues, that version did not seem susceptible to most of the serious safety errors we found in 1.1.4, and passed cursory 

rw
and 
append
tests. We tested only a single node at a time, so we cannot speak to the safety of the replication system.

From May through July 2026, the ParticleDB download page offered users (at least) versions 1.1.4 through 1.1.18.11.rc-18.13 All exhibited serious safety errors during normal operation. It seems likely that users who ran these versions of ParticleDB would have lost or corrupted data. In version 1.1.18.11.rc-22, using WAL mode 

sync
and transaction mode 
serializable
significantly reduced the frequency and severity of these issues, but did not prevent internal anomalies or Lost Update.

Given the severity and extent of the safety errors we found, and the process which produced them, Jepsen recommends users halt further deployment of ParticleDB and migrate to other databases as soon as possible. Consider checking and/or rebuilding database state from more reliable sources, if available.

4.2 The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Generative Testing

The 

compare
workload generated random schemas and SQL statements over them, then ran both against ParticleDB and Postgres to see if they produced equivalent results. This should not have worked. There are many ways in which two “Postgres-compatible” databases can legitimately differ, such as floating-point details, which error message takes precedence, or the order of unsorted results. The space of SQL queries is large and difficult to cover. Many queries trivially return equivalent results (e.g. 
SELECT ... WHERE FALSE
) and therefore tell us little information. We can test only sequential behavior, which is easy for a database’s standard test suite to check.

Nevertheless, roughly two thousand lines of generative tests found a wealth of bugs in ParticleDB, and unlike Jepsen’s typical workloads, the examples generated were usually easy to reproduce. ParticleDB offered a good deal of “low-hanging fruit” that should have been caught by example-based tests—but the 

compare
workload also found more subtle errors. For example, the fact that 
0.90...
was apparently equal to 
5
hinged on a high-precision floating-point literal. Inspired by this success, we extracted part of the ParticleDB SQL generator into a shared library so it could be used in other tests, like the new 
internal-sim
workload.

Jepsen has traditionally focused on thorough analysis of a small set of carefully chosen transactions. This is partly because checking (e.g.) Serializability in general is NP-complete, and also because we assume that if transactions of (e.g.) reads and appends work correctly on strings, so will other operations on other datatypes. Our intuition is that the “hard part” is in the concurrency control mechanism, and sequential datatype behavior is generally covered by existing tests. This was not the case in ParticleDB. We’ve expanded Jepsen’s 

list-append
and 
rw
tests to use alternate encodings and transaction structures, in an attempt to cover more codepaths. This revealed new bugs in ParticleDB, and we hope it will in other databases too.

Unfortunately, 

compare
became less useful as Orkes fixed major bugs and left smaller incompatibilities in place. For example, Postgres checks unique constraints when updating each row of a table, but ParticleDB only checks after all have been updated. This means that a statement like 
UPDATE bools SET x = NOT x
can swap the values of 
TRUE
and 
FALSE
in ParticleDB without violating a unique constraint. Toward the end of our engagement, the 
compare
test generally hit these minor inconsistencies early, then spent most of its time searching for minimal cases of them—potentially missing more serious bugs.

4.3 Quality Assurance

Orkes said they ran Jepsen’s existing Postgres test harness to find and resolve issues in ParticleDB prior to this work. However, that same test harness failed immediately when run against ParticleDB 1.1.4. Orkes also stated they tested ParticleDB with SQLancer, which generates far more complex queries than the ones we tested. It is therefore perplexing that nearly every feature we tested exhibited glaring errors, and that our findings seemed to come as a surprise.

Most databases have a test suite which exercises at least basic examples of each feature, and it is customary to run those tests before release, if not continuously. Where were those tests for ParticleDB? How does one add support for 

NOT NULL
constraints without a test which tries to insert at least one 
NULL
value? What development process adds a 
CHARACTER(10)
type without trying to write a string of (e.g.) five characters?

Our findings suggest structural deficiencies in the ParticleDB QA process. Perhaps tests were not run prior to release. Perhaps the test suite was run, but failed to exercise basic functionality. Perhaps engineers entrusted Large Language Models with the work of testing.

Jepsen recommends that Orkes (and all database vendors!) build and exercise an automated test suite for their database. This suite should include at least simple, example-based tests covering important database features. These tests should fail when the feature is unimplemented or malformed, and pass when it is complete. Observing these failures is an important part of the development process: it validates that the test measures something. Tests should be run at least once before each release, and results disseminated to the team. Afterwards consider more sophisticated generative tests, establishing adversarial QA and implementation teams, and subtly breaking safety mechanisms to determine whether the tests are still effective.

4.4 Documentation

ParticleDB’s website gave the appearance of a mature database project. It made strong claims: a general-purpose OLTP/OLAP SQL database a thousand times faster than PostgreSQL, “formally verified” by a TLA+ proof, multiple APIs, and client libraries in thirteen languages. It offered a variety of deployment options and extensive documentation on database features and operations.

On closer inspection, that documentation was riddled with omissions, inconsistencies, and confabulations. The TLA+ proof was not published, and in any event did not prevent disaster—perhaps it was incorrect, formalized the wrong properties, or did not describe the system as built. Safety-critical configuration settings like the transaction mode were described in vague terms, like “safe under concurrency”. The documentation for the Java client library referred to non-existent dependency coordinates and seemed to make up class and package names which were nowhere to be found in the library itself. The quickstart and client examples made up a variety of default usernames for ParticleDB, all of which were wrong. The stored procedure documentation even fabricated an imaginary email address for the author of this report. In Orkes’ unpublished operator guide, the descriptions of transaction modes omitted basic phenomena like G0 and confused G1a (Aborted Read) with G1c (Circular Information Flow). The Snapshot Isolation modes made no mention of G-single or the other boundary phenomena proscribed by Snapshot Isolation, and non-real-time models made no mention of the real-time phenomena they allowed, like Stale Read.14

Technical writing is important work that requires skill and care. Jepsen recommends that Orkes work with a human technical writer to rewrite ParticleDB’s documentation, with an eye toward concisely and accurately communicating intended behavior and the guarantees users should expect.

Jepsen is no stranger to working with early software projects, and there is nothing intrinsically wrong with offering buggy or badly broken software. However, it would be polite for a maintainer aware of serious safety issues to display a prominent warning on the website. One might place a large red box on the download page which reads, “This project is in early development and is not suitable for production”, or more bluntly, “This software has lost data and will do so again”. Orkes should clearly explain the general status of ParticleDB, and advise users of specific known safety issues.

Orkes has since published a release history which describes versions 1.1.4 through 1.1.18.11.rc-22 as “unsupported pre-release builds”. It is not clear how users would have understood versions like 1.1.4 or 1.1.18.9 as “pre-release”. Pre-release software generally has a version number beginning with 0, or including “rc”, “alpha”, etc. The documentation did not describe these versions as pre-release or experimental at the time. Indeed, the Docker install page told users how to deploy these versions “for production”.

4.5 LLM Tradeoffs

Large Language Models enable small teams to produce software and documentation of staggering scope and complexity in a remarkably short time. Five years ago, writing a distributed SQL database of this scope might have taken hundreds of person-years.15 With LLM assistance, a handful of engineers at Orkes were able to produce a highly sophisticated database system.

However, our work on ParticleDB (and other databases) suggests there are serious tradeoffs to relying on LLMs. Quality suffered: ParticleDB was a fractal of correctness errors. LLM tooling repeatedly claimed that bugs had been fixed when, in fact, they had not. ParticleDB also seemed more difficult to understand than typical databases. As with most Jepsen analyses, Orkes engineers offered detailed explanations of the causes and resolutions of the bugs we found in Conductor. However, Orkes seemed unable to explain the causes or resolutions of nearly every issue we found in ParticleDB. New builds simply materialized, sans changelogs, each hopefully containing a slew of improvements. Why did ParticleDB sometimes turn strings into integers? Why did it stop in 1.1.18.11.rc-1? Perhaps no one knows. With LLMs, perhaps no one needs to.

More broadly, the use of LLMs erodes trust in documentation, in software, and in the organizations which produce them. Perhaps engineers will begin to treat any new software project with suspicion—the signals we used to rely on as markers of a mature system are now easier to counterfeit.

Where correctness matters—and surely it ought to matter for databases!—our industry must acknowledge and mitigate these tradeoffs.16 In the age of LLMs, what can maintainers do to ensure correct software and accurate documentation? Having done that work, how can they demonstrate its quality to users?

4.6 Future Work

As of 1.1.18.11.rc-22, our 

append
and 
rw
workloads were still finding anomalies in every transaction mode. We recommend further testing as development continues. The 
compare
workload also revealed a number of minor compatibility bugs which we have not discussed here. These small differences could mask more serious errors. Bringing ParticleDB into closer alignment with Postgres could help the 
compare
workload reveal them.

Toward the end of our collaboration we tested ParticleDB’s on-disk format, and found that corruption in WAL files could cause data loss. The variety of error messages we found suggests that there might be multiple paths to data loss—further tests should stress on-disk storage more thoroughly. Furthermore, in almost all our tests ParticleDB wrote only WAL files, and stored its tables entirely in memory. We recommend Orkes add a configuration option to reduce the thresholds for on-disk storage, so that future tests can exercise those codepaths.

The ParticleDB client has a number of slow network timeouts we did not know how to change. These timeouts could conceal safety errors by preventing the test from observing the database during a critical moment. We worked around this by using the Postgres client (whose timeouts are configurable), but we recommend testing the ParticleDB client with lower timeouts as well.

ParticleDB offered a massive set of features, and we only tested a small portion of them. The SQL specification and Postgres’ dialect of it are astonishingly complex languages, and we used only a small fraction of their syntax and vocabulary. We also did not evaluate ParticleDB’s Redis or HTTP APIs, temporal or geospatial queries, materialized views, vector search, or multi-tenant features. The replication system remains to be tested.

Orkes is now adding stored procedures to ParticleDB. These user-defined functions, called Particles, are written in Python or Rust and executed by the database server. Both Rust and Python have large surface areas, and executing user-provided binaries in a database environment seems like an exciting opportunity. Our previous work with stored procedures suggests this feature is worth further investigation.

ParticleDB claimed to be “1087x faster than PostgreSQL”. In our tests ParticleDB was nowhere near this fast, and over the course of an hour we observed a linear decline in throughput and a significant increase in median latencies—even over small datasets. Once ParticleDB reaches a baseline level of correctness, revisiting performance benchmarks could be worthwhile.

Do the Math: How We Took Over ChatGPT Accounts

By bypassing ChatGPT's sandbox and content security policy, our research team was able to take over anyone's account simply by sending them a link.

October 26, 2026Fixed

P1Severity

In October 2026, we found a way to turn an ordinary public ChatGPT share into attacker-controlled JavaScript running inside the signed-in session of whoever opened it.

An attacker could create a malicious conversation, publish it using ChatGPT's normal sharing feature, and send the link to another user. When the victim opened the share, the payload executed as 

chatgpt.com
with access to the victim's authenticated web session.

We confirmed that this code could enumerate the victim's conversation list, retrieve content from a private conversation that had never been shared with the attacker, and send data from that conversation to an external server. The payload was not limited to modifying the page or displaying an alert—it could directly call ChatGPT's authenticated APIs as the victim, essentially allowing us to take over the victim's ChatGPT account.

The victim did not need to install an App, join a Project, paste code, or open developer tools. Opening the shared link was the only required action.

OpenAI fixed the issue on October 26 and classified the report as P1.

The chain started with two features that looked harmless on their own: a graph and an HTML Preview.

      
flowchart TD
  A["Attacker publishes a ChatGPT conversation"] --> B["Victim opens the public share"]
  B --> C["HTML Preview crosses into the main chatgpt.com page"]
  C --> D["JavaScript runs in the victim's signed-in session"]
  D --> E["Any action on chatgpt.com as the victim:<br/>read private chats and files, change the account"]

Technical walkthrough

It started with a graph and a Preview

The question that started this research was simple: if Preview code was boxed into a different website, what could it still talk to?

ChatGPT can turn an HTML code block into a small working webpage using its Preview feature. Since that page may contain arbitrary code, ChatGPT sensibly runs it inside a restricted frame on a different web address. The Preview can run JavaScript, but the browser treats it as a different website, so it cannot directly inspect the signed-in ChatGPT page around it.

At first, this isolation appeared to work exactly as intended. Code inside the Preview could not read 

parent.document
, which is the main ChatGPT page. That should have been the end of it.

The conversation also contained a normal interactive graph. Behind the scenes, the graph loaded a math library called MathLive. Loading it did more than draw the graph: it also installed a listener in the main ChatGPT page for messages related to MathLive's on-screen keyboard.

Browsers call the relevant API 

postMessage
. It is a legitimate way for two frames to exchange information even when the browser prevents them from reading each other's pages. You can think of it as a mail slot: the restricted Preview cannot walk into the main page, but it can still drop a message through the slot.

That is safe only if the receiver checks who sent the message.

MathLive did not make that check for the setting we were interested in. Its listener accepted a message that changed the virtual keyboard layout, and that layout was allowed to contain HTML. The graph had installed the receiver; the Preview gave us an untrusted sender. Two unrelated features had accidentally created a path across the sandbox.

The message that should not have worked

The Preview sent two messages. The first replaced a virtual keyboard layout with our HTML. The second told MathLive to show the keyboard, causing it to render the new layout:

      
top.postMessage({
  type: "mathlive#virtual-keyboard-message",
  action: "update-setting",
  layouts: [{
    label: "Compatibility",
    layers: [{ id: "compatibility", markup }]
  }]
}, "*");

top.postMessage({
  type: "mathlive#virtual-keyboard-message",
  action: "show",
  animate: false
}, "*");

MathLive recognized the public message type, but its top-level controller used an origin policy of 

none
, which meant it accepted messages from any web address. It also did not require the 
update-setting
message to come from a previously approved window. Anyone who could reach the listener and knew the message format could replace the layout.

When the keyboard opened, MathLive joined the supplied layout into an HTML string and assigned it to 

innerHTML
in the real ChatGPT page. A helper named 
createHTML
initially looked like it might sanitize the value. It did not; it marked the string as trusted and passed our HTML through unchanged.

This was exciting, but we still did not have JavaScript execution. Script tags inserted through 

innerHTML
do not automatically run.

The next step was an 

iframe
using 
srcdoc
, a browser feature that lets an embedded frame carry its HTML directly in an attribute:

      
<iframe srcdoc="..."></iframe>

MathLive inserted this frame into the main ChatGPT document without a 

sandbox
attribute. That gave us two useful properties. First, 
srcdoc
created a fresh document where an external script could execute. Second, because the main ChatGPT page created the frame, the browser gave it the 
https://chatgpt.com
web identity. Code inside the new frame could therefore access the parent page.

Bypassing CSP with YouTube JSONP

ChatGPT's Content Security Policy, or CSP, stopped us again. CSP controls which scripts a page may load, and the policy correctly blocked inline JavaScript. It did, however, allow scripts from YouTube.

YouTube's oEmbed endpoint supported a JSONP-style callback parameter. JSONP responses are JavaScript rather than ordinary JSON. The endpoint tried to reject an invalid callback, but it placed the callback into the response before returning its validation error:

      
https://www.youtube.com/oembed?callback=globalThis.__marker=31337;void

The response began like this:

      
// API callback
globalThis.__marker=31337;void({
  "error": {
    "code": 400,
    "message": "Invalid JSONP callback name: ..."
  }
});

Ending our callback with 

;void
made the object appended by YouTube harmless. Since YouTube was already allowed by ChatGPT's CSP, the browser loaded this response as a script and ran our callback inside the new frame.

We now had arbitrary JavaScript running as chatgpt.com. The original Preview was still isolated on its separate web address; we had crossed the boundary by convincing a trusted component in the main page to create a new, unsandboxed document for us.

Turning a lab bug into one public link

So far, this was a useful sandbox escape, but the first working version was not a convincing real-world attack. It used a researcher-controlled ChatGPT App as the sender. A victim would have needed to connect or invoke that App first.

We next tried delivering the payload through a native HTML attachment. ChatGPT opened the attachment inside the same sandboxed Preview, so it could send the malicious MathLive messages.

However, when we exported the conversation as a public share, the attachment remained but the earlier graph did not. The graph was essential because it loaded MathLive and installed the vulnerable message listener in the main page. Without it, the attachment could still send the messages, but nothing was listening to or processing them.

The breakthrough was putting both pieces directly into one ordinary public conversation:

  • A genuine graph initialized MathLive in the main page.
  • A later assistant-authored HTML Preview sent the two messages.

The resulting 

/share/<id>
link preserved the complete chain for another user. The attacker could deliver it the same way they would send any shared ChatGPT conversation—through email, chat, a support ticket, or a social post. The victim did not need to install anything, join the attacker's workspace, or authenticate through an attacker-controlled flow. They just had to visit the attacker's shared ChatGPT URL.

We observed two versions of the interaction. In the most stable reproduction, the victim opened the share and clicked ChatGPT's normal Preview button once. We later marked the HTML block as previewable so that ChatGPT opened it automatically. In that version, opening the link was the victim's only click. We reproduced the automatic path three times in a separate account, including after a hard refresh, with the alert appearing after roughly 4.5 seconds.

The graph and Preview loaded independently, so the payload could occasionally send its message before MathLive was ready. The final carrier handled that race by retrying the same two messages for a limited period. A guard in the main page ensured that the private-conversation proof and outbound request ran only once.

At this point, the public share could display an alert identified by the browser as coming from 

chatgpt.com
and could add a banner to the main page. That proved where our code was running. It did not yet show why the bug mattered.

An alert was not enough

At this point, displaying an alert was the least interesting thing the exploit could do. The JavaScript was running as 

chatgpt.com
, which meant it could interact with ChatGPT using the same authenticated context as the normal web application.

The payload read the session information already available to the page and used it to call ChatGPT's conversation APIs. First, it enumerated the victim's recent conversations. It could then request message data for a selected private conversation:

      
GET /backend-api/conversations?...&limit=20&order=updated&offset=0
GET /backend-api/conversations/<conversation-id>?num_turns=2

This was direct authenticated API access, not scraping text that happened to be visible on screen. The target conversation was unrelated to the malicious share and had never been shared with the attacker.

Once the response was available to the injected JavaScript, sending data off-site was straightforward. The payload created an ordinary image request to a server we controlled.

We deliberately sent only a small piece of the response off-site. The injected code had already received the authenticated conversation response and could read its contents, so the vulnerability was not limited to that value.

The result was a practical cross-user data-theft primitive. A malicious public conversation could execute inside another user's signed-in ChatGPT session, enumerate private conversations, retrieve private message content, and send selected data to an attacker-controlled server.

Because the code ran with the web application's authenticated access, its reach was not inherently limited to the conversation endpoints used in our proof. Other data and actions exposed through ChatGPT's authenticated frontend APIs sat inside the same security boundary. Essentially, we could perform any action on behalf of the victim's ChatGPT account.

What went wrong and what OpenAI fixed

The Preview sandbox itself worked. Code inside Preview could not directly read the ChatGPT page. The problem was that another component in the main page was willing to accept its instructions.

Three design decisions turned that mistake into a full exploit:

  • MathLive accepted a state-changing message without proving which frame sent it.
  • The message format allowed raw HTML to be supplied as keyboard-layout data.
  • That HTML could create an unsandboxed document that inherited the 
    chatgpt.com
    identity.

The YouTube response was the final workaround for CSP, not the root cause. Blocking only that endpoint would have removed one path to JavaScript while still leaving an untrusted Preview able to insert HTML into ChatGPT.

OpenAI reproduced both the JavaScript execution and the private-conversation proof. The fix was rolled out across two versions of ChatGPT's web frontend. During the rollout, CSP changes blocked part of the original chain while one version still needed an update. On October 26, OpenAI confirmed that both versions now rejected the cross-frame MathLive messages used by the report and marked the issue resolved.

The defensive lessons are fairly simple:

  • A message receiver should verify both the sender's web address and the exact window that was given permission.
  • Cross-window settings should not contain raw HTML.
  • Dynamically created documents should remain sandboxed when their content can be influenced by an untrusted frame.
  • CSP should be a backup control, not the only barrier after attacker-controlled HTML reaches the main site.

This bug was interesting because every individual feature looked reasonable. Preview was isolated. Graphs could load a math keyboard. Frames could exchange messages. CSP allowed a major media provider. Public conversations could be shared. The vulnerability appeared only when all of those features were combined in one page.
