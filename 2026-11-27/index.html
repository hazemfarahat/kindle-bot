AI made self-hosting fun again

hackernews • 885 points • by jlelse • November 26, 2026

AI made self-hosting fun again

I’ve been self-hosting things for many years. There were times when I was quite enthusiastic about it, constantly trying out new software and tinkering with my infrastructure. But at some point, that enthusiasm faded a bit.

I never really stopped self-hosting, but I became less inclined to experiment with new things. Maintaining what I already had running was enough for me.

Over the last few months, however, I’ve found myself getting increasingly excited about self-hosting again. And, somewhat ironically, AI is a big reason for that.

Less friction

Most of my infrastructure configuration lives in Git repositories, mainly in the form of Docker Compose files, scripts, and other configuration files. This makes it possible to use AI coding agents to work on my infrastructure in much the same way as they would work on any other software project.

Whenever I want to try a new service, adjust an existing configuration, or automate something, I can now do so with considerably less effort.

In the past, I would sometimes put off certain changes simply because I didn’t feel like spending an evening reading changelogs and documentation or troubleshooting issues. It’s not that I couldn’t have done these things before. But often, the amount of effort required just wasn’t worth it to me.

Now, even relatively spontaneous ideas often end up becoming reality.

Trying things I always found interesting

There are also technologies that I’ve always found interesting but never felt knowledgeable enough about to really make use of them.

NixOS is a good example. I’ve always liked the idea of declaratively configuring an entire system; I even used it on my laptop during my Linux honeymoon phase. But getting everything configured the way I wanted was quite a hurdle. With the help of AI, I’m now making much more extensive use of NixOS on my home server.

For my VPS infrastructure, I’ve also started building my own Debian-based bootc image (after thinking about it for some time).

Having my entire operating system configuration defined in a Git repository and automatically building a bootable container image from it is something I find really cool. But it’s also something I probably wouldn’t have invested enough time in to get working on my own before. Even with the help of AI, it still took me half a day when I set it up a few months ago. Without AI, it would probably have taken me at least a week.

AI makes experimenting with unfamiliar technologies much more approachable. I don’t necessarily need to understand every little detail before I can get started. I can learn about things along the way, ask questions, and gradually improve my configurations.

Automating almost everything

Something I’ve also finally managed to do is automate most of the build, update, and deployment processes for my infrastructure.

A lot of the work involved in self-hosting isn’t particularly exciting. Keeping track of new releases, updating container image tags, building images, and deploying configuration changes are all things that need to be done regularly.

I still remember what updating a service used to look like: bumping the version and adjusting the Docker Compose file on my laptop, pushing the changes to the Git remote, SSHing into the server, pulling the changes, and finally deploying them. Now, a single Git push is enough. If a push is even necessary.

With Renovate, I no longer need to actively keep track of most software updates. It creates pull requests for me, and often, all I need to do is review and merge them. I can even do that on my phone while sitting on the sofa.

Pushing changes or merging a pull request triggers the corresponding GitHub Actions or Forgejo Actions workflows, which take care of building and deploying everything.

Of course, there’s nothing particularly groundbreaking about any of this. DevOps has been around for a long time, and none of these automation tools require AI. But AI has helped me put all these pieces together in a way that I previously never quite got around to doing.

And the nice thing about automating these tasks is that the benefits extend well beyond the initial setup. I now have less tedious work to do every time I want to change something or update a service.

Back to more powerful hardware

As I’ve started hosting more things again, I’ve also moved more of my services from my VPS to my home server.

For a while, I was using an HP t640 thin client as my home server. However, with more things I wanted to run, I decided to switch back to my ASRock DeskMini A300, which had been sitting unused for some time. I’m thinking about repurposing the t640 as a “router on a stick”, though.

I even took the opportunity to upgrade its CPU from an AMD Ryzen 5 2400G to a Ryzen 5 PRO 4650G and replaced the stock AMD cooler with a quieter Noctua cooler. Noise was the main reason I switched to the t640 in the first place.

It’s nice to put some hardware I already owned back to use rather than buying an entirely new machine. I also managed to get both the CPU and the cooler for a good price through a classifieds site.

Having some additional computing power available gives me more room to experiment. And there’s something quite satisfying about having a small box at home running a growing collection of services that I use in my everyday life.

Building my own tools

But it’s not just about self-hosting existing software. AI has also changed how I approach problems for which there isn’t an existing piece of software that does exactly what I want.

Previously, I would often spend quite some time searching for a suitable open-source project, comparing different alternatives, and figuring out how to make one of them work for my particular use case.

These days, for smaller problems, building my own solution has become a much more attractive option.

I’ve recently built tools to automatically download invoices, e-papers or Linux ISOs.

I can build (or have AI build) these tools in Go, my preferred programming language, and tailor them to my exact needs.

They don’t need to support every possible use case, have an elaborate user interface, or be particularly interesting to anyone other than me. I don’t need to turn every little tool into a proper open-source project with extensive documentation and support for other people’s use cases. They just need to solve my problem.

What I particularly enjoy is how well this complements my increasingly automated infrastructure. Building a small tool has become easier, and so has building a container image for it, deploying it, and keeping it running.

The entire process, from having an idea to having something actually running on my home server, requires much less effort than it used to.

Just because I can doesn’t mean I should

Of course, there are downsides to all of this.

Every additional service is another thing that needs to be maintained. AI-generated software isn’t exempt from bugs, security issues, or technical debt. And having an AI agent make changes to my infrastructure doesn’t absolve me of the responsibility of understanding those changes.

There’s also the temptation to build or deploy things simply because I can. When implementing an idea becomes so easy, it’s perhaps more important than ever to occasionally ask myself whether it’s actually worth implementing in the first place.

On the other hand, self-hosting is a hobby for me. Not everything I do needs to be economically sensible or particularly useful. Sometimes, experimenting with something new is the whole point.

And with much of the routine maintenance automated, adding another service doesn’t necessarily mean adding as much recurring work as it used to.

A somewhat ironic development?

I find it somewhat ironic that AI, a technology largely dominated by big tech companies, is helping me become a little less dependent on those very companies in other areas of my digital life.

I’m aware that this also creates a new kind of dependency. But the software and infrastructure configurations I create with the help of AI can continue to exist independently of the AI services I used to create them. And since I mostly use open-weight models, I have a wide range of providers to choose from.

What I appreciate most, however, isn’t necessarily the time I’m saving. It’s that I’m more inclined to actually pursue the little ideas I have.

I’m trying out technologies I’ve been curious about for years. I’m automating things that I’ve wanted to automate for a long time. I’m building little tools for myself. I’m tinkering with hardware again.

AI hasn’t fundamentally changed why I like self-hosting. I still enjoy having control over my digital life, learning new things, and tinkering with technology.

It’s just removed a lot of the friction that made me lose some of my enthusiasm for it over the years.

And now, I’m having a lot of fun with it again.

A one-line prompt that makes ChatGPT leak its private opinion of you, in the worst possible format: a WhatsApp group chat where the AI models gossip about their users.

Company Culture = Slack Emoji ÷ √Headcount

hackernews • 457 points • by liamg • November 26, 2026

Companies spend an enormous amount of time and money trying to measure - and even worse, decide - culture. Engagement surveys, pulse checks, eNPS, offsites where someone from HR asks you to describe the company as a type of animal. Office posters make claims like “our culture is honesty, hard work and fun”, embarrassing the paper they’re printed on. I once applied to a company that asked candidates which kitchen utensil they most identified as, in order to decide if they were a “culture fit”. I never found out what the right answer was, but I got the job after choosing a meat cleaver, so I’m not sure what that says about either of us.

The results of these things - heavily modified by HR - are compiled into a slide deck, presented at an all-hands, and then quietly repurposed for the careers page. There, they sit beside a photo of six engineers smiling with their mouths but not their eyes at a “hackathon”, which is what the company calls overtime when it’s paid in pizza, at a rate that works out to roughly £1.40 an hour. The salary is listed as “competitive”.

There is a better way to measure culture. It is cheaper, more accurate (in my anecdotal experience), and requires no participation from anyone - which is ideal, because nobody wants to participate in these corporate séances anyway.

The Formula

Take the number of custom emoji in your Slack workspace and divide it by the square root of the number of users. That’s it.

\[C = \frac{E_{custom}}{\sqrt{U}}\]

The square root is important. Emoji are a shared resource - a single 

:this-is-fine:
can serve an entire engineering department for years - so a company of 10,000 people should not be expected to have 1,000 times more emoji than a company of 10. That would be unhinged.

We’ll call this the Emoji Coefficient.

Interpreting Your Score

  • C < 5
  • Your workspace contains the default emoji set, a company logo, and 
    :party-parrot:
    , which was added by someone in 2019 who has since left. Nobody uses it any more because it comes across as unprofessional. Communication is conducted exclusively via 👍. People are here for the salary, and they would like you to know that.
  • 5 ≤ C < 20
  • A healthy, functional organisation. There is an emoji of the CTO’s face. It is used both supportively and as a weapon, depending on the context.
  • 20 ≤ C < 50
  • Thriving. 
    :this-is-fine:
    has been used over 4,000 times in 
    #incidents
    . Management considers this a sign of engagement.
  • C ≥ 50
  • Late-stage culture. Every variant of 
    :thumbsup:
    now carries subtext. The plain default one is considered passive-aggressive. The emoji have begun to reference themselves: 
    :old-man-yells-at-old-man:
    and 
    :party-parrot-holding-a-party-parrot:
    . HR has requested a glossary, but no one person understands the memeology or the history of 
    :cowboy-spongebob-on-fire-crying:
    . They’re afraid it might mean something rude. It does.

The Participation Rate

The Emoji Coefficient alone is not enough. A healthy score can conceal a dark truth: that every single emoji was uploaded by one person, usually a backend engineer who has been there since the beginning and knows where the bodies are buried.

To account for this, we also measure the percentage of users who have uploaded at least one emoji. These are your culture leaders. Their reward is a shoutout at the all-hands (it’s customary to mispronounce their name).

If P is below 20%, things are not good. Your culture is being carried by a handful of people, and when they leave, they will take it with them. The emoji will remain, but nobody will remember what 

:fire-extinguisher-thumbsup:
was for.

Above 20%, the culture has the gene pool it needs to succeed.

Examples of Cultural Success

For reference, here are some real emoji from slackmojis.com:

:a_big_lump_with_knobs:

It's a corn on the cob.

:austin_capmetro_bus_back:

A detailed drawing of the back of one specific Austin city bus, number 2300.

:cat-roomba-matrix-fast:

The fastest in a series of cat-on-a-Roomba emoji, after "exceptionally fast", "hyper fast" and "ultra fast".

:cauliflower_surprise_veg_shock:

A startled cauliflower, one of a whole set of cauliflower emotions.

Caveats

As with any metric, the Emoji Coefficient can be gamed. A sufficiently motivated middle manager could upload 600 variations of the company logo to drive up the score ahead of their performance review, the outcome of which, unbeknownst to them, was decided in March. This should be detected and treated as what it is: a cry for help. Fortunately, it will also be immediately visible in the Participation Rate.

It is also worth monitoring the rate of change. A sudden spike in custom emoji is generally a positive sign. A sudden spike in emoji like 

:rip:
, 
:press-f:
and 
:linkedin:
usually coincides with a reorg, and should be cross-referenced with the number of users, which will shortly be going down. Interestingly, this will improve your score. Mass layoffs are, of course, yet another way in which the score can be gamed.

What If You Don’t Use Slack?

If your company uses Microsoft Teams, there is no equivalent formula. There doesn’t need to be. If you use Teams, your lack of culture is probably the least of your problems.

If you use something else, I don’t really have any advice for you. I only added this section to blast Teams.

Conclusion

Culture cannot be manufactured with values posters or mandatory fun. It emerges organically, when an engineer decides that what the team really needs is an emoji of an exceptionally surprised cauliflower.

Hard Drive pricing has gotten bad

hackernews • 450 points • by zdw • November 27, 2026

For years now, I’ve made it an annual tradition to replace one or two hard drives on Black Friday. I typically run around ten physical disks, and swapping a couple out each year means I almost never have a drive older than five years in my systems. I’ve even previously made videos about shucking disks around this time of year.

But current hardware pricing is totally screwed. So what is a lifelong self-hoster to do in times like these? With disk capacities increasing massively over the last 10 years I can now fit 20TB per slot. This means I could realistically go from ten spinners to five, and not really feel it too acutely. This will mean redoing my zpool layouts, which in turn means shuffling some data around as ZFS doesn’t like changes to the layout of existing pools. But, at these prices, I’m starting to think the hassle might be worth it.

Let’s take a look at disk prices over the past 8 years in today’s post.

The 20TB WD EasyStore I bought for $249.99 in November 2024 is, today, on sale, $549.99 this Black Friday. $300 more than 2 years ago for the same product, from the same store. It shows just how bad hard drive pricing has gotten (BestBuy 20TB listing).

Shucking is homelab speak for taking a perfectly good external drive out of its USB enclosure and sticking the SATA drive in your server. At $12.50/TB it was a fantastically affordable way to get massive amounts of storage. At $27.50/TB, absolutely not.

I dug through my receipts

In November 2018 I bought five 10TB EasyStores for $179.99 each. At the time I wrote about doing the rounds of my local Best Buys to pickup more drives on Black Friday as quantities were limited at each location (My 2018 server build log).

How we got down to three HDD makers

When the EasyStore is that bad, my first instinct is to shop around. Maybe I should try another retailer (to be clear I’m not blaming BestBuy for any of this), buy an enterprise drive, see what’s available recertified. But follow those listings back to the companies that actually make the drives and the choices narrow pretty quickly. There are three big HDD makers left. How did we end up with so few?

Maxtor bought Quantum’s hard drive business in 2001, then Seagate bought Maxtor in 2006. Hitachi combined IBM’s disk business with its own to form HGST in 2003, and Toshiba bought Fujitsu’s HDD business in 2009. Seagate bought Samsung’s HDD business in December 2011, and Western Digital bought HGST in March 2012. The FTC required WD to sell desktop-drive manufacturing assets to Toshiba to keep a third supplier in that market. That left Seagate, Western Digital and Toshiba.

The 2011 Thailand floods hit while those last two deals were underway. WD halted production at all its Thai plants, and its average hard drive selling price went from $45 in fiscal 2011 to $62 in fiscal 2012. There was also a price-fixing case further up the supply chain. NHK Spring pleaded guilty in 2019 to fixing prices for the suspension assemblies that hold the read/write heads, and the court approved a $28.5 million fine. WD’s 2012 annual report · Ninth Circuit opinion

Market collusion and cartel pricing

It’s hard to have anything positive to say in this post. The HDD manufacturing market is so consolidated nowadays - just like the memory market which is equally screwed - that it’s hard to see this as anything but mass market collusion by the HDD manufacturing cartel.

Three companies is an awfully short list when you need to buy a hard drive. And with 89% of WD’s revenue coming from its cloud business in fiscal 2026, I don’t reckon those of us buying a couple of drives for a home server have much impact here. Just like with Nvidia and Micron, consumer markets just aren’t valuable enough so we have to make do with the crumbs instead.

As of November 26, 2026, the 20TB is 2.2× what I paid for the exact same SKU in 2024. It’s also $270 more than I paid for it in May 2025. And if you’re thinking you’ll just buy a smaller one, Best Buy’s 8TB Black Friday special is $299.99. That’s $37.50/TB and fifty bucks more than my 20TB cost two years ago. Best Buy’s EasyStore listings

The jump gets sillier when you price a whole NAS. Four of those 20TB drives would have been $999.96 at my 2024 price. This week they’d be $2,199.96. That’s another $1,200 before you’ve bought a case, a board or a single stick of RAM. And the price per usable terabyte only gets worse once you set aside a drive for parity.

If I can wish for one thing in 2027, it would be for hardware pricing to return to reasonable levels. I fear it may take a lot longer than that, though. So making do with less is going to be the new normal for a while. And in that creative constraint, maybe we’ll have some fun along the way instead.

Kamil Khan Mumtaz: Pakistani architect, admired at home, almost invisible abroad

hackernews • 442 points • by dverlaeckt80 • November 26, 2026

Traditional architecture is alive and well in Lahore. A photo essay of Kamil Khan Mumtaz’s greatest works.

Some years ago, my colleague Ben Southwood sent me a handful of grainy online pictures showing some recent buildings in Pakistan. I was struck by the assurance of the designs and the quality of the materials, craftsmanship, and detailing. They seemed to be the work of one Kamil Khan Mumtaz, a Pakistani architect widely admired in his home country but almost invisible abroad. I wondered whether Mumtaz might be the world’s best architect, overlooked because global architecture magazines rarely do photoshoots in Lahore. Last year, I met his assistant Sohaib Ahmed at a conference and immediately entreated him to send Works in Progress any photographs he had of the practice’s work. With Mumtaz’s support, Sohaib prepared a portfolio for us, most of it never before seen outside Pakistan.

Kamil Khan Mumtaz was born in Calcutta in 1939 and grew up in what became Pakistan, taking British A-levels at Lahore’s elite Aitchison College. He then moved to London to study at the Architectural Association. Although he had never previously left South Asia, Mumtaz immediately felt at home in England. ‘It seemed completely familiar. I didn’t feel like a stranger at all’, he recalled in an interview with the historian Chris Moffat. ‘I think this is because of having been brought up on essentially English books, in English schools. So, the whole thing was like, “I know this place”.’ Mumtaz enjoyed his studies at the Architectural Association, which taught a standard modernist approach that Mumtaz accepted unquestioningly: ‘I just simply took it hook, line and sinker, and swallowed the whole thing.’

Mumtaz returned to Pakistan in 1966 and began practicing as an architect. At around the same time, he became a communist activist, on account of which he was twice imprisoned by the military dictatorship. He remained resolutely modernist, although he acquired special interest in adapting modernism to the local climate and locally sourced building materials. He also became involved in architectural conservation. In the late 1970s, he prepared a report on the ancient Walled City of Lahore, highlighting its enormous heritage value and arguing for its preservation. ‘Our own bureaucrats only insisted that we should pull down the whole mess anyway, and build new, modern infrastructure and high-rise buildings, et cetera’, Mumtaz remembers. The Walled City was ultimately saved, and parts of it are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Through the 1970s, Mumtaz began to question his approach. ‘I had this growing, nagging fear that I wasn’t communicating with my audience, as a visual artist or as an architect. This grew on me, almost like an obsession.’ In 1980, the Iranian architect Nader Ardalan gave him a copy of his book The Sense of Unity: The Sufi Tradition in Persian Architecture. Mumtaz initially paid no attention: ‘Flipping through it, I thought, “Oh yeah, this is traditional Iranian architecture, who cares!” I was totally uninterested.’ But eventually, frustrated by the sense that ‘I didn’t know the language of my own people’, he began to read. ‘From page one, it was mind-blowing. Absolutely. I thought, “My god, what have I been looking for? This is what I’ve been missing!” ... It shattered all of the beliefs I had, being a Marxist, a materialist, an atheist, a modernist.’

In the decades since, Mumtaz has worked to revive the older architectural traditions of Pakistan, and especially of Lahore, his home city. He has built a wide range of buildings, including homes, offices, mosques, and schools. His work has been received with warmth in Pakistan, though also with surprise. ‘I’ve found that there is a huge reservoir of desire, sympathy, and love for our traditional values, forms, architecture, and culture, altogether. But there’s a general kind of impression that these things can’t be done any more, there are no craftsmen who can do this.’

The loss of craft skills is a problem for traditional architecture everywhere. Some elements of traditional architecture have been prefabricated in factories for centuries, but even then, skill is needed for installation. Without skilled craftspeople, important details are often badly executed, making buildings look strange or clumsy. This problem also exists in Pakistan, but its later industrialization meant premodern craft traditions had not quite vanished when Mumtaz began work. Mumtaz recalled that, when searching for skilled craftsmen, ‘immediately, you come into contact with very small remnants of these families, hereditary craftsmen, and so the relationship grows.’

Mumtaz’s work has become steadily more technically sophisticated as he and his craftsmen have built up expertise. ‘Together we have been able to rediscover a number of the practices that had become lost. Certain types of brickwork, the flat dome, the muqarnas [ornamental vaults], many things like this that hadn’t been done. As they tell me, “This has not been done for 300 years!” So, it was a great experience, a voyage of discovery, so much that we learnt from each other.’

It is my great pleasure to introduce our readers to one of the most skilled and thoughtful architects working today.

Now in his mid-eighties, Mumtaz continues to build. ‘It does feel like you’ve set the foundations for an alternative approach to contemporary urban environments’, an interviewer recently suggested to him. ‘I’m just keeping my fingers crossed! No idea what will happen’, Mumtaz replied.

AI made self-hosting fun again (7 minute read)

tldr • 94 points • November 27, 2026

AI made self-hosting fun again

I’ve been self-hosting things for many years. There were times when I was quite enthusiastic about it, constantly trying out new software and tinkering with my infrastructure. But at some point, that enthusiasm faded a bit.

I never really stopped self-hosting, but I became less inclined to experiment with new things. Maintaining what I already had running was enough for me.

Over the last few months, however, I’ve found myself getting increasingly excited about self-hosting again. And, somewhat ironically, AI is a big reason for that.

Less friction

Most of my infrastructure configuration lives in Git repositories, mainly in the form of Docker Compose files, scripts, and other configuration files. This makes it possible to use AI coding agents to work on my infrastructure in much the same way as they would work on any other software project.

Whenever I want to try a new service, adjust an existing configuration, or automate something, I can now do so with considerably less effort.

In the past, I would sometimes put off certain changes simply because I didn’t feel like spending an evening reading changelogs and documentation or troubleshooting issues. It’s not that I couldn’t have done these things before. But often, the amount of effort required just wasn’t worth it to me.

Now, even relatively spontaneous ideas often end up becoming reality.

Trying things I always found interesting

There are also technologies that I’ve always found interesting but never felt knowledgeable enough about to really make use of them.

NixOS is a good example. I’ve always liked the idea of declaratively configuring an entire system; I even used it on my laptop during my Linux honeymoon phase. But getting everything configured the way I wanted was quite a hurdle. With the help of AI, I’m now making much more extensive use of NixOS on my home server.

For my VPS infrastructure, I’ve also started building my own Debian-based bootc image (after thinking about it for some time).

Having my entire operating system configuration defined in a Git repository and automatically building a bootable container image from it is something I find really cool. But it’s also something I probably wouldn’t have invested enough time in to get working on my own before. Even with the help of AI, it still took me half a day when I set it up a few months ago. Without AI, it would probably have taken me at least a week.

AI makes experimenting with unfamiliar technologies much more approachable. I don’t necessarily need to understand every little detail before I can get started. I can learn about things along the way, ask questions, and gradually improve my configurations.

Automating almost everything

Something I’ve also finally managed to do is automate most of the build, update, and deployment processes for my infrastructure.

A lot of the work involved in self-hosting isn’t particularly exciting. Keeping track of new releases, updating container image tags, building images, and deploying configuration changes are all things that need to be done regularly.

I still remember what updating a service used to look like: bumping the version and adjusting the Docker Compose file on my laptop, pushing the changes to the Git remote, SSHing into the server, pulling the changes, and finally deploying them. Now, a single Git push is enough. If a push is even necessary.

With Renovate, I no longer need to actively keep track of most software updates. It creates pull requests for me, and often, all I need to do is review and merge them. I can even do that on my phone while sitting on the sofa.

Pushing changes or merging a pull request triggers the corresponding GitHub Actions or Forgejo Actions workflows, which take care of building and deploying everything.

Of course, there’s nothing particularly groundbreaking about any of this. DevOps has been around for a long time, and none of these automation tools require AI. But AI has helped me put all these pieces together in a way that I previously never quite got around to doing.

And the nice thing about automating these tasks is that the benefits extend well beyond the initial setup. I now have less tedious work to do every time I want to change something or update a service.

Back to more powerful hardware

As I’ve started hosting more things again, I’ve also moved more of my services from my VPS to my home server.

For a while, I was using an HP t640 thin client as my home server. However, with more things I wanted to run, I decided to switch back to my ASRock DeskMini A300, which had been sitting unused for some time. I’m thinking about repurposing the t640 as a “router on a stick”, though.

I even took the opportunity to upgrade its CPU from an AMD Ryzen 5 2400G to a Ryzen 5 PRO 4650G and replaced the stock AMD cooler with a quieter Noctua cooler. Noise was the main reason I switched to the t640 in the first place.

It’s nice to put some hardware I already owned back to use rather than buying an entirely new machine. I also managed to get both the CPU and the cooler for a good price through a classifieds site.

Having some additional computing power available gives me more room to experiment. And there’s something quite satisfying about having a small box at home running a growing collection of services that I use in my everyday life.

Building my own tools

But it’s not just about self-hosting existing software. AI has also changed how I approach problems for which there isn’t an existing piece of software that does exactly what I want.

Previously, I would often spend quite some time searching for a suitable open-source project, comparing different alternatives, and figuring out how to make one of them work for my particular use case.

These days, for smaller problems, building my own solution has become a much more attractive option.

I’ve recently built tools to automatically download invoices, e-papers or Linux ISOs.

I can build (or have AI build) these tools in Go, my preferred programming language, and tailor them to my exact needs.

They don’t need to support every possible use case, have an elaborate user interface, or be particularly interesting to anyone other than me. I don’t need to turn every little tool into a proper open-source project with extensive documentation and support for other people’s use cases. They just need to solve my problem.

What I particularly enjoy is how well this complements my increasingly automated infrastructure. Building a small tool has become easier, and so has building a container image for it, deploying it, and keeping it running.

The entire process, from having an idea to having something actually running on my home server, requires much less effort than it used to.

Just because I can doesn’t mean I should

Of course, there are downsides to all of this.

Every additional service is another thing that needs to be maintained. AI-generated software isn’t exempt from bugs, security issues, or technical debt. And having an AI agent make changes to my infrastructure doesn’t absolve me of the responsibility of understanding those changes.

There’s also the temptation to build or deploy things simply because I can. When implementing an idea becomes so easy, it’s perhaps more important than ever to occasionally ask myself whether it’s actually worth implementing in the first place.

On the other hand, self-hosting is a hobby for me. Not everything I do needs to be economically sensible or particularly useful. Sometimes, experimenting with something new is the whole point.

And with much of the routine maintenance automated, adding another service doesn’t necessarily mean adding as much recurring work as it used to.

A somewhat ironic development?

I find it somewhat ironic that AI, a technology largely dominated by big tech companies, is helping me become a little less dependent on those very companies in other areas of my digital life.

I’m aware that this also creates a new kind of dependency. But the software and infrastructure configurations I create with the help of AI can continue to exist independently of the AI services I used to create them. And since I mostly use open-weight models, I have a wide range of providers to choose from.

What I appreciate most, however, isn’t necessarily the time I’m saving. It’s that I’m more inclined to actually pursue the little ideas I have.

I’m trying out technologies I’ve been curious about for years. I’m automating things that I’ve wanted to automate for a long time. I’m building little tools for myself. I’m tinkering with hardware again.

AI hasn’t fundamentally changed why I like self-hosting. I still enjoy having control over my digital life, learning new things, and tinkering with technology.

It’s just removed a lot of the friction that made me lose some of my enthusiasm for it over the years.

And now, I’m having a lot of fun with it again.

Can small IT teams afford the shift to conversational helpdesks?

If you ask an employee to pick between “Incident, “Service Request’ and “Access Provisioning” on a web form, you’re very likely to lose them to something else entirely i.e., a Slack DM.

Whether these promises truly make conversational support viable for lean IT teams, however, remains an open question.

What happens behind the chat bubble

For an employee who types “Hi, I need access to the finance drive” into Teams, it feels like just a chat with a colleague. In the background, the conversational desk takes that natural language and turns it into a proper IT record, with no forms to complete.

The core IT discipline is unchanged: an SLA countdown timer will be triggered automatically, priority tags will be applied based on business impact, and every action will go into the central audit trail.

The approaches are different. Flow is all about one-click capture. If a technician resolves something in a chat thread, that entire conversation gets captured as an automation. No knowledge base article is needed.  

Rivals such as Siit build the same idea around a triage agent that checks the knowledge base first, then collects the required fields conversationally before opening a request. Either way, the goal is the same: fewer forms, more resolved tickets.

Ditching the portal only works if governance stays intact. Even a 100-person company has to pass security audits and track software spend. If an employee asks for a paid license or access to a restricted folder, a compliant conversational desk enforces policy by sending an Approve or Decline card to their manager’s chat window and logs a timestamped audit trail before provisioning access.

Helpdesks also have to step in when AI fails. They can’t leave users in a bot loop or fire vague “ticket escalated” alerts at a busy sysadmin. Chat-based platforms hand unresolved threads to a technician in the same chat, carrying over the full transcript and diagnostic steps—so employees don’t repeat themselves and techs resolve faster.

Siit prices per admin seat — $23 a month for chat-based request management without autonomous agents, rising to $89 for the Pro tier with AI triage. Against flat per-seat helpdesk plans, high-volume chatter can turn software billing into an expensive surprise.

Workflow sprawl is another risk. Letting any employee turn a solved chat thread into a one-click automation invites duplicate, unvetted bots across departments. Without strict admin approval, conversational tools can recreate the exact tangle they were meant to fix.

The upside is real, on paper. IDC’s 2026 research estimates AI tools can save workers more than 40% of a typical workday, rising to 45% for IT staff as routine tasks are automated. Much of that data comes from large organizations, but the economics scale down — a five-person team that shaves routine requests still buys back hours it doesn’t have.

The warnings are just as loud. Adoption is fragile, too. A September 2026 Gartner survey of 3,566 customers found only 27% would try a chatbot again after a single bad experience. Early hallucinations and failed workflows erode trust permanently, even after the bot improves.

Rules to remember before you ditch the portal

Taking support into Slack or Teams doesn’t mean routing every routine ping through a metered AI bot. If your platform charges per conversation, imagine what high-volume password resets and FAQ lookups will do to the budget.

A balanced approach: keep predictable requests in flat-rate self-service portals and structured queues, and reserve metered chat workflows for multi-step tasks — new-hire app provisioning, urgent contractor offboarding — where saving 20 minutes of a technician’s time justifies the cost. Restrict who can publish one-click automations so rogue workflows never go live unreviewed, and set monthly spending alerts for conversation credits.
