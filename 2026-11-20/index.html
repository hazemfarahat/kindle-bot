Pancreatic cancer halted by virus injection in three patients

hackernews • 818 points • by cybermango • November 19, 2026

A virus has stopped pancreatic cancer in its tracks in three people in a clinical trial in the US. Further evaluation is needed in larger trials, but the early results are encouraging, especially since only small doses of the virus were administered for initial safety testing.

“We only injected one-tenth of the dose we are eventually aiming at, so the efficacy is better than I expected, especially as this is pancreatic cancer,” says Masato Yamamoto at the University of Minnesota, who led the development of the viral treatment.

Pancreatic cancer is notorious for being the deadliest type of the condition. One reason is that symptoms tend to appear late, when the cancer has already spread and cannot be surgically removed. Once diagnosed, people usually live only around three to six months.

Another reason is that pancreatic tumours have tough, fibrous exteriors that block chemotherapy drugs from getting in. “They’re hard as hockey pucks,” says Yamamoto. Immunotherapies that boost immune activity against cancer are also ineffective, because pancreatic tumours can hide from the immune system.

The first patient in the trial, who had a pancreatic tumour 7 centimetres across, was administered the treatment a year ago, and the other two have been since then. At the time, their tumours hadn’t spread beyond the pancreas. Since being treated, their tumours haven’t grown any further. “They are all still alive and have clinically stable disease,” says Yamamoto, who presented the results at the annual meeting of the American Society of Gene and Cell Therapy in Boston, Massachusetts, earlier this month. Another 15 patients will now be given higher doses to find the optimum level.

“I think this is an interesting early signal, but as a pancreatic surgeon, I think it’s important to keep perspective,” says Kai Brown at Royal North Shore Hospital in Sydney. “The history of oncology is littered with promising early signals that vanished by the time rigorous phase III [late-stage] testing was done, so I think these preliminary conference results should probably just be seen as hypothesis-generating at this stage.” He also notes that the trial so far hasn’t included a control group, so it is hard to know whether the cancer-killing virus works better than other treatments or none at all.

The virus is an adenovirus that has been genetically engineered to replicate only inside tumours and not healthy tissue. Its replication is specifically activated by an enzyme called cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), which is present at much higher levels in cancer cells than in normal ones. After cancer cells become infected with the virus, they burst open and die, releasing more virus that can infect neighbouring cancer cells.

In the trial, the virus was injected directly into the patients’ tumours through a thin tube that was guided down a patient’s throat to their pancreas. The tube had an ultrasound probe on the end to allow visualisation of the tumours.

The reason the tumours have stopped growing but haven’t become any smaller may be due to the low treatment dose, says Yamamoto. He thinks they may start to shrink as the virus has more time to replicate.

As the tumour cells start to break apart and leak their contents, the immune system may also be able to recognise and fight the cancer, says Yamamoto. “The patient’s immune system can realise something is wrong, and then go and attack the tumour,” he says. The hope is that the immune system will also recognise and destroy any tumour cells that have spread to other parts of the body. If so, the treatment could be effective against metastatic pancreatic cancer.

In the 1950s, for instance, women with cervical cancer were injected with an unmodified adenovirus in a clinical trial, with partial success. However, it became clear that adenoviruses must be engineered to make them selectively target cancer cells, for safety and efficacy reasons.

Claude solves two open problems in the physics of black holes

Claude has solved two open problems in theoretical physics. Both concern black holes and the search for a theory of quantum gravity. In the first, it completed a calculation that physicists set up more than 20 years ago, but which had never been solved. Claude’s result showed that a sudden transition associated with the formation of black holes persists even where spacetime no longer has a definite shape. In the second, Claude found the exact limit of the simplest version of a technique used to test candidate theories of quantum gravity. This post explains both findings, first in plain terms and then in more technical detail. The preprints are available here and here.

Introduction

Modern physics rests on two frameworks: general relativity and quantum mechanics. General relativity describes very large things, like stars; quantum mechanics describes very small things, such as atoms. Both are astonishingly good at predicting how the universe behaves, but they’re mathematically incompatible. Finding a theory that unites them—“quantum gravity”—is one of the field’s biggest goals. Black holes are a natural testing ground, because they’re one of the few areas where both relativity and quantum mechanics matter—they pack immense mass into a very small space.

One of the most useful tools for studying quantum gravity is called holographic duality. The real universe has three dimensions of space and one of time, which relativity combines into four-dimensional spacetime. But physicists studying quantum gravity often work with simpler model universes that they can understand in more detail.

The basic idea of holographic duality is that some of these theoretical universes can be described in two ways: as a complete universe with gravity and spacetime, and in quantum terms, with no gravity at all, “written” on the universe’s outer boundary. Just as a real hologram encodes a 3D image on a 2D plane, the quantum model encodes all the information of the universe in one dimension fewer (a universe with four dimensions of space, for instance, can be fully described by a quantum theory with three). This lets physicists turn hard questions about gravity into questions about quantum physics, which are sometimes easier to answer.

The first problem Claude solved concerns what happens when you heat up one of these theoretical universes. At low temperatures, the universe fills up with a gas of particles. Above a certain temperature, a black hole forms instead. In 1983, Stephen Hawking and Don Page showed that this switch is abrupt: it’s a phase transition, similar to how water suddenly boils at 100°C. But Hawking and Page’s calculation relies on general relativity, which holds only when the universe is vast, compared with the tiny scale at which that description breaks down. As the theoretical universe shrinks toward that smaller scale, spacetime loses any definite shape and becomes blurry, and nobody knows how to do the calculation on the gravity side of the duality. On the quantum side, though, the same situation can be calculated, at least in principle.

In 1999, physicists found that there was a phase transition in that regime as well. But they didn’t know whether it was abrupt, like Hawking and Page’s, or smooth. In 2003, a group of physicists set out the calculation that would settle the question, but it was extremely complex and was never completed. Claude did it by running a small research group of copies of itself, in which it worked out almost every piece of the calculation independently and checked its math against known results. It showed that the phase transition is abrupt. The same sudden switch appears at both extremes, suggesting that black hole formation isn’t just a feature of relativity but is actually built into the underlying quantum theory.

The second problem concerns a simplified theoretical universe with two dimensions of space instead of three. This universe is straightforward enough to study in detail, but still has black holes and other mysteries similar to those of our own universe. Even in this simpler universe, physicists don’t know how to create a full theory of quantum gravity, so they approach it from the other direction: rather than building a single, correct theory, they try to rule out wrong ones by showing that they are internally inconsistent. This method is called bootstrapping.

Since 2009, physicists have used a version of this method to set a ceiling on how heavy the lightest black hole in such a universe could be. Any theory that breaks the ceiling is ruled out, and each lowering of the ceiling narrows the search. Results from numerical calculations hinted at where the best possible ceiling lay, but they couldn’t prove it, and the best proven ceiling was well short of what the numerical results suggested.

Claude Fable 5.2 noticed a pattern in the numerical results that physicists had missed and borrowed a method from elsewhere in math and physics to calculate the precise ceiling for the simplest version of this technique and prove that it could not be lowered. It doesn’t identify the correct theory, but it may point physicists in the right direction.

Neither result describes our own universe directly, and neither has practical applications, but both contribute to a long-running theoretical effort to understand how quantum mechanics and relativity fit together.

The Hawking-Page transition

To understand the first problem, we need to start with something much more familiar: a walk through New York City.

Imagine that you’re heading from Penn Station to the High Line entrance on West 30th Street and 10th Avenue, and there are two equally good routes. You could walk west along West 33rd Street and turn south, or head south first and then walk west along West 30th Street. Either way, you have to pick one. In the quantum world, though, that’s not true: a particle can take both routes simultaneously.

This is most clearly demonstrated in the famous double-slit experiment. In this test, you fire particles, one at a time, at a barrier cut with two narrow slits, and record where, on a screen behind it, each one lands. Each particle lands at a particular point on the screen, as a particle should. But as you fire more of them, they build up a pattern of stripes that would only be possible if each one had passed through both slits, like a wave.

To predict what a quantum particle will do more generally, physicists have to add up every path it could possibly take, known as its possible histories. Not every history counts equally; some contribute far more to the sum than others. It’s an extravagant and mysterious way to describe how a single particle behaves, but it’s also one of the most successful ideas in physics.

This approach, known as the “sum over histories,” is well established for particles and anything made of them. But what about gravity? Einstein showed that gravity is a consequence of curved spacetime: objects with mass bend space and time around them, and we feel that bending as gravity. In a quantum theory of gravity, therefore, what gets summed over is the shape of spacetime itself, rather than just the paths of particles moving through it. To make predictions in a quantum theory of gravity, we would have to add up every shape that the universe could possibly take, and nobody yet knows how to do that. Much of modern quantum gravity research is an attempt to work it out.

Hawking and Page found a case where this sum is simple enough to handle but still interesting. To explain Hawking and Page’s work, we need to imagine a different kind of universe, called anti-de Sitter spacetime. Imagine you’re standing in a giant bowl. No matter how hard you throw an object, it will always roll back to the place you threw it from.

Our universe isn’t like that. The difference comes down to a quantity called the cosmological constant, which describes the energy of empty space. In our universe, this energy is positive, which makes space expand ever faster. In anti-de Sitter spacetime, the energy is negative, so everything is pulled back to where it started. Hawking and Page chose this universe for their calculation because of how it behaves when it heats up: the bowl keeps heat in so it can stay at a steady temperature.

At any given temperature, two histories matter far more than all the others. Here, a “history” means an entire version of the universe: a particular shape of spacetime, along with everything in it. In one, the bowl is filled with an ordinary gas of particles—including gravitons, the particles believed to carry gravity. In the other, it contains a black hole. Whichever contributes more to the sum is, in effect, what we would observe—it “wins.” At low temperatures, the gas history wins, and the universe is full of gas; at high temperatures, the black-hole history wins, and the universe contains a black hole. At one particular temperature—which physicists now call the Hawking-Page temperature—the universe switches abruptly from one to the other. This is the phase transition.

It’s a rare case where we can see two very different shapes of spacetime competing in the sum over histories, and can say which one wins.

This is where the holographic principle enters the story. In the 1970s, Jacob Bekenstein and Hawking found that the amount of information a black hole can hold is proportional to its surface area, not to its volume. That’s very different from ordinary matter, where every bit of volume holds roughly the same amount of information.

In the early 1990s, Gerard ’t Hooft and Leonard Susskind proposed that this idea applies to gravity in general—that everything inside a region of space can be fully described by the information on its boundary, just as a flat hologram can display a three-dimensional image. In 1997, Juan Maldacena formalized the idea further. Gravity inside the anti-de Sitter bowl, he argued, is equivalent to an ordinary quantum theory of particles, with no gravity at all, existing on the rim of the bowl (the universe’s outer boundary). These are the gravity side and the quantum side of the duality we described in the introduction—they’re two languages for describing the same underlying reality.

In the case Claude studied, the quantum theory on the rim is called N=4 super Yang-Mills theory. It’s related to the theories that describe how particles interact in the real universe, but it has extra symmetries that make it much easier for physicists to work with. (Claude has worked with this theory before, calculating how particles interact within it to a new level of precision.) Since the gravity side predicts two histories competing with each other, the obvious question is: does N=4 super Yang-Mills theory (the quantum side) show the same thing?

For now, physicists can analyze this theory in detail only when its particles barely interact with each other, a condition known as weak coupling. This is the opposite end of the spectrum from where Hawking and Page’s gravitational calculation works. Weak coupling on the quantum side corresponds to the small, blurry universe on the gravity side. So there was no guarantee that any of Hawking and Page’s findings would hold there.

Remarkably, they do. Bo Sundborg in 1999, and after him Ofer Aharony, Joseph Marsano, Shiraz Minwalla, Kyriakos Papadodimas, and Mark Van Raamsdonk, showed that even at weak coupling, the theory on the rim goes through a transition as it heats up. This is its counterpart of the switch from gas to black hole inside the bowl. But it was still unknown whether this transition is abrupt, like Hawking and Page’s, or smooth and gradual.

When particles barely interact, physicists have to calculate how they behave one step at a time. They start with a simplified version of the theory in which particles don’t interact at all. Then they add a series of corrections, each more precise and harder to compute than the last. Physicists call these steps loops. In 2003, Aharony and his colleagues realized that whether the phase transition was an abrupt jump, as Hawking and Page predicted, or gradual, hinged on a single number that appeared only three loops in. Computing that number involves many thousands of terms, known as diagrams—far too many to work through by hand. In 2005, the same group completed the calculation for a simpler cousin of the theory and found an abrupt transition. But the version for N=4 super Yang-Mills, more than 10 times larger, was never completed.

The scale of the computational problem, while daunting, wasn’t the only obstacle to completing the calculation. There was a conceptual issue, as well: naively adding up all the parts of the calculation gives an infinite answer, and those infinities have to be tamed carefully to extract a meaningful result.

To solve the problem, Claude ran a small research group of agents, all copies of itself. Almost every piece of the calculation was done twice, by agents acting independently. For extra consistency checks, parts of the calculation could also be compared to previous results in the literature.

The result Claude found is an abrupt jump as the temperature rises, exactly analogous to what Hawking and Page had found using the sum over spacetime histories. The emerging picture is very simple: the transition is abrupt at both weak and strong coupling—the blurry and the smooth ends, in the gravity description. The most natural possibility is that it stays abrupt everywhere in between.

This is a beautiful outcome. It shows that a calculation using the sum over histories in gravity qualitatively matches the behavior of an ordinary quantum theory with no gravity at all.

Many open questions remain. The abrupt transition Claude found is very much like the one we see whenever we boil water. One interesting feature of abrupt transitions is that the losing side of the competition can survive for a while: for instance, water can be superheated above 100°C without boiling, as you could see for yourself if you were to put a cup of extremely pure water in the microwave. (But please don’t do this; it’s dangerous, and Anthropic would rather not be responsible for an epidemic of scalded readers.)

Something similar happens here. Above the Hawking-Page temperature, the hot gas of gravitons and other particles can survive for a long time in a superheated state. But at a higher temperature it can no longer survive: this is another transition, the Hagedorn transition, which is a characteristic signature of string theory. On the gravity side, that transition has many interesting properties of its own, and it would be valuable to check in detail whether they also appear in N=4 super Yang-Mills theory.

Another open question concerns the other possible histories of spacetime. Hawking and Page considered three: one in which their model universe is filled with gas, one that contains a large black hole, and one containing a smaller black hole that never comes to dominate the sum over histories. In principle, there are many more possible histories. As far as anyone knows, none of them ever dominates. But given certain changes to the starting conditions—if the universe is set spinning, for instance, or given a fixed amount of energy or charge—then some of these histories do take over. Working out exactly which ones win, and when, is an interesting problem for the future.

The modular bootstrap

As we saw earlier, one useful model universe has just two dimensions of space and one of time: it’s called “3D gravity” because it describes gravity in three spacetime dimensions. According to the principle of holographic duality, 3D gravity inside the anti-de Sitter bowl is equivalent to an ordinary quantum theory living on the bowl’s rim.

As a model, 3D gravity is much simpler than the real thing, but it still allows for black holes and many of the same mysteries. It provides a useful laboratory for studying the sum over spacetime histories. It’s also a good place to test the idea that spacetime isn’t a basic ingredient of the universe, but emerges from something deeper. In general relativity, black holes look smooth. But in quantum gravity, if you zoom in to the smallest scales—the Planck length, around 10-35 meters, where quantum effects on gravity become impossible to ignore—black holes are made of an enormous number of tiny, individual building blocks. Physicists believe the same is true in our own, four-dimensional universe, but the 3D version is much easier to analyze with holographic duality.

Claude’s results build on a method called the bootstrap, a general strategy for solving quantum theories. Counterintuitively, the strategy doesn’t try to solve a theory directly. For 3D gravity, that isn’t even possible, because nobody knows how to define the theory precisely. Instead, the bootstrap rules theories out by showing that they contradict themselves. Physicists scan over all possible quantum theories and try to eliminate them one by one.

Remarkably, in many cases this method makes it possible to pinpoint a single quantum theory and calculate precisely how it behaves. The requirements of relativity and quantum mechanics are so demanding, and so delicately balanced, that together they rule out every alternative. In 2012, the bootstrap was used to solve a quantum field theory called the 3D critical Ising model, a simple model of magnetism that also describes water at the point where liquid and vapor become indistinguishable. Since then, it has been applied to many different quantum systems.

In 2009, Simeon Hellerman introduced a technique called the modular bootstrap, which applies the bootstrap method to 3D gravity. Its goal is to rule out inconsistent theories of quantum gravity in three spacetime dimensions. By combining it with other bootstrap methods, physicists hope to eventually eliminate every inconsistent theory. Whatever is left must be consistent. There could be no consistent theories, or just one, or whole families of them. Any of these outcomes would be interesting, because it would help us understand what spacetime looks like at the Planck scale in this simplified universe, and give us hints about the Planck scale in our own.

Here’s how the modular bootstrap works. 3D gravity in anti-de Sitter spacetime has a quantity called the central charge (c), which roughly counts how many microscopic building blocks the universe is made of. When general relativity holds, c is very large, because large black holes contain an enormous number of building blocks. A second quantity, M₁, is the mass of the lightest black hole. To run the bootstrap, physicists scan over every possible theory, with every possible value of c and M₁, and look for inconsistencies.

To understand how that search works, we need to go back to the sum over spacetime histories. In 3D gravity, that means adding up different 3D shapes of spacetime. They can be simple, like a ball, or more complicated, like the donut and two-holed donut in the figure below. The spacetime is the inside of the shape—the dough inside the donut. Its surface, like the rim of the bowl in the earlier section, is where the quantum theory is “written,” in one fewer dimension.

The modular bootstrap starts with a spacetime shaped like an ordinary, one-holed donut, or torus. There are two different ways to interpret this shape, depending on which direction we call “time” and which we call “space.” In the first, the long direction around the hole is time and the other, shorter loop, around the tube, is space. In the second, they’re swapped: the long loop is space and the shorter loop is time.

In calculations like these, the length of the time loop sets the temperature: the shorter the loop, the hotter the universe. So swapping the two loops trades a cold universe for a hot one. The two interpretations correspond to the two competing histories that Hawking and Page studied. In 3D gravity there are no gravitons, but there is a transition similar to the one that Claude analyzed in N=4 super Yang-Mills theory.

Now we come to the crucial step. In relativity, space and time are on an equal footing. So it can’t matter which loop we label space and which we label time. As far as relativity is concerned, the two interpretations are equivalent, and the physics must be the same for every possible experiment.

Quantum mechanics, however, treats time differently from space. To calculate anything, you pick a direction to call time and track how the system evolves along it. So, when we calculate observable quantities in quantum gravity, the two interpretations of the donut lead to calculations that look very different. But in relativity, it doesn’t matter which loop is called time, so the two calculations have to give exactly the same answer.

If we knew the theory of 3D quantum gravity, we could do both calculations and confirm that they match. We don’t, but with the bootstrap method, that isn’t a problem. Physicists scan over the possible values of c (the number of building blocks) and M₁ (the mass of the lightest black hole). For each pair of values, they try to prove that the theory would be inconsistent—that the two calculations on the donut couldn’t possibly give the same answer. This involves searching for a special mathematical object called a bootstrap functional. If physicists find one, then the theory is inconsistent, and those values are ruled out.

The hope, supported by the bootstrap’s successes elsewhere, is that combining this approach with other bootstrap methods will eventually rule out most possible theories of 3D quantum gravity. The theories left standing would be the only ones consistent with both gravity and quantum mechanics. Physicists could then use them to explore some of the biggest questions in quantum gravity: is there only one theory? Does it rely on string theory, or are there alternatives? What are black holes made of at the microscopic level, and how do we sum over spacetime histories?

This strategy can work in principle, but it’s very difficult in practice, because bootstrap functionals are hard to find. What matters for quantum gravity is what happens when c is large—but the larger c gets, the harder the search becomes. In the original paper on the modular bootstrap, Hellerman proved that in any consistent theory of 3D gravity, M₁ can be no larger than c/6, up to small corrections. Theories with a heavier lightest black hole can’t possibly be consistent with both gravity and quantum mechanics.

The goal of the modular bootstrap is to push this ceiling as low as possible, pinning down how heavy the lightest black hole can be. Physicists write the ceiling as M₁ ≤ c/Λ. A lower ceiling means a larger number Λ. The results below, including Claude’s, concern the simplest and most-studied version of the method, which ignores how the black holes spin.

Over the past 15 years, physicists have worked to lower the ceiling and pin down the theory as tightly as possible. There are two ways to do this. One is analytical—building a bootstrap functional by hand, with pen-and-paper mathematics. The other is numerical—using computers to search for functionals and optimize them. The results of these numerical calculations suggested that the modular bootstrap could push the ceiling down to about c/9.1. But these calculations get harder as c grows, so they couldn’t settle the question. On the analytical side, Tom Hartman, Dalimil Mazáč, and Leonardo Rastelli proved a ceiling of c/8.5, but because this was higher than the numerical results had suggested, this clearly wasn’t the best the modular bootstrap could do. Since then, just how tightly the modular bootstrap can constrain quantum gravity has remained an open question.

Claude set about solving the problem by running numerical experiments and reproducing results from the literature. In doing so, it noticed a pattern that physicists had so far missed, involving the way the numerical ceiling shifted slightly as c grew. Based on that pattern, Claude developed a hypothesis about the behavior of the bootstrap functional, using a mathematical tool called a spectral curve. Spectral curves are well known in other areas of mathematics and physics, but had never been applied to this problem. Claude found the spectral curve that governs the modular bootstrap at large values of c. It then used it to prove that the lightest black hole in 3D quantum gravity can be no heavier than c/Λ, where Λ is given by an exact formula and is approximately 9.1266. It also proved that this ceiling is optimal: it’s the lowest this version of the modular bootstrap can ever go. Claude delivered the solution across four research notes, which it later merged into a single paper.

This is one of the first times the bootstrap has been used to derive an exact result in the regime of quantum gravity where black holes matter. It also shows that this version of the method can’t, on its own, push the ceiling down to c/12, the mass at which black holes first appear; that will take other ingredients. Although it doesn’t pin down the theory of quantum gravity in three dimensions by itself, it provides a starting point and helps to narrow the search.

Conclusion

These two results show Claude doing two very different kinds of research. The first is a known calculation that physicists had set aside for two decades, because the work was too long and error-prone to complete by hand. The second required a new idea: spotting a pattern others had missed and bringing in a mathematical tool from another area of physics. Both were carried out largely autonomously. Neither result settles the question of quantum gravity, or even comes close to doing so, but each pushes physics forward in a longstanding area of research.

Abstract

In our previous post, Redwood, we showed that given an architecture, our AI systems could implement, verify, and deploy an inference accelerator in record time. However, similar to other scientific domains, we believe that AI systems, given the correct freedom and guardrails, can co-design software and hardware together, making optimizations across the stack and opening a design space so large it was previously inaccessible. We set out to answer the question: “Could our AI systems & tools lead to a novel hardware discovery that radically outperforms human-made designs?”

Today, we're introducing Frostbite, a general-purpose CPU with a new instruction set built from the ground up. Frostbite exploits the properties of interaction nets, namely that every step is local, every dependency is explicit, and every value has exactly one owner, to reach a step-function gain in CPU performance that has long stagnated. On a set of 25 initially chosen workloads, including 15 held out until the final evaluation, we achieved a 4.59x performance-per-watt multiple when compared to a modern, silicon-proven superscalar (dual-issue, in-order) CPU core.

Our AI systems autonomously iterated over the compiler, ISA, architecture, microarchitecture, RTL, verification, and final GDS. When we took a closer look at the discovered architectural primitives, we were surprised to find novel hardware implementations and runtime features that would have otherwise been difficult to architect. In spite of stagnation in CPU core improvement, we were able to take a workload concept all the way to a final GDS, and prove a step-function increase in perf/W and perf/area. We believe that as AI systems become more capable, every class of chip we know today, from datacenter-grade CPUs, to AI accelerators, to networking switches and NICs, will go through similar fundamental architectural shifts.

Introduction to interaction nets

Mainstream programming languages, when compiled for general-purpose CPUs, hide the dependencies between the parts of a program. Any piece of code may read or write any variable through a shared, mutable state, and so modern CPU hardware assumes the worst case and rediscovers, at runtime, which instructions actually depend on each other. Moreover, single-core CPU performance has largely stalled since the early 2000s, pushing people towards large multicore CPUs such as Intel Xeon, AMD Zen, and NVIDIA Vera. This forces CPUs to maintain the illusion that every core sees the same memory, and keeping that illusion consistent and performant requires deep cache hierarchies and coherence protocols that cost area and power, and scale poorly.

Interaction nets1 take a different approach. A program is a graph of nodes, and computation happens by rewriting pairs of nodes that are connected through their ports. Every rewrite only touches a small, local part of the graph. Because each node has exactly one principal port, no two of these active pairs ever overlap, so they can be rewritten in any order, or all at once, and the result is always the same. Interaction nets are also linear: every value is used exactly once, so there is no shared mutable state for the hardware to guard. Languages such as Vine2compile ordinary-looking programs directly to interaction nets. For an excellent introduction to interaction nets and why they suit hardware, we highly recommend Tendrils' “On Interaction Nets.” 3

Fig. 1 shows the difference on a small search problem, placing five queens on a five-by-five board. The sequential program visits one candidate at a time. The interaction net exposes all of its independent work: on average, about twelve interactions are ready to run at every step. Nothing about the program was rewritten to get this parallelism; the dependencies were simply never thrown away.

In theory, this should have two large advantages. The first is multithreading for free: rather than varying levels of coarse and fine-grained parallelism, parallelism comes straight from the program. No threads, locks, or race conditions, and none of the out-of-order machinery a conventional core uses to dig it back out. The second is that, since every value has one owner and every step is local, data can live in small local memories next to where it is used. That removes much of the area and power a conventional chip spends on large caching subsystems, and much of the system-level complexity of keeping those caches coherent.

Process

Frostbite was designed with zero architectural priors. We followed the process below, riding multiple agent swarms along the way.

Architecture exploration and selection

To begin architectural exploration, we had to establish the set of workloads Frostbite should do exceedingly well in. We decided to look at different ways a program could expose its work: many independent pieces at once, rounds of parallel work, long sequential chains, data-dependent branches, and recursion. Under each category, we created a search set and a held-out eval set to evaluate the final Frostbite on, ensuring the design generalized to programs it was never tuned on. The kernels for FFT, radixsort, and LU follow the implementation in the SPLASH-2 benchmark, although 64 times smaller, because at regular sizes they are not suitable for exploratory work.

Later, we added five more practical workloads to the held-out set: a Kalman filter, PCA, finite element analysis, an LU solve, and LZ4 decompression.

Agents synthesized research papers and existing benchmarks, and built Python models to trade off high-level architectural ideas against each other, before narrowing to a single architectural concept. The output of this exploration was a 141-page PDF covering the proposed programming model, the runtime, and the high-level blocks of the chip. This was nowhere near the final architecture, but rather a starting point where all the prior findings, high-level requirements, constraints, experiments, and runtime traits were described. Once the first architectural iteration completed (first RTL generation), this was deleted, since many of the numbers and estimates were well off what was physically possible.

Formal contracts and system modeling

Once the architecture candidate was selected, our system moved on to building the full software model of the chip. Building software models is essential when exploring an architectural space, as they allow fast iteration between the workloads, the ISA, and the abstracted view of the hardware. Whether we build a CPU, GPU, NPU, or networking switch, mastering this iteration loop allows the fastest exploration of the design space within a finite time. To do this repeatedly, we designed a new in-house specification language, ASL, and wrote Frostbite's system model “Arcmodel” in it. ASL supports machine-checkable translations and refinements between RTL, system models, UVM, SVA, and more, all against a formally backed specification. Its power and performance estimates are accurate to within 1–5%, it runs roughly 200x faster than RTL simulation, and it is formally verifiable through “contracts.”

In every silicon program to date, human architects have written many English PDFs before handing off to an implementation team and a verification team. However, natural language cannot capture every detail an implementation needs precisely and unambiguously, and it cannot guarantee that every agent hallucination is caught while the ASIC is being designed and verified. Natural language was never built for this, and in a world where AI writes the specification, we opt for a formal, machine-checkable specification language instead.

ASL can formally describe both high-level, protocol-like behavior, such as out-of-order handling, CSR programming, memory ordering, and coherency, and low-level details, such as pipelines, arbitration mechanisms, and compute transfer functions. This lets the model leave the specification open-ended where that is useful and constrain it exactly where a precise behavior is required. ASL also translates natively to Lean,4 which we use for all theorem proving when checking AI outputs across RTL, UVM, C, SVA, and more. We will discuss ASL in more detail in a future post.

Throughout the entire development of Frostbite, our AI iterates on the Frostbite ISA, Arcmodel, Design, and Verification tightly together, maintaining the ASL Contract as the source of truth. We've found that having a single, provable source of truth prevents a large class of bugs, silly mistakes, and reward-hacking instances when deploying large agent swarms. Ultimately, this common language allows the models to work across the stack nimbly, reflecting the changes propagated from the workload down to the final hardware. By the end, our Frostbite ASL lowered down to 800k+ lines of Lean.

RTL and alignment

Because ASL is a formally backed specification, designing and verifying the RTL derived from it is the fastest part of the entire Frostbite process. Arcmodel, however, is not physically aware, so its power and performance estimates at first diverge from the true numbers of the timing-converged, synthesis-clean RTL, in the worst case by almost 250%.

Before continuing the ASL autoresearch, one of our systems recalibrates Arcmodel against the RTL. This is a fully autonomous pipeline. It first identifies the power hotspots in the RTL and traces them down to key counters in the formal specification, such as SRAM reads and writes, operation counts, flits sent and received, credit returns, and frame misses. The model may then write any scalar Python function over those counters to predict power, and it repeats this process until the RTL and Arcmodel agree to within 5%.

We find this style of non-gradient learning extremely powerful. Once this first physical alignment has been applied, later iterations are much faster. These are, of course, still pre-silicon power numbers, and over multiple tapeout loops, ASL models can be correlated to true post-silicon, production power numbers.

By aligning the Arcmodel and RTL so closely, the continued swarms were able to explore and exploit much faster. The full Arcmodel chip simulations are much faster than the RTL (660x faster), which led to many cross-stack optimizations. For instance, our AI systems discovered very fine-grained clock gating tricks, and adjusted the software runtime accordingly to accommodate them in a matter of hours. Such a cross-stack optimization is near-impossible to execute on in a traditional ASIC flow.

Contracts, Arcmodel, RTL, and power, iterated together

Once Arcmodel and the RTL agreed, the original Python models and the architecture PDF were deleted, and the ASL contracts became the only specification of the chip. From then on, contracts, Arcmodel, RTL, and power moved forward together. Swarms of agents proposed architecture revisions in parallel worktrees; each change was checked against the contracts, modeled in Arcmodel, implemented in RTL, and measured for power, and then either kept or dropped. Across four searches and a final round of ablations, the swarm tried 58 ideas and kept 37 of them, from clock gates on a running tile to smaller L3 tables and one shared expander.

Architectural novelty

The architecture in full detail would warrant its own post. Instead, we choose to focus here on the features that we considered either novel or creative and highlight the capability of our systems to push the frontiers of hardware design.

Programs for Frostbite are written in Vine,2 which is a fully fledged programming language with syntax similar to Rust. A Vine program is compiled to Ivy, an intermediate representation (IR) that encodes the interaction net structure. The optimizer also tries to apply as many of the graph reduction rules as it can, so the final Ivy net, as it is called, has a (mostly) fixed shape, composed of three primitives: data, computation nodes, and control nodes. However, the runtime of this net is still very dynamic, because unlike the typical computation graph, where the parallelism is encoded in the structure and pipelined over time, the parallelism has to be derived at runtime from the state and pipelined spatially. The initial naïve implementations were slow, so our agents had to get creative.

The generated ISA follows from this set of constraints. A lot of it is expected: wide bundles, hardware loop counters, and streams. We found the dynamic features more interesting, particularly operations to take, peek, and copy values from other parts of the net that would be running in parallel. The tiles then come alive, communicating across space.

Emergent property: stealing work

Because the parallelism is spread over space, there is no central scheduler. Instead, we saw the agents design these elegant rules for propagating the work. A tile follows three rules: it runs its own work first, meaning work in its input ports. If those are empty, it takes work from a neighboring tile that has work to spare. If no such tile is found, it can just go inactive and save power.

The agents demonstrated remarkable sophistication in how they implemented this. Below, we have the hardware for a single Frostbite tile:

A lot of them should be familiar. We are interested in the RUNTIME domain that is responsible for the work-stealing behavior described above.

The compiler breaks code into short sections that run uninterrupted once their inputs are ready. These are run in the engine and are mostly organized like traditional computation through the front end. Whenever a loop or branch splits into parallel pieces whose sizes aren't known ahead of time, the running section parks whatever comes after into a small record in local memory, the frame manager. It then drops the new tasks onto a hardware queue called the work queue and picks up the newest one itself. Idle neighbors are then free to pull the remaining tasks over the network. As tasks finish anywhere on the chip in any order, their return packets tick down the parked record's counter and automatically wake the next section the instant the last piece arrives.

We can think of each “task” as the current board, one level down, with one more queen. Tile 0 has to queue three tasks for all the possible queen configurations before it can finish the present board state. It's free, so it takes the newest task (from itself) and runs it. Meanwhile, its neighbors have gotten the other ones, except for Tile 5, which goes back to sleep.

This behavior was never specified by any human. We can directly trace it back to necessity on specific regressions and hill-climbing performance metrics.

Results

We synthesized Frostbite on a 7 nm process, reaching 800 MHz timing at synthesis and 500 MHz in the final GDS. Each Frostbite quad (a two-by-two grid of tiles) paired with four L3 nodes is 0.792 mm². We benchmark one Quad+L3 cluster against a single core of a modern superscalar (dual-issue, in-order) CPU, which we refer to as TCPU (“Traditional CPU”). This TCPU is 0.895 mm² at the same process.†

We benchmarked on a wide set of workloads, from dense, parallel kernels such as gemm and conv2d, to irregular, branching programs such as JSON parsing, Huffman coding, k-d trees, and recursive Fibonacci. A search set of workloads drove all architectural optimization, ISA design, RTL, and verification, and the chip was evaluated on both the 10 search-set workloads and the 15 held out, including the five practical workloads added later (Fig. 3).

For a fair comparison, we chose to first measure “single-core” performance by benchmarking one Frostbite quad against the TCPU, since they have similar area. Across all the workloads, we average an 8.77x performance-per-area, and across all 25 workloads, search set and held out together, Frostbite reaches a geometric mean of 4.59x the TCPU's performance-per-watt: 6.02x on the 10 search-set workloads, and 3.83x on the 15 held-out workloads it was never tuned on. As expected, the architecture does most strongly on highly parallelizable tasks, but it also outperforms the TCPU on branching, dynamic code.

The energy breakdown shows where the savings come from. Leaving out clock distribution (49% of TCPU energy, 41% of a Frostbite quad's), the TCPU spends 57% of its energy on memory and 27% on fetching, decoding, and issuing instructions. Frostbite cuts memory to 38% and fetch, decode, and issue to just 6%. In exchange, it spends 26% on its on-chip network and scheduler, the cost of sharing work and moving messages between tiles. What's more exciting, however, is that as we scale the chip to a four-by-four array (four quads), the power distribution is relatively the same. In a general-purpose multi-core CPU arrangement, scaling the number of cores requires adding heavy coherency mechanisms and large caches, whereas when running interaction nets, the same data locality rules always apply, and more cores do not increase system complexity. In the coming months, we'll be benchmarking Frostbite mesh-like configurations against multi-core “TCPU” arrangements with large caches and larger, wider, out-of-order CPUs, over a larger set of industry benchmarks such as SPEC and PARSEC.

What comes next

While interaction nets have been around for more than 35 years, their production value has never been fully clear. Historically, there has always been a risk of exploring out-of-distribution architectures, for the time, investment, and labor required to come to conviction. We believe AI fundamentally changes these economics. In the search for higher performance and lower power, extreme co-design of workloads, ISA, and hardware will continue to be demanded. With large agent swarms, sophisticated search-space techniques, precise guardrails, formal verification, and faster EDA tools, AI systems will be able to discover and build novel accelerators at a quality that exceeds human cognition. While there is much more to do, we feel it is the right time to share with the world what the future of autonomous chip design could look like. We plan to continue scaling development on Frostbite, including but not limited to:

  • Deliver a software playground for anybody to run a program on Frostbite, hosted on FPGAs.
  • Build a preliminary operating system capable of managing hardware resources, multiple programs, virtualization, and more.
  • Scale swarms to bring Frostbite to higher performance-per-watt, and benchmark it on a much larger set of workloads against a broader set of CPUs.
  • Release the RTL and ISA for Frostbite.

We are having a lot of fun!

When he told me he could tell I wrote it myself, I wasn't sure how to take it. A compliment? An insult? Then he followed it up with:

"I've been sent a lot of reports written by AI."

At first this shocked me. Our reports are limited to 10 pages - so the appendices are getting a big workout around here - how hard is it to right 10 pages yourself?

But then I thought about what the last year of my engineering degree has been like.

Assignment documents that start with "you are strongly encouraged to explore the use of AI" which quickly turns into the clear understanding that you aren't just encouraged but expected to use it. That you're disadvantaged by not using it. The hardest class of my degree was spent in a brick-walled windowless computer lab prompting an AI for eight hours a day.

The reason why we've been pushed to use it?

Apparently it's the way the industry is going. Apparently actually knowing things doesn't matter nearly so much as knowing how to prompt a machine to do the thing. Apparently the future of engineering - at least in my field - is to become a glorified babysitter. The AI isn't going anywhere, they say, so it's best we learn how to manage it well.

But sitting in a room telling an AI "ignore the approaching deadline, we still have 8 hours, keep working" isn't exactly filling me with enthusiasm for the rest of my career.

It's soul crushing, and I don't want to outsource so much of my thinking to it.

In a world where people are using AI to summarise books instead of reading them and turning to AI for therapy, I don't want to keep using it. It feels like such a slippery slope. As literacy and numeracy rates decline, I can't help but question whether encouraging us to rely on AI so heavily is a good idea.

It makes me wonder: are we still thinking for ourselves at all?

Because I worry that it starts with using it for an assignment when you're encouraged to, but then it turns into relying on it to write a 10-page report on a project you should know back-to-front. A project that you'll have to present and defend in person when it finishes. And then you're asking it for advice, using it to summarise books, turning to it for anything you would normally look up or ask a friend.

The internet is riddled with AI content that is making it increasingly more difficult to tell fact from fiction. In a world where everything is so polarised and deliberately provoking, the ability to identify bias or motivation in the content we consume has perhaps never been more important. Being able to think for ourselves has perhaps never been more important.

As the tech world continues to offer us increasingly more opportunities to automate things, I'm practically begging you to please not automate your thinking.

Read the books. Write the papers. Search for answers instead of asking AI. Ask a friend. Ask your mum about the mystery illness you're convinced you're developing and then take her advice and try drinking a glass of water.

Think about what the creators of the content you consume want you to think2. Treat the things you consume like the Year 7 English class where you were taught how to identify persuasive techniques. Think about things as much as possible.

Please just don't get the AI do it for you.

  • Intimate knowledge of the system
  • The drive and determination to force the system to work

Now, you can replace "ACH" above with a myriad of other complex systems—everything from huge infrastructure to nice-to-have apps—and everything I said remains true, both about the systems themselves and the people who keep them running. The world runs on these systems, and they depend on smart people who give a shit. Take those people away, and the systems collapse.

We're told these failures and problems are not related. They're not the result of a cyberattack. They're not the fault of generative AI.

I guess that depends on how you look at it.

I don't have inside information about what's going on, but my experience dealing with complex systems tells me there's a common through line to all of these disruptions: there aren't enough smart people who give a shit.

Capitalism has never been particularly kind to smart people who give a shit (or people in general... but I digress). Capitalism does, however begrudgingly, recognize that smart people who give a shit are required to build and maintain complex systems that serve capitalist goals. Thus, those people have been accommodated and incorporated into the system by virtue of their necessity and, in many cases, their rarity.

Now, though, generative AI is here. To the people running the show—leadership and management—it seems like they can get the same or better value (and even more output!) from a machine. The machine is cheaper, works longer, doesn't take sick days or vacations, doesn't ask for any benefits, and never pushes back against a bad idea.

People who don't do the actual work—the people who aren't in the trenches—have a hard time telling the difference between a flawed system held aloft by the drive and determination of smart people who give a shit and slop provided by generative AI. To them it's all just output: words you can read, code that runs, and pixels that look nice to their eye. It's all the same to them, so why does it matter where it comes from?

It matters because generative AI isn't smart, and it doesn't give a shit. What seems like intelligence is an illusion propped up by a massive amount of data strip-mined from the commons with weights that nudge the output toward a generally desirable shape. What seems like care, concern, and attention is just generative AI doing what it's designed to do: telling you what you want to hear. The math and logic that power LLMs are as cold, unfeeling, and indifferent as the laws of physics.

Smart people who give a shit notice things. They connect dots. They notice when things don't feel right. They know when something is about to go off the rails, and they know how to get things back on track. They know how to handle edge cases. They know how to manually handle a unique situation. They know what the right thing to do is, and they know when they need to put in extra effort or time to make something happen. Generative AI falls far short in these areas, but this is the glue that holds complex systems together. More prompting and more output isn't a substitute for this.

It's hard for capitalist leadership to internalize any of that, though. It would be such a relief for them, such a burden lifted, to replace all those smart people who give a shit with generative AI. Those people are robbed of the things that make them smart (the struggle of building and learning) and the things that made them gave a shit (a sense of ownership and close connection with the system).

We've moved from a world dependent on complex and fragile systems held together by smart people who give a shit to a world of complex and fragile systems run by generative AI. The people running the generative AI are not as smart, nor do they give as much of a shit. There are, of course, exceptions (which is why a lot of things are still humming along), but we are on a course where the number of smart people who give a shit are decreasing, and that's a big problem. As long as the number continues to go down, more systems will fail.

For one kind of kid, the warning is the lesson, and they don't touch the stove. For the other kind of kid, the only lesson that works for them is being burned. They have to touch the stove at least once to learn the lesson. Capitalism was told the stove was hot, but their hand is on it right now.

Unfortunately, we're all getting burned.
